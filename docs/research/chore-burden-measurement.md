# How chore burden is measured

Research ticket: issue #4. Searches run 2026-11-16.

**Question.** What validated instruments and taxonomies exist for measuring the burden of household tasks, across time use, physical effort, unpleasantness or enjoyment, cognitive labour and schedule inflexibility? Which dimensions have evidence of mattering to perceived burden, how is each elicited, and is there evidence on combining them into a single measure?

## Short answer

- **No validated instrument measures the burden of a household chore across all five dimensions, and no study was found that validates a single composite burden score for housework.** Each dimension has its own measurement tradition, and they have rarely been put in one model.
- **Time** is the best-instrumented dimension. Time diaries are the reference method. "Stylised" self-estimates (how many hours do you usually spend) are systematically biased, in one US comparison about 50% higher than diary figures (Bianchi et al. 2000), and partners frequently disagree about who does what (Naujoks 2024).
- **Enjoyment or unpleasantness** has good instruments from well-being research (day reconstruction, episode affect ratings, the U-index) (Kahneman et al. 2004; Kahneman & Krueger 2006; Krueger 2007). Chores differ a lot from one another in experienced affect, people differ in which chores they dislike, and enjoyment is linked to both who does the work and whether the split is seen as fair (Stratton 2012; Poortman & van der Lippe 2009; Grote et al. 2002).
- **Cognitive labour** has a widely used taxonomy (anticipate, identify options, decide, monitor) (Daminger 2019) and consistent evidence of links to stress, well-being and relationship quality (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023; Aviv et al. 2024; Ciciolla & Luthar 2019; Petts & Carlson 2023). A 2023 systematic review found no validated scale (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023); scales published since are new and little replicated (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024; Wayne et al. 2023; Vettoretto et al. 2026).
- **Schedule inflexibility** has an established taxonomy (routine or low-schedule-control tasks versus non-routine or high-schedule-control tasks) (Barnett & Shen 1997; Hook 2010; Carlson et al. 2018; Craig & Powell 2016). The evidence that inflexible time is more harmful than flexible time rests on one supportive study (Barnett & Shen 1997). The one later study found (Tao et al. 2010) used a different measure and did not find the same pattern, so the claim has not been directly retested.
- **Physical effort** can be measured objectively (metabolic equivalents, METs) (Ainsworth et al. 2011; Herrmann et al. 2024), but no study was found that links the physical effort of chores to perceived burden or fairness between partners. This is the weakest-evidenced dimension of the five.
- **Perceived fairness is only loosely tied to any measured burden.** It depends on which tasks each partner does more than on hours (Baxter 2000), and on comparison referents, feeling appreciated, and agreement between partners (Carriero & Todesco 2016; Lachance-Grzela 2012; Sarmiento et al. 2024; Coundouris & Henry 2026). A burden score can inform a fair split but cannot by itself predict that a couple will feel it is fair.

## Findings by theme

### 1. Time use

**Strength of evidence: strong for how to measure and for the bias in self-estimates. Mostly large surveys, several countries, method-comparison designs.**

- **Instruments.** Three methods are in use: time diaries (activities recorded in 10 or 15 minute intervals over a sampled day), stylised survey questions (usual hours per week on an activity), and experience sampling (random-moment prompts) (Juster et al. 2003; Zhou et al. 2025). A 2025 review states that stylised data "tend to be less accurate than time-diary data, especially when it comes to measuring the time spent on housework and caregiving" (Zhou et al. 2025, full text).
- **Self-estimates run high.** In the US, survey estimates of housework from the National Survey of Families and Households were about 50% higher than time-diary figures, though both sources gave similar conclusions about the gap between partners (Bianchi et al. 2000, abstract). A separate comparison of the same survey against diary data found that both husbands and wives over-report their housework, with the size of the over-report varying with gender attitudes, class, education and family size (Press & Townsley 1998, abstract).
- **The error is systematic, not random.** A British study with both measures from the same respondents (632 men, 666 women) found the gap between stylised and diary estimates was generally smaller for women, grew with irregular housework hours and with housework done as a secondary activity, and grew with dependent children. The author concludes there are systematic errors in stylised estimates (Kan 2008, working-paper abstract, full text).
- **Shares are more robust than hours.** In 265 US couples, estimates of housework hours "differ substantially and significantly across various measures", but the share done by each partner "differs somewhat less" (Lee & Waite 2005, abstract).
- **Diaries are not always better.** A methods assessment found exceptions: stylised and diary estimates agree closely for regular paid work hours and for historical trends (Juster et al. 2003, abstract). This is about paid work and trends, not housework levels.
- **Partners disagree.** In 414 German couples followed across the transition to parenthood, 45% showed a reporting gap between partners on the division of housework (Naujoks 2024, abstract). Social desirability matters too: women interviewed face to face reported more housework hours to a female interviewer than to a male one (Acht & Rebaudo 2025, abstract).
- **Time alone misses things.** Time-estimate methods capture primary activities, while mental labour often runs as a secondary activity alongside something else (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023, full text). One experience-sampling study that added "time spent thinking about household tasks" put this at two to three hours a week per partner (Lee & Waite 2005; Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023, full text table).

### 2. Physical effort

**Strength of evidence: strong that it can be measured objectively; no evidence found that it drives perceived burden or fairness.**

- **Instrument.** The Compendium of Physical Activities assigns each activity an energy cost in METs. The 2011 edition has 821 activity codes, 68% with measured values (Ainsworth et al. 2011, abstract). The 2024 adult edition has 1,114 activities, 912 with values measured by indirect calorimetry (Herrmann et al. 2024, abstract).
- **Household values.** The 2024 Compendium's home-activities list gives, for example: ironing 1.8; washing dishes 2.0; light cooking 2.0; folding or hanging laundry 2.3; vacuuming 3.0; making beds 3.0; mopping 3.3; moderate-effort cooking 3.5; scrubbing floors or bathroom 3.5 (moderate) to 6.5 (vigorous); carrying groceries upstairs 5.3; moving furniture 5.8 (Herrmann et al. 2024, compendium web page). Most routine chores sit in a narrow band of roughly 2 to 4 METs.
- **Self-report alternative.** The NASA Task Load Index includes a physical demand subscale rated by the person doing the task (Hart 2006; Hertzum 2021). It was not found applied to housework.
- **Link to burden: not found.** No study was found that elicits the physical effort of individual chores from partners and relates it to perceived burden, fairness or satisfaction. Studies that speak of "physical" household labour use the word to mean the doing of tasks, as opposed to planning them (Aviv et al. 2024; Coundouris & Henry 2026), not exertion.
- **Nearest evidence.** Episode-level affect data include "tired" and "pain" ratings by activity (0 to 6 scale). Tiredness during cleaning (2.85) and home repairs (2.76) is in the same range as during paid work (2.72) (Krueger 2007, full text). This is weak, indirect evidence and does not isolate physical effort.

### 3. Unpleasantness and enjoyment

**Strength of evidence: strong for measurement; moderate that it matters for allocation and fairness (few studies, mostly cross-sectional, one small longitudinal).**

- **Instruments.** The Day Reconstruction Method has people rebuild the previous day as episodes and rate feelings in each; it was validated against experience sampling in 909 employed women (Kahneman et al. 2004, abstract) (PubMed). The U-index is the share of time spent in an episode whose strongest feeling is negative; its authors note it needs only an ordinal reading of feelings (Kahneman & Krueger 2006, abstract; Krueger 2007, full text).
- **Chores differ in experienced affect.** In the 2006 Princeton Affect and Time Survey in the US (3,982 respondents, weighted to the population, 37% response rate), net affect on a 0 to 6 basis was 3.14 for cooking and 3.34 for gardening, but 2.28 for dishes, 2.46 for laundry and ironing, 2.63 for cleaning and 2.22 for home repairs. Paid work scored 2.55. The author labels the laundry, dishes and cleaning cluster "mundane chores": slightly above paid work on net affect but well below it on interest (Krueger 2007, full text). Single study; activity ratings are conditional on who chooses to do the activity, though the author reports a 0.92 correlation between raw and within-person rankings.
- **People differ in which chores they dislike.** UK time-use data with stated chore preferences show that laundry, ironing, cleaning and food shopping are generally less enjoyable than gardening or DIY, that preferences vary across people, and that preferences correlate with time spent (Stratton 2012, abstract). In 732 Dutch respondents, how much each partner enjoys, sets standards for, and feels responsible for cleaning, cooking and childcare was associated with who does it; a person's own attitudes mattered more than the partner's (Poortman & van der Lippe 2009, abstract).
- **Enjoyment is linked to perceived fairness.** A three-wave study of married couples expecting a first child found women saw an unequal split as fairer when they enjoyed family work and when both spouses saw them as competent at it. The social-comparison hypothesis was not supported (Grote et al. 2002, abstract). Single study, one life stage.
- **Meanings vary by task and by person.** In 309 spouses and cohabitors, the affective meanings attached to nine chores differed by gender for about a third of the measures, and most of those differences disappeared once each person's paid and unpaid work pattern was controlled (Kroska 2003, abstract). This suggests ratings track what a person currently does, not only fixed taste.
- **Caution on direction.** Because enjoyment helps explain why unequal splits are judged fair (Grote et al. 2002) and ratings shift with current work patterns (Kroska 2003), a stated preference may partly reflect the existing arrangement. No study found tests whether asking partners to rate chore aversion produces splits they later judge fairer.

### 4. Cognitive labour

**Strength of evidence: strong that it is a distinct, consequential dimension (one systematic review, many surveys). Weak on validated measurement. Samples are mostly mothers and different-sex parents.**

- **Taxonomy.** Interviews with 35 couples (70 people) defined cognitive labour as four components: anticipating needs, identifying options, making decisions, and monitoring progress. The author describes it as taxing, often invisible to both partners, and a frequent source of conflict (Daminger 2019, abstract). A later theoretical paper argues the "mental load" is cognitive plus emotional labour, and is invisible, boundaryless and never complete (Dean et al. 2022, abstract).
- **Systematic review.** A PRISMA review of 31 studies found women do more mental labour and report more stress and lower life and relationship satisfaction from it. On measurement it states "there are no validated scales for recording mental labor", so only single facets are measured, and it calls for a multidimensional scale to be developed and tested (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023, abstract and full text) (PubMed).
- **How it has been elicited.**
  - *Share-of-responsibility items per task.* A 21-item battery given to 3,000 US parents split into two factors: daily tasks tied to family well-being, and episodic tasks tied to maintenance and finances. Question wording changed the contributions parents reported (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024, abstract).
  - *Planning versus execution on the same task.* 322 mothers rated who plans and who executes each of 30 tasks adapted from a commercial card deck. Cognitive and physical scores were each highly internally consistent (alpha 0.94 and 0.93), women's share of planning was more lopsided than their share of execution, and cognitive share was associated with depression, stress, burnout and relationship functioning. The authors state the measure "has not been empirically validated" and the sample was a well-educated, high-income convenience sample (Aviv et al. 2024, full text) (PubMed).
  - *Intensity scales.* A nine-item "invisible family load" scale with managerial, cognitive and emotional subscales was developed across five studies (Wayne et al. 2023, abstract). A multidimensional scale was used with 2,309 Italian mothers (Vettoretto et al. 2026, abstract) (PubMed).
  - *Experience sampling.* Random-moment prompts in 402 mothers and 291 fathers recorded what people were thinking about (Offer 2014, abstract).
  - *Both partners asked.* A 2025 preprint proposes survey items put to both partners about who organises recurring tasks, and reports that partners underestimate women's burden (Barigozzi et al. 2025, preprint abstract, not peer reviewed).
- **Outcomes.** Cognitive labour share or load is associated with: lower well-being and relationship satisfaction in 393 US mothers (Ciciolla & Luthar 2019, abstract); family-work conflict for women but not men across 10 European countries (Haupt & Gelbgiser 2023, abstract); lower psychological well-being for mothers (Petts & Carlson 2023, abstract); emotional exhaustion and work outcomes over seven weekly surveys, including women without children (Krstić et al. 2025, abstract) (PubMed); negative affect, anxiety and depressive symptoms in two samples of women and mothers, who most often named "constantly thinking about tasks" and tasks never seeming finished as the causes (Jungmann et al. 2026, abstract) (PubMed).
- **Disagreement: not uniformly negative.** The nine-item scale study found emotional load had uniformly negative correlates, but managerial load related to greater family-work enrichment and cognitive load to greater family satisfaction and job performance (Wayne et al. 2023, abstract). In the experience-sampling study mothers and fathers thought about family matters equally often, and only mothers' well-being suffered (Offer 2014, abstract).
- **Disagreement between partners matters.** In 235 cohabiting different-sex couples, larger gaps between partners' perceptions of who carries the physical, cognitive and emotional loads went with poorer relationship quality. For the cognitive load this held only for men (Coundouris & Henry 2026, abstract).
- **Beyond different-sex parents.** Interviews with same-gender couples without children identified trust and explicit negotiation as what made cognitive labour feel shared (McLean et al. 2023, abstract) (PubMed). Qualitative and small.

### 5. Schedule inflexibility (routine versus non-routine tasks)

**Strength of evidence: strong for the taxonomy. Thin on whether inflexibility adds burden beyond time: one supportive study, never directly retested.**

- **Taxonomy.** Research separates routine tasks (cooking, cleaning, laundry, dishes) from non-routine ones (home maintenance, gardening, repairs) (Zhou et al. 2025, full text; Carlson et al. 2018; Craig & Powell 2016). A cross-national study of 36 time-use surveys in 19 countries analyses "time-inflexible housework" as a category; its abstract does not list which tasks that covers (Hook 2010, abstract) (PubMed). Sources do not classify every task the same way: shopping is listed with less routine tasks in one review (Zhou et al. 2025) and with low-schedule-control tasks in another scheme (Tao et al. 2010). The cognitive-labour battery showed the same split into daily and episodic tasks (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024).
- **Definition by control.** Low-schedule-control tasks "must typically be done every day and at certain times", with little discretion over timing (preparing meals, washing dishes, cleaning, shopping, washing and ironing). High-schedule-control tasks (outdoor tasks, paying bills, vehicle maintenance) can be done when the person chooses and without urgency (Tao et al. 2010, full text, describing the scheme of Barnett & Shen 1997) (PubMed).
- **Supportive finding.** In 265 dual-earner couples, hours in low-schedule-control tasks were associated with psychological distress, while total housework hours and hours in high-schedule-control tasks were not. Absolute hours mattered, not the share. The association did not differ by gender (Barnett & Shen 1997, abstract). Cross-sectional, single study, 1997.
- **Later study, different measure, different pattern.** In 293 Canadian employed parents of preschoolers, perceived *relative* time in low-schedule-control tasks was unrelated to distress for mothers or fathers. That null is not a clean contradiction: the earlier study had itself predicted and found that absolute hours mattered and proportional time did not. The discordant result is that, for mothers, more relative time in *high*-schedule-control tasks went with more distress. The authors call their findings inconsistent with the earlier work and suggest overload as an explanation (Tao et al. 2010, abstract and full text) (PubMed). Cross-sectional. Absolute hours in inflexible tasks were not retested.
- **Routine tasks and fairness.** In National Survey of Families and Households data, husbands' time in feminine-typed, routine chores partly accounted for wives' fairness perceptions (James & Clay-Warner 2015, abstract). Among middle- to low-income couples, the division of dishwashing was the single task most consequential for relationship quality, especially for women (Carlson et al. 2018, abstract).
- **Related: time intensity.** In 9,177 Australian survey respondents, often or always feeling rushed was associated with poorer self-rated and mental health, separately from the number of hours worked and cared (Strazdins et al. 2016, abstract) (PubMed). This concerns a person's overall time pressure, not a property of individual chores.
- **Terminology note.** The standard 2000 review of this field uses "routine housework" (Coltrane 2000, abstract). This note could not confirm from retrieved text that it uses the word "intermittent", so that label is not attributed to it.

### 6. What predicts perceived fairness (the project's objective)

**Strength of evidence: strong and replicated that perceived fairness is distinct from the measured split; moderate on the specific drivers.**

- **Perception outweighs the measured split for outcomes.** Perceived equity, not the actual division, was associated with lower depression among husbands and wives in the National Survey of Families and Households (Glass & Fujimoto 1994, abstract) (PubMed). In 4,924 adults living with a partner, satisfaction with the division, not the division itself, was associated with life satisfaction (Wagman et al. 2017, abstract) (PubMed). In 2,644 US adults, perceived fairness carried the association between chore hours and later well-being, relationship quality and health, among respondents of lower socioeconomic status only (Rodriguez-Stanley et al. 2020, abstract).
- **Most people call unequal splits fair.** Cross-national data (8,556 respondents) and Australian panel data both show perceptions of fairness are far more common than the split would suggest (Braun et al. 2008; Perales et al. 2015, abstracts). In Sweden, the largest group of couples both shared equally and agreed it was fair (33%) (Ruppanner et al. 2017, abstract).
- **Tasks beat time.** In an Australian national survey, "the key factor determining perceptions of fairness is the division of tasks", and time spent "is less important than who does what" (Baxter 2000, abstract). A study using a count of equally shared tasks found it related to better relationship quality for both partners, unlike the overall share (Carlson 2022, secondary summary of the abstract; cross-sectional).
- **Non-burden drivers.** Comparison referents affect fairness judgements, including in a vignette experiment on an Italian probability sample, and unfavourable comparisons weigh more than favourable ones (Carriero & Todesco 2016, abstract). Feeling appreciated by one's partner changed how 141 Canadian mothers judged fairness (Lachance-Grzela 2012, abstract). In interviews with nine US families, agreement and emotional satisfaction were treated as more important than a specific amount of labour (Sarmiento et al. 2024, abstract). In 389 German-speaking dual-earner couples, perceived justice related to wives' satisfaction, and to husbands' through conflict (Mikula et al. 2012, abstract).

### 7. Combining dimensions into one measure

**Strength of evidence: none found within housework. What follows is analogy from other fields and is labelled as such.**

- **Within housework.** No study was found that builds or validates a weighted composite of time, effort, unpleasantness, cognitive load and inflexibility for chores. The nearest things are:
  - simple averages of per-task share items, which are internally consistent (Aviv et al. 2024);
  - a five-item unidimensional "home management hassles" scale validated among members of a Japanese family-nursing research association, measuring overall felt burden, not per-chore burden, with retest reliability and general-population validity still untested (Sato et al. 2025, abstract);
  - a ten-question food-work questionnaire covering planning, getting, preparing and cleaning up, which has expert content validation only (Cahill et al. 2024, abstract) (PubMed).
- **Evidence against a time-only measure.** Total hours did not predict distress where low-control hours did (Barnett & Shen 1997); time mattered less than task identity for fairness (Baxter 2000); one task (dishes) stood out from the total (Carlson et al. 2018).
- **Analogy: occupational workload (NASA-TLX).** This index combines six self-rated subscales, including mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, effort and frustration, originally weighted by each rater's own paired comparisons of the subscales (Hart 2006; Hertzum 2021, abstracts).
  - Unweighted ("raw") and weighted scores correlate very highly: r above 0.95 in one study of 25 participants (Ma et al. 2021, abstract); 0.84 and 0.95 in two experiments (Miyake et al. 2019, abstract).
  - Findings on sensitivity disagree: the first of these found weighted scores more sensitive to experimental factors (Ma et al. 2021); the second concluded the unweighted version is useful without the comparison step (Miyake et al. 2019).
  - One study argues each subscale should be examined separately instead of relying on the global score (Galy et al. 2018, title and machine summary only).
  - A meta-analytic review of 556 studies found subscales are more inter-correlated in real-life settings than in the laboratory (Hertzum 2021, abstract).
- **Analogy: unit weighting.** A classic review of linear prediction models found that equal ("unit") weights are "quite robust" for predicting a numerical criterion and outperform unaided judgement (Dawes 1979, abstract). This concerns prediction of an external criterion, which a chore burden score does not yet have.
- **Analogy: time-weighted affect.** The U-index aggregates across activities by weighting each activity's unpleasantness by the time spent in it (Krueger 2007, full text). This is a published precedent for a "time multiplied by unpleasantness" construction, at population level, not within couples.

## Implications for the allocation method

The prototype scores each chore as a weighted mix of aversion (1 to 5 preference rating), effort (physical and cognitive, 1 to 5 each) and time (minutes times frequency, penalised for inflexibility). Read against the evidence:

1. **The choice of dimensions is defensible; the combination is unvalidated.** Time, unpleasantness, cognitive load and inflexibility each have a research tradition and at least some evidence of mattering. No source supports any particular weights, or a weighted sum over another form. The method should describe its formula as a design choice.
2. **Time: keep it, but do not trust a single self-estimate.** Self-estimates over-report and the error varies by person and circumstance (Kan 2008; Bianchi et al. 2000; Press & Townsley 1998). Partners disagree (Naujoks 2024). Options that follow from the evidence: have both partners estimate each chore and surface disagreements; prefer logged durations where possible; rely on relative comparisons, which are more robust than absolute hours (Lee & Waite 2005).
3. **Aversion: keep it, per person.** People differ in which chores they dislike (Stratton 2012; Poortman & van der Lippe 2009), and enjoyment is linked to perceived fairness (Grote et al. 2002). A per-person rating fits the evidence better than a fixed unpleasantness ranking. The 1 to 5 single item itself is not a validated instrument; the validated tools rate specific feelings during episodes (Kahneman et al. 2004; Krueger 2007).
4. **Aversion: guard against ratings that mirror the status quo.** Stated enjoyment helps explain acceptance of unequal splits (Grote et al. 2002) and shifts with what a person currently does (Kroska 2003). A mechanism that hands each partner the chores they say they mind least could entrench an existing pattern. This is an inference from those two findings, not a tested result.
5. **Physical effort: weakest case for inclusion.** It can be measured, but nothing found ties it to perceived burden, and routine chores span a narrow MET range (Herrmann et al. 2024). It may be reasonable to give it low default weight, or to treat it as a constraint for partners with physical limitations instead of a general cost. That last suggestion is an inference by this note, not a finding.
6. **Cognitive labour: a 1 to 5 "cognitive effort" rating per chore does not match how the literature models it.** Research treats cognitive labour as the work of anticipating, planning, deciding and monitoring (Daminger 2019), often separable from execution of the same task (Aviv et al. 2024), running in the background (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023), and never finished (Jungmann et al. 2026; Dean et al. 2022). Recording who *owns the planning* of each chore as its own allocatable item, alongside who executes it, is consistent with that literature, and a per-chore intensity rating would miss ownership. This is an inference by this note: it rests on one qualitative taxonomy and one measure its own authors call unvalidated, and no study tests it.
7. **Inflexibility: the taxonomy is sound, the penalty size is not evidenced.** Classifying chores as time-bound and recurrent versus deferrable is standard (Barnett & Shen 1997; Hook 2010). Whether inflexible minutes cost more than flexible ones rests on one 1997 study that has not been directly retested (Barnett & Shen 1997; Tao et al. 2010). A multiplier on time is a plausible modelling choice with no empirical basis for its magnitude; letting couples set or disable it would be consistent with the evidence.
8. **Weights: equal defaults with couple adjustment has the best available support, by analogy only.** In workload research, individually weighted and unweighted composites correlate very highly (Ma et al. 2021; Miyake et al. 2019), and equal weights are robust in linear models (Dawes 1979). Workload research also warns against hiding the subscales behind a total (Galy et al. 2018). Showing the per-dimension breakdown next to any total follows from that.
9. **A burden score will not by itself deliver perceived fairness.** Fairness depends on which tasks each partner does (Baxter 2000), on shared tasks (Carlson 2022), on appreciation (Lachance-Grzela 2012), on comparison referents (Carriero & Todesco 2016) and on partners agreeing about who does what (Coundouris & Henry 2026; Sarmiento et al. 2024). Since the project's objective is the couple's own perceived fairness, the method needs to elicit that judgement directly and treat it as the criterion, with burden scores as an input. Surfacing where partners' ratings or estimates disagree is supported as valuable in its own right (Coundouris & Henry 2026; Naujoks 2024).
10. **Generalisability.** The evidence base is dominated by different-sex couples, parents and mothers' self-reports, mostly in the US, Europe and Australia. Gendered findings here are evidence about how things go wrong, as the project brief already assumes. Little of it has been tested in childless or same-gender couples (Krstić et al. 2025; McLean et al. 2023).

## Open questions and gaps

- **No per-chore, multi-dimensional burden instrument exists** in the sources found. Any such score in this project would be novel and would need its own validation against couples' fairness judgements.
- **No evidence on weights or functional form** for combining dimensions in housework. Additive, multiplicative (time times unpleasantness) and profile approaches are all untested here.
- **Physical effort and perceived burden**: no direct study found.
- **Inflexibility**: two cross-sectional studies that used different measures (absolute hours in 1997, perceived relative time in 2010) and found different patterns. No direct replication, and no experimental or longitudinal test, was found.
- **Stated preference as an allocation input**: no study found on whether allocating by stated aversion improves later perceived fairness, or on how stable chore ratings are over time.
- **Cognitive labour measurement is unsettled.** The new scales (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024; Wayne et al. 2023; Vettoretto et al. 2026) have not been compared with one another, and none is designed to attach a cognitive cost to an individual chore.
- **Is cognitive load always a cost?** One scale study found positive correlates for the managerial and cognitive components (Wayne et al. 2023). Whether owning the planning of a chore is a burden may depend on the person.
- **Effect sizes** were not extracted, because most sources were read at abstract level. Any decision that hinges on magnitude needs full-text reading of Barnett & Shen (1997), Tao et al. (2010), Baxter (2000), Grote et al. (2002) and Aviv et al. (2024).
- **Not covered here:** emotional labour as a separate dimension (it appears in Wayne et al. 2023, Vettoretto et al. 2026, Coundouris & Henry 2026 and Dean et al. 2022, but is outside the ticket), childcare, and the fair-division and preference-elicitation literature from economics and computer science.
