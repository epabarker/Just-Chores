# What causes chore conflict in couples

Research ticket: issue #2. Searched 2026-11-16.

## Short answer

The literature points to one dominant proximal cause and several things that feed it.

- **Perceived unfairness, not the measured split, is the main driver.** Across national surveys, cross-national datasets and a few longitudinal studies, a partner's sense that the division is unfair predicts lower relationship satisfaction, more frequent housework disagreement and (for women) higher odds of separation. Where the two are compared, measured hours or shares predict these outcomes less well than perception does (Coltrane 2000; Greenstein 1996; Lavee & Katz 2002; Frisco & Williams 2003; Tai & Baxter 2018; Smith et al. 2026).
- **Unfairness is a judgement, built from more than the task count.** It depends on comparison standards, on whether the arrangement was decided jointly, on whether contributions are noticed and appreciated, and on the rest of each partner's workload (Thompson 1991; Hawkins et al. 1995; Kluwer et al. 2002; Koster et al. 2021).
- **Cognitive labour (anticipating, planning, monitoring) is a distinct, largely invisible load.** It is more unevenly divided than physical chores, is linked to worse wellbeing and relationship outcomes, and is a named source of conflict. The evidence is recent, mostly cross-sectional and mostly from parents (Daminger 2019; Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023; Aviv et al. 2024; Ciciolla & Luthar 2019; Petts et al. 2025).
- **Feeling appreciated buffers the damage of an unequal split.** One multi-sample study with longitudinal follow-up found the satisfaction cost of doing more disappeared when the person felt appreciated (Gordon et al. 2022); older cross-sectional work agrees (Hawkins et al. 1995; Klumb et al. 2006).
- **Differing standards of cleanliness are widely theorised but poorly tested.** The peer-reviewed evidence shows that standards are socially enforced more harshly on women, not that men and women perceive mess differently (Thébaud et al. 2019). Direct tests of "mismatched standards cause conflict" are confined to a dissertation on roommates (Riforgiate 2011).
- **Gender shapes who feels unfairly treated and who gets heard.** Gender ideology and national context change the threshold at which inequality is felt as unfair (Greenstein 1996; Lavee & Katz 2002; Greenstein 2009); conflict over housework is structurally asymmetric, with the dissatisfied partner demanding and the other defending the status quo (Kluwer et al. 2000).
- **Same-sex couples show the fairness mechanism without the gender script.** Satisfaction with the division predicts relationship quality in every couple type, though less strongly than in different-sex couples; many same-sex couples divide unequally and still judge the split fair (Smith et al. 2026; Kurdek 2007; Beban & Roberts 2023).

No meta-analysis of housework fairness and relationship outcomes was located in a limited search (one bibliographic-database query and two web searches). The strongest syntheses located are one narrative review (Coltrane 2000) and two systematic reviews on narrower topics (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023; Brewster 2016). Most findings rest on cross-sectional self-report, so causal language below is deliberately hedged.

## Findings by theme

### 1. Perceived unfairness

**Strength: strong for the association; moderate for causation.**

What the evidence shows:

- A review of more than 200 works from 1989 to 1999 concluded that more balanced divisions go with women perceiving fairness, less depression and higher marital satisfaction (Coltrane 2000).
- In data from the National Survey of Families and Households, perceived inequity was tied to lower marital quality, more strongly for egalitarian wives, and the author argued research should move from objective inequality to perceived inequity (Greenstein 1996). An Israeli study across three ethnic-religious groups found sense of fairness mediates the link between division of labour and marital quality (Lavee & Katz 2002). A random sample of 382 two-earner couples found the division of labour affects marital satisfaction mostly through perceived fairness, and that what counts as fair differs between husbands and wives (Wilkie et al. 1998) **(secondary)**.
- Outcomes over time: in a nationally representative sample of people in dual-earner marriages (country and design not stated in the abstract), perceived inequity was linked to lower marital happiness for both spouses and to higher odds of divorce for wives only (Frisco & Williams 2003). In Swedish couple data (N = 1,057 couples) matched to registers over five years, unions were more likely to dissolve where the woman reported doing more housework, and where her partner credited her with less than she reported (Ruppanner et al. 2017).
- Conflict specifically: across 29 nations, perceived fairness mediates the link between the division of labour and the frequency of housework disagreement, more strongly for women (Tai & Baxter 2018). Across 25 European countries, both distributive-justice and relative-resources explanations of housework conflict were supported for men and women (Ruppanner 2009).
- Subjective beats objective: in the National Couples' Health and Time Use Study (N = 3,455 individuals in same-sex and different-sex couples, survey plus time diary), satisfaction with the division predicted relationship and sexual satisfaction more strongly than diary-measured time (Smith et al. 2026).
- Both directions of unfairness hurt: in German panel data (N = 11,012 cohabiting respondents, cross-sectional wave), relationship satisfaction was lower both for those who felt they did more than their fair share and for those who felt they did less (Adriaans et al. 2026). A large convenience sample (N = 10,236, recruited through a news website) found unfairness to oneself predicts relationship quality more strongly than unfairness to one's partner (Gillespie et al. 2019).
- Unequal is often judged fair: in an Italian sample of 404 dual-earner couples, most wives and husbands reported both fairness and satisfaction although wives did about two-thirds of the chores (Carriero 2011). Dutch data show unequal divisions are often seen as fair and that fairness is judged against total workload, paid work included (Koster et al. 2021).

What builds the fairness judgement:

- The distributive-justice framework names three inputs: outcomes valued (not only time and tasks), comparison referents and justifications (Thompson 1991).
- In a test of that framework on 234 US dual-earner wives, feeling appreciated was the strongest predictor of fairness and deciding together how to divide the work was the second (Hawkins et al. 1995). A three-wave study of 293 couples across the transition to parenthood supported wants and values, social comparison and procedural justice as predictors for wives; for husbands only the first two (Kluwer et al. 2002).
- Comparison referents matter: women compare both with their partner and with other women, and each comparison independently affects family-life satisfaction across 30 countries (Hu & Yucel 2017). Japanese data likewise found comparison with similar others drives perceived fairness (Nakamura & Akiyoshi 2015).
- Agreement itself can stand in for fairness: in interviews with nine US families, a recurring theme was "if we agree and are happy, then it's fair" (Sarmiento et al. 2024) (small qualitative study).

Disagreements and nulls:

- A British panel study found no main effect of equity in hours or of task specialisation on relationship satisfaction; effects appeared only for men with egalitarian attitudes (Blom et al. 2016).
- A German fixed-effects panel study (1,315 couples) found no within-couple effect of the division or of perceived fairness on sexual satisfaction or frequency, and argued earlier between-person findings may reflect unobserved confounding (Hajek 2019). This covers sexual outcomes only, but it is the main caution against reading the cross-sectional literature causally.
- A dyadic study of 235 heterosexual cohabiting partners found that partner discrepancies in *equality* perceptions, but not *equity* perceptions, related to poorer relationship quality (Coundouris & Henry 2026). This cuts against a simple "perceived fairness is all that matters" reading and has not been replicated.
- Reverse causation is plausible. One study of 487 couples found men's communication quality appears to be an outcome of housework arrangements, while women's communication quality predicts the arrangement (Carlson et al. 2020).

### 2. Cognitive labour (mental load)

**Strength: moderate that it is a distinct, unevenly held load linked to worse outcomes; limited that it causes conflict.**

- Definition: an interview study of 35 couples defined cognitive labour as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. It reported this work is taxing, often invisible to both partners, and a frequent source of conflict. Women did more overall and more of the anticipating and monitoring; decision-making was roughly equal (Daminger 2019). This is the origin of the conflict claim and it is qualitative.
- A PRISMA systematic review of 31 studies found women do the larger share of mental labour and report more negative consequences, including stress and lower life and relationship satisfaction. Samples were mostly US or European middle-class (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023).
- In 322 mothers of young children, cognitive labour was more unevenly split than physical labour within the same 30 tasks, and was associated with depression, stress, burnout and poorer relationship functioning (Aviv et al. 2024). In 393 US mothers (mostly upper-middle class), feeling solely responsible for household management was associated with lower partner satisfaction after controlling for intimacy (Ciciolla & Luthar 2019). Both are cross-sectional, mothers only.
- In US parents, equal sharing of cognitive housework was associated with the highest relationship satisfaction for both mothers and fathers (Petts et al. 2025) (cross-sectional).
- Across 10 European countries, a high share of cognitive labour raised women's family-work conflict but not men's (Haupt & Gelbgiser 2023).
- Cognitive labour splits by type much as physical chores do: daily "core" tasks versus episodic ones such as maintenance and finances (N = 3,000 US parents) (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024).
- It resists renegotiation: in 2,133 US parents, women's employment and earnings reduced their physical housework but not their cognitive load (Weeks et al. 2025).
- Experience sampling in dual-earner families (the 500 Family Study) found mothers and fathers thought about family matters equally often, but these thoughts were tied to worse emotional wellbeing only for mothers (Offer 2014).

Disagreement:

- In the dyadic study of 235 partners, discrepancy on cognitive load was related to worse relationship quality only among men, whereas physical and emotional load effects were stronger for women (Coundouris & Henry 2026). This does not fit the dominant account and is a single study.

Gaps: almost all samples are parents; child-free couples are barely studied. Measurement is not standardised, and question wording changes reported shares (Weeks & Ruppanner 2024).

### 3. Differing standards of cleanliness

**Strength: thin for mismatched standards as a cause of couple conflict; moderate that standards are gendered through social accountability.**

- A survey experiment showed respondents photos of a clean or messy room. Men and women did not differ in how messy they judged it or how urgent cleaning was. What mattered was the supposed occupant: women were held to higher standards and judged more responsible (Thébaud et al. 2019). This is evidence against "men do not see the mess" and for standards being externally enforced.
- Dutch survey data (N = 732) found women report higher standards for housework, enjoy it more and feel more responsible, and these attitudes go with doing more of it (Poortman & Van Der Lippe 2009). Self-reported standards differ even though perception in Thébaud et al. (2019) did not; the two are compatible if standards reflect accountability, not eyesight.
- A theory paper proposes that partners have different "threshold levels" for disorder, so the lower-threshold partner acts first, becomes the default owner of the task and is then under-thanked (Alberts et al. 2011). It is a theoretical integration, not a test.
- The only direct test located is a doctoral dissertation on same-sex roommates, which reported that pairs with differing thresholds had lower relational satisfaction and more frequent conflict (Riforgiate 2011). It is not peer-reviewed, not on romantic couples, and was read only through a search summary.
- Indirect support: among routine tasks, the division of dishwashing was the most consequential for relationship quality in middle- to low-income couples (Carlson et al. 2018), which fits a visible, standard-laden task being a flashpoint. The study does not test standards.

No peer-reviewed study was found that measures both partners' standards in cohabiting couples and relates the gap to conflict. This is the weakest-evidenced cause in the ticket despite its prominence in popular accounts.

### 4. Appreciation and gratitude

**Strength: moderate.**

- Three samples of people cohabiting with a partner during the pandemic (N = 2,193, including 476 couples, with follow-up over months): those doing more and perceiving more unfairness were less satisfied, but the effect disappeared when they felt appreciated. Appreciation also buffered the cost of doing *less*. Results held across gender, employment and relationship length (Gordon et al. 2022). The abstract describes the predictions as a priori; the design is observational, not experimental.
- Diary data from 52 German dual-earner couples: the satisfaction cost of unequal contribution disappeared after accounting for perceived appreciation of contributions (Klumb et al. 2006) (small sample).
- Appreciation was the strongest predictor of wives' fairness judgements in Hawkins et al. (1995). In 141 Canadian mothers, egalitarian ideology was linked to a sense of unfairness only when the woman felt little appreciation from her partner (Lachance-Grzela 2012).
- In same-sex partners (N = 165), feeling appreciated for household contributions correlated with relationship satisfaction (Sutphin 2010).
- Who feels appreciated: in 1987 to 1988 data from the National Survey of Families and Households, wives with more egalitarian attitudes and more alternatives outside the marriage felt less appreciated for housework; shared time with the husband went with feeling more appreciated (Lee & Waite 2010).
- Context: "lack of respect/appreciation" was among the most common reasons given for relationship breakdown in a British probability sample (Gravningen et al. 2017). This is not specific to chores.

Caveats:

- No intervention study was found showing that *inducing* appreciation reduces chore conflict.
- Appreciation can make an unequal arrangement feel acceptable without changing it. The literature treats this as a buffer (Gordon et al. 2022); whether it is a remedy or a mask is a value question the studies do not settle.
- One related study on mattering and wives' fairness (Kawamura & Brown 2010) was located but its abstract could not be retrieved; it is **unverified** here.

### 5. Gendered expectations

**Strength: strong (different-sex couples only, by construction).**

These are evidence about failure modes for a gender-neutral mechanism, not inputs to it.

- Ideology moves the fairness threshold. The same inequality is more likely to be seen as unfair, and the unfairness more damaging, for egalitarian than traditional wives (Greenstein 1996; Lavee & Katz 2002). Across 30 nations, both links are strongest where national gender equity is highest (Greenstein 2009).
- Conflict is structurally asymmetric. In two scenario experiments, spouses reported more conflict over housework than over paid work or childcare. Wives more often wanted change, producing a wife-demand/husband-withdraw pattern, and defenders of the status quo were expected to prevail more often than complainants (Kluwer et al. 2000). In a survey of 494 Dutch couples, traditional wives tended to avoid the conflict despite discontent, and lower discontent went with constructive, integrative interaction (Kluwer et al. 1997) **(secondary)**.
- Discrediting contributions: where a man reported his partner did less than she reported, her satisfaction was lower and dissolution more likely (Ruppanner et al. 2017).
- Over- and under-benefit are felt differently by gender: lower satisfaction was tied more to perceived *under*contribution among women and to perceived *over*contribution among men (Adriaans et al. 2026).
- Effects may not be confined to women. In a study of 157 dyads followed from before a nationwide lockdown, exploratory analyses indicated that men who were primary caregivers or not working full-time also had worse relationship outcomes when they did more housework and parenting (Waddell et al. 2021). The authors label this result exploratory.
- The link has changed over time. Equal sharing was more positively related to relationship and sexual satisfaction in a 2006 survey cohort than in a 1992 to 1994 one, attributed to the growing role of perceived equity (Carlson et al. 2018; Carlson et al. 2016).
- Cognitive labour "sticks" to women regardless of resources (Weeks et al. 2025), and women are held to higher cleanliness standards by observers of both sexes (Thébaud et al. 2019).

### 6. Same-sex couples

**Strength: limited to moderate; one large recent comparative study, otherwise small or convenience samples.**

- The fairness mechanism generalises. In the largest comparative study (N = 3,455), satisfaction with the housework division predicted relationship and sexual satisfaction in all couple types. The association with relationship satisfaction was *stronger* in different-sex couples, so gender structure adds to a general fairness effect (Smith et al. 2026).
- In 43 gay and 36 lesbian couples, satisfaction with the division affected relationship satisfaction and stability through perceived equality; who did what tracked interest in the task, even controlling for skill (Kurdek 2007).
- Unequal can be fair. Interviews with ten same-sex couples in New Zealand and Australia found most did not share equally, yet most judged their division fair. The authors attribute this to flexibility, communication and valuing unpaid work as equal to paid work (Beban & Roberts 2023) (small qualitative study).
- A systematic review of 28 studies (2000 to 2015) found lesbian couples divide housework more equally than heterosexual couples and allocate by preference or ability, with samples skewed by race, class and location (Brewster 2016). A seven-country dataset (N = 723) found female couples divide tasks more equally than male couples (van der Vleuten et al. 2020). Adoptive lesbian and gay couples shared more equally than heterosexual ones, with work-hour and income gaps still predicting who did more (Goldberg et al. 2012).
- An analytic review cautions that same-sex couples both "do" and "undo" gender and often frame specialised arrangements as pragmatic and chosen (Goldberg 2013).
- Discrepancy from the ideal matters: in 335 gay fathers (internet sample), the gap between actual and ideal division was related to parent wellbeing and couple functioning (Tornello et al. 2015) **(secondary)**.
- Partner gender moderates harm: unequal division was more negatively related to satisfaction for women partnered with men than with women (N = 227) (Dours & Eaton 2025).
- Appreciation correlates with satisfaction here too (Sutphin 2010).
- Cognitive labour: one interview study of same-gender couples without children describes trust-based patterns and a strengths-based framing that removed gendered opposition (McLean et al. 2023) (small, qualitative).

Caution: a nationally representative time-diary study notes earlier same-sex research relied on convenience samples and self-reports, and that couples may present their divisions as more equal than they are (Fischer 2024).

### 7. Cross-cutting: each partner sees their own work best

**Strength: moderate for the bias; limited for its role in chore conflict.**

- A classic set of experiments, including 37 married couples, found people recall their own contributions to joint activities more readily and accept more responsibility than others attribute to them (Ross & Sicoly 1979) **(secondary)**.
- Over-claiming is reduced when people explicitly consider others' contributions first (Schroeder et al. 2016) (student and academic groups, not couples).
- Partner disagreement about who does what is itself linked to worse outcomes (Ruppanner et al. 2017; Coundouris & Henry 2026).

Cognitive labour is the limiting case: it is invisible even to the person doing it (Daminger 2019).

## Implications for the allocation method

Each item states what to avoid or address and the evidence it rests on.

1. **Optimise perceived fairness for each partner separately; do not assume an equal split is the target.** Unequal divisions are often judged fair (Carriero 2011; Koster et al. 2021; Beban & Roberts 2023) and perception outweighs measured time (Smith et al. 2026). Evidence: strong that perception is the better predictor; mixed on whether equality can be set aside. Counter-evidence: equal sharing of cognitive housework went with the highest satisfaction for both parents (Petts et al. 2025); equal sharing of routine tasks has become more positively tied to relationship quality across cohorts (Carlson et al. 2018; Carlson et al. 2016); and one study found discrepancy in *equality* perceptions, not equity perceptions, is what relates to relationship quality (Coundouris & Henry 2026). Equality may be what many couples now perceive as fair, especially for cognitive labour.
2. **Check both directions.** A partner who feels they do too little is also less satisfied (Adriaans et al. 2026; Gordon et al. 2022). The method should flag over-benefit, not only over-burden. Evidence: moderate (two studies).
3. **Surface and reconcile the two partners' accounts before allocating.** Self-serving recall is expected (Ross & Sicoly 1979), prompting each person to consider the other's contributions reduces it (Schroeder et al. 2016), and unreconciled discrepancy predicts poor outcomes (Ruppanner et al. 2017; Coundouris & Henry 2026). Avoid any design where one partner's account is silently overridden. Evidence: moderate.
4. **Make the process joint.** Deciding together predicts fairness independently of the outcome (Hawkins et al. 1995; Kluwer et al. 2002), and agreement is part of what couples mean by fair (Sarmiento et al. 2024). Avoid presenting an allocation as a verdict. Evidence: moderate.
5. **Treat cognitive labour as first-class work with an owner.** Enumerate anticipating, planning and monitoring explicitly, because otherwise it is invisible, unevenly held and uncredited (Daminger 2019; Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023; Aviv et al. 2024). Avoid assigning execution while leaving noticing and reminding with the other partner. Evidence: moderate that it matters; the specific "own the whole task" remedy is an inference, not a tested intervention.
6. **Build in acknowledgement.** Feeling appreciated removes much of the satisfaction cost of imbalance (Gordon et al. 2022; Klumb et al. 2006; Hawkins et al. 1995). The method should make completed and invisible work visible to the other partner. It should not use appreciation as a substitute for correcting an allocation one partner finds unfair. Evidence: moderate; no intervention trials.
7. **Agree the standard for a task before assigning it.** Evidence that mismatched standards cause conflict is thin (Alberts et al. 2011; Riforgiate 2011), but the mechanism is plausible and cheap to guard against. Avoid letting the partner with the stricter standard become the default owner. Treat this as a hypothesis to test.
8. **Give the dissatisfied partner a route to renegotiate that does not depend on winning an argument.** Housework conflict is asymmetric and favours the status quo (Kluwer et al. 2000). Scheduled review is a reasonable inference. Evidence for the asymmetry: moderate (scenario experiments and one survey, different-sex couples).
9. **Keep the mechanism gender-neutral but test it against gendered failure modes.** The fairness mechanism works across couple types (Smith et al. 2026; Kurdek 2007), while default ownership, discounted contributions and sticky cognitive tasks are the recurring ways things go wrong (Ruppanner et al. 2017; Weeks et al. 2025; Thébaud et al. 2019).
10. **Allow preference- and interest-based allocation.** Same-sex couples commonly allocate by interest and ability and still report fairness (Kurdek 2007; Brewster 2016; Beban & Roberts 2023). Evidence: limited.
11. **Decide how total workload enters.** Fairness of housework is judged against paid work and other load (Koster et al. 2021), and comparison referents vary by person (Hu & Yucel 2017; Thompson 1991). A method that looks at chores in isolation may compute a "fair" split that neither partner experiences as fair.
12. **Measure outcomes by both partners' fairness and satisfaction with the division, tracked over time.** These are the measures that predict relationship outcomes (Tai & Baxter 2018; Smith et al. 2026); time logs alone are the weaker predictor.

## Open questions and gaps

- **No meta-analysis** of housework fairness and relationship satisfaction or conflict was located. The search for one was limited (one bibliographic-database query, two web searches; a filtered Consensus search failed on quota), so one may exist. Pooled effect sizes were not located, and none are reported here.
- **Causality.** Most evidence is cross-sectional. The few within-person analyses include a clear null for sexual outcomes (Hajek 2019). Unhappy partners may simply rate the same split as less fair.
- **Standards of cleanliness** lack any peer-reviewed dyadic test in romantic couples.
- **Interventions.** No trial was found testing whether an allocation procedure, an appreciation practice or explicit cognitive-labour assignment reduces chore conflict. The implications above extrapolate from observational associations.
- **Child-free couples.** Cognitive-labour studies are dominated by parents, especially mothers. Transfer to two-adult households without children is untested.
- **Conflict as the outcome.** Most studies measure satisfaction, not conflict frequency or intensity. Cross-national conflict studies exist (Tai & Baxter 2018; Ruppanner 2009), but couple-level daily conflict data specific to chores were not found. One diary study bundles chores with relatives, leisure and friends in a composite that was the most frequent conflict category, so chore-specific frequency cannot be read from it (Papp et al. 2009).
- **Equity versus equality perceptions.** One study finds discrepancy in equality, not equity, perceptions is what relates to relationship quality (Coundouris & Henry 2026). If replicated this bears directly on the project's "perceived fairness, not 50/50" objective.
- **Samples.** Overwhelmingly Western, different-sex and often married. Same-sex evidence is thin for male couples and for cognitive labour.
- **Measurement.** Fairness is usually a single survey item. Cognitive labour has no standard instrument.
- **Coverage limits of this review.** Consensus quota ran out after one search; PubMed indexes little sociology. Abstracts, not full texts, were the unit of reading.
