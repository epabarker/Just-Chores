# How the deciding process affects perceived fairness

Research ticket: [#6](https://github.com/epabarker/Just-Chores/issues/6). Searched November 2026.

**Question.** How does the *process* by which partners divide household labour affect perceived fairness and satisfaction, independent of the resulting split? Covers procedural justice, voice, explicit negotiation versus implicit drift, communication patterns such as demand/withdraw, how often arrangements should be revisited, and whether people accept algorithm- or procedure-made allocations as fair.

## Short answer

Process matters, and the evidence for that is consistent in direction but mostly correlational.

- In couple studies, deciding together and feeling appreciated predict perceived fairness and relationship quality over and above who does what. The effects are found more reliably for women than for men (Hawkins et al., 1995; Kluwer et al., 2002; Taniguchi & Kaufman, 2020; Gordon et al., 2022).
- In the small qualitative studies available, couples mostly did not explicitly decide. Arrangements drifted into place, and drift tended to reproduce conventional, unequal patterns even among couples who wanted to share (Wiesmann et al., 2008; Miller & Sassler, 2010; Daminger, 2020).
- The damaging communication pattern is demand/withdraw. It is driven largely by an asymmetry in who wants change, more than by gender as such, and it appears in same-sex couples too (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Kluwer et al., 2000; Holley et al., 2010). A meta-analysis links it to worse relational outcomes at a moderate effect size (Schrodt et al., 2013).
- No study was found that tests how often a chore arrangement should be revisited. The nearest evidence points to explicit deciding at set-up, review at life transitions, and a scheduled check-up, with the specific interval left as a design choice.
- People do not automatically accept an algorithm's allocation as fair. In the one direct experiment found on algorithmic versus discussed "social division" decisions, algorithmic allocations were judged less fair than discussed ones (Lee & Baykal, 2017). Letting people adjust the result improved perceived fairness; explaining the algorithm's fairness guarantees alone did not (Lee et al., 2019).

## How to read this document

- **Source basis.** Unless stated otherwise, findings are taken from article abstracts and bibliographic records returned by Crossref and OpenAlex, plus publisher pages where they could be fetched. Full texts were mostly not accessible. Effect sizes are reported only where the abstract gives them.
- **Design and country labels.** "Cross-sectional" is inferred from an abstract describing a single survey with no follow-up; abstracts rarely state it. Sample country is given only where the abstract or publisher page states it.
- **Search limits.** The Consensus quota was exhausted before any query returned. PubMed indexed almost none of this (largely sociological and communication) literature. The search therefore relied on Crossref, OpenAlex and web search, and is not a systematic review.
- **Strength labels.** *Strong*: meta-analysis or systematic review, or several convergent designs. *Moderate*: several independent studies, mostly correlational. *Weak*: single studies, qualitative work, or extrapolation from another setting.
- **Gender.** Almost all couple samples are different-sex couples. Gendered results are reported as evidence about failure modes, in line with the project's gender-neutral mechanism.

## Findings

### 1. Procedural justice and voice in couples' division of labour

**Strength: moderate. Consistent direction; cross-sectional or survey-based; effects clearer for women.**

- **Deciding together predicts fairness.** In 234 dual-earner wives in the western United States, feeling appreciated was the strongest predictor of perceived fairness of family work, and "deciding together how things would be divided" was also a strong predictor. Other factors worked only indirectly, through the division itself (Hawkins et al., 1995). Cross-sectional; wives only.
- **Procedural justice predicts fairness over time, mainly for wives.** A three-wave longitudinal survey of 293 couples (country not stated in the abstract) across the transition to parenthood tested wants and values, social comparisons and procedural justice as predictors of fairness judgements. The full model was supported for wives at every wave. For husbands the main predictors were wants and values and social comparisons (Kluwer et al., 2002). The abstract does not report how procedural justice was measured.
- **Perceived justice and satisfaction: wives yes, husbands only indirectly.** In 389 dual-earner couples from Austria, Germany and Switzerland, perceived distributive and procedural justice related to wives' relationship satisfaction but not husbands'. Wives' perceived justice related to husbands' satisfaction through the conflict husbands experienced (Mikula et al., 2011). Cross-sectional. The abstract does not separate the procedural effect from the distributive one, so this study does not by itself show an independent process effect.
- **Joint decision-making helps regardless of the split.** In a Japanese midlife sample, joint decision-making predicted higher marital quality for both genders, more strongly for women. Perceived fairness of the housework division predicted marital quality for wives only. The authors conclude joint decision-making may ease strain even where the division is uneven (Taniguchi & Kaufman, 2020). Cross-sectional; the decision-making measure is general, not specific to chores.
- **Fair treatment in conflict matters more to women, unless commitment is high.** Four Dutch studies (critical incidents and scenarios; dating, cohabiting and married participants) found the link between fairness of treatment and affect or satisfaction was stronger for women. The gender difference disappeared under high relationship commitment, and making the relationship salient raised men's sensitivity to fair treatment (Kluwer et al., 2009). About conflict in general, not chores.
- **Appreciation acts as an interpersonal-justice buffer.** Across three samples of people cohabiting with a partner during the COVID-19 pandemic (N = 2,193, including 476 couples), doing more of the housework and perceiving the division as unfair predicted lower satisfaction, but these effects disappeared when people felt appreciated. The buffering generalised across gender, employment status, age, socioeconomic status and relationship length (Gordon et al., 2022). A priori predictions, three samples, followed over weeks to months; a strong single paper.
- **Framework.** The distributive-justice account of family work lists outcome values, comparison referents and justifications as the three sources of a sense of fairness (Thompson, 1991). Later empirical work added procedure to these, as above. A 1998 review of household labour and perceived justice exists (Mikula, 1998), but its content could not be retrieved, so nothing is claimed from it (**unverified content**).

**Null and limiting findings.** Justice effects for men are repeatedly absent or indirect (Mikula et al., 2011; Kluwer et al., 2002; Taniguchi & Kaufman, 2020). No experiment was found that manipulates the deciding process for housework in real couples while holding the split constant. "Independent of the split" therefore rests on statistical control in surveys, not on experimental isolation.

### 2. Voice and the fair-process effect outside couples

**Strength: strong in organisational settings; transfer to couples is an assumption.**

- **Procedural justice has unique effects.** A meta-analysis of 183 organisational justice studies found that distributive, procedural, interpersonal and informational justice are moderately to highly related, yet each explains incremental variance in fairness perceptions and in outcomes such as satisfaction and commitment (Colquitt et al., 2001). Organisational setting, with a formal authority that couples lack.
- **Process matters most when the outcome is poor.** A review of 45 independent samples found procedural justice is more strongly related to people's reactions when outcome fairness or favourability is low, and outcome matters more when procedural justice is low (Brockner & Wiesenfeld, 1996). Organisational and other non-intimate settings. Implication: a partner who gets the worse end of an allocation is the one for whom a fair process counts most.
- **Fair process aids acceptance of negotiated agreements.** Two correlational studies of bilateral negotiation found that procedural justice encouraged acceptance of the agreement and went with more integrative bargaining (Hollander-Blumoff & Tyler, 2008). Peer negotiation, which is closer to the couple case than employer-employee studies.
- **Procedures are valued in themselves.** Economics reaches the same position. A review introduces "procedural utility", the value people place on how an outcome is reached (Frey et al., 2004). Lottery experiments find procedural fairness is conceptually distinct from allocation fairness, though linked to it (Bolton et al., 2005). Laboratory games with strangers and money.
- **Voice without influence.** A classic experiment on instrumental versus non-instrumental voice exists (Lind et al., 1990), but its abstract could not be retrieved, so its result is not relied on here (**unverified content**).

### 3. Explicit negotiation versus implicit drift

**Strength: weak to moderate. Convergent small qualitative studies plus one panel study. Claims about what "most couples" do rest on samples of 20 to 35 couples.**

- **Couples in the one study that examined it mostly did not explicitly decide.** A qualitative study of 31 Dutch couples in the formative, pre-child years found couples generally did not look explicitly at their division of paid and domestic work, and that this often perpetuated a gendered division. Explicit decision-making occurred when a couple strongly preferred equal sharing, or when partners disagreed or were frustrated. Even couples preferring equality often ended up in traditional patterns. The authors suggest couples wanting to share should decide explicitly "until they have developed egalitarian routines" (Wiesmann et al., 2008).
- **Drift moves toward convention.** In interviews with both partners of 30 cohabiting couples with moderate education (country not stated in the abstract), few began by sharing equally, and the number in equal exchanges declined over time while conventional exchanges grew (Miller & Sassler, 2010).
- **Couples describe the process as pragmatic and adjust when problems arise.** A grounded-theory study of 20 heterosexual dual-earner couples found couples saw themselves as first dividing work in the way most beneficial to them as a couple, then adjusting to minimise the impact when a task or arrangement caused problems (Carlson & Hans, 2017). Self-reported, retrospective accounts of process.
- **A process that feels fair can hide an unequal outcome.** In 32 different-sex, college-educated couples, respondents separated allocation processes from outcomes. When they understood the process as gender-neutral, they wrote off unequal outcomes as incidental compromises. This prevented conflict, but the author argues it may also facilitate stasis by directing attention away from inequality (Daminger, 2020). This is the main caution against treating perceived procedural fairness as sufficient.
- **Cognitive labour is the part most likely to be left implicit.** Interviews with 35 couples describe cognitive labour as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. Being taxing but often invisible to both partners, it is a frequent source of conflict. Women did more anticipating and monitoring, while participation in decision-making was roughly equal (Daminger, 2019). A PRISMA systematic review of 31 articles confirms women do the larger share of mental labour and bear more of its negative consequences (Reich-Stiebert et al., 2023).
- **Same-sex couples.** An analytic review reports that same-sex couples with specialised divisions tend to interpret their arrangements as pragmatic and chosen (Goldberg, 2013). In 43 gay and 36 lesbian couples, who did a task tracked interest in it even after controlling for skill, and satisfaction with the division affected relationship satisfaction and stability through perceived equality (Kurdek, 2007). These fit the view that preference-based, chosen allocation supports perceived fairness, but neither study measures explicit negotiation directly.
- **Theory.** An integrative theory proposes that differing "threshold levels" for noticing mess, together with self-organising routines and the economy of gratitude, let one partner become the default performer of a task without any decision being made (Alberts et al., 2011). Theoretical paper, not tested here. A chapter on implicit and explicit decision-making styles in couples was located (Sillars & Kalbfleisch, 1989) but its content could not be retrieved (**unverified content**).

**Negotiation and conflict are not the same as good process.**

- **Raising the issue can change the split.** In 219 newlywed heterosexual US couples followed over about 3.5 years (three waves), more conflict about labour inequity predicted declines in inequity by the next wave, and greater inequity predicted declines in conflict (Adelson et al., 2021). Voicing dissatisfaction works to some degree. The second result suggests unequal couples go quiet rather than resolve the issue.
- **Whether negotiation pays off depends on the partner's response.** In 204 newly cohabiting Canadian heterosexual couples, the link between a partner's housework contribution and satisfaction ran through equity-restoring strategies and depended on how strongly the other partner used equity-resisting strategies (Charbonneau et al., 2019). Dyadic, cross-sectional; taken from the publisher's abstract page.
- **Communication quality is entangled with the division itself.** In 487 heterosexual couples from the 2006 Marital and Relationship Survey, the quality of women's communication predicted how housework was divided, whereas men's communication quality appeared to be an outcome of the arrangement. Men's communication quality mediated the link between division and women's satisfaction (Carlson et al., 2020). Instrumental-variable and dyadic models on cross-sectional 2006 data, so causal direction is suggestive only.
- **Sharing tasks, not just balancing totals.** In two datasets, the US National Survey of Families and Households (N = 10,498) and the 2006 Marital and Relationship Survey (N = 1,065), the number of tasks partners shared equally was positively associated with relationship quality, including perceived equity, for both men and women. The male partner's overall share was positive for women but negative for men (Carlson, 2022). Cross-sectional. This is an outcome-structure finding, not a process finding, but it bears on what a protocol should offer as options.

### 4. Demand/withdraw

**Strength: strong for the general pattern (meta-analysis); moderate for its link to housework (several studies, two based on hypothetical scenarios).**

- **General effect.** A meta-analysis of 74 studies (N = 14,255) found a moderate association between demand/withdraw and outcomes overall (r = .360), with similar sizes for wife-demand/husband-withdraw (r = .380) and husband-demand/wife-withdraw (r = .392). Effects were larger in distressed or clinical samples (r = .413) than non-distressed ones (r = .345), and larger for relational (r = .423) and communicative (r = .418) outcomes than for well-being (r = .249) (Schrodt et al., 2013). Not chore-specific; associations, not causal estimates.
- **Distinct from general negativity.** Demand/withdraw accounted for variation in marital satisfaction over and above partners' negativity and affection, and the association may be weaker when a partner frequently expresses affection (Caughlin & Huston, 2002).
- **Housework is a prime trigger.** A survey of 494 Dutch couples found the wife's discontent with housework related to wife-demand/husband-withdraw interaction, which predicted destructive conflict outcomes. Mutually integrative interaction predicted constructive outcomes and was more likely at lower discontent. Traditional wives, and wives with traditional husbands, tended to avoid the conflict despite being discontent (Kluwer et al., 1997). Two scenario experiments found spouses reported more conflict over housework than over paid work or childcare, and more wife-demand/husband-withdraw only when the wife wanted change in the husband's contribution. The authors describe an asymmetric conflict in which one partner wants change and the other benefits from the status quo (Kluwer et al., 2000). Hypothetical scenarios, not observed behaviour.
- **The driver is largely who wants change.** In 31 couples observed in two discussions, there was a significant main effect of gender (wife-demand/husband-withdraw was more likely than the reverse) and an interaction with conflict structure: that asymmetry held only when discussing a change the wife wanted. Each partner demanded more when discussing a change they wanted and withdrew more when discussing a change their partner wanted. Men were more withdrawn overall, but women were not more demanding overall (Christensen & Heavey, 1990). A coding study of influence techniques found the role of "person wanting change" predicted influence behaviour and gender did not (Sagrestano et al., 1998). In 63 heterosexual, gay and lesbian couples, the pattern appeared in every couple type, and differences in desired change predicted who demanded and who withdrew (Holley et al., 2010). Small samples, but the result replicates across designs.
- **Counter-evidence on gender asymmetry.** In home diaries from 116 US couples, the two gendered forms occurred equally often, the conflict initiator tended to be the demander, and both forms predicted negative emotion and lower conflict resolution after accounting for marital satisfaction (Papp et al., 2009).
- **An earlier paper** on marital conflict about household labour and paid work was located (Kluwer et al., 1996) but its abstract could not be retrieved (**unverified content**).

**Reading for the project.** Demand/withdraw is a structural result of one partner holding a grievance while the other holds the status quo and controls whether the topic gets discussed. A gender-neutral mechanism should expect it whenever only one partner wants to renegotiate.

### 5. How often to revisit

**Strength: weak. No direct evidence on cadence for chore arrangements was found.**

- **Gap.** No study was found that compares review intervals for a household division, or that tests scheduled chore check-ins against none.
- **Transitions are when divisions shift and expectations break.** In an Australian two-wave panel of 1,091 men and women, men's housework time was stable across most transitions, women's changed considerably around parenthood, and the authors call the transition to parenthood a critical moment for the emergence of an unequal gap (Baxter et al., 2008). Violated expectations about the postpartum division were related to more negative feelings about some aspects of the marriage, in 670 women cross-sectionally and 48 longitudinally (Ruble et al., 1988). A newlywed sample assessed 7 to 11 times found the link between confirmed expectations and satisfaction change depended on the type of expectation (Lawrence et al., 2007), so the expectancy effect is not uniform.
- **Early explicitness.** The suggestion to decide explicitly until routines form comes from one qualitative study (Wiesmann et al., 2008). No evidence was found on how long routines take to set.
- **Scheduled check-ups work for relationship health in general.** A randomised trial of the Marriage Checkup (215 US married couples; assessment and feedback visits at baseline and one year later; nine assessments over two years) found small to medium between-group differences in intimacy (median d = .37), women's felt acceptance (median d = .34), men's felt acceptance (median d = .25) and relationship distress (median d = .23) (Cordova et al., 2014). The checkup is therapist-led and not about chores. It shows an annual, structured review can produce measurable benefit; it does not show annual is the best interval.
- **Communication skills are teachable, modestly.** A meta-analysis of 143 evaluation studies of marriage and relationship education found modest effects on communication skills, for both well-functioning and more distressed couples (Blanchard et al., 2009).

### 6. Acceptance of algorithm- or procedure-made allocations

**Strength: weak. Two small HCI studies bear on division tasks; the two systematic reviews cover adjacent settings and their abstracts give no direction of effect; no couple-specific study was found.**

- **Discussion beat the algorithm in the closest available test.** A qualitative study and an experiment examined provably fair division algorithms applied to social division tasks. About a third of participants in the first study perceived the algorithmic decision as less than fair (30% for self, 36% for the group). Reasons were that the algorithm's assumptions did not allow for multiple concepts of fairness or for social behaviour, and that quantifying preferences through the interface was error-prone. In the experiment, algorithmic decisions were perceived as less fair than discussion-based ones, depending on participants' interpersonal power and programming knowledge. The authors conclude algorithms and interfaces should account for social and altruistic behaviour (Lee & Baykal, 2017). Conference paper; groups, not romantic couples.
- **Control over the outcome helps; explanation of guarantees alone does not.** A within-subjects laboratory study of a goods-division interface found that explaining the algorithm's fairness properties did not by itself increase perceived fairness. Outcome explanation had mixed effects. Letting the group interactively adjust the allocation "universally improved perceived fairness" (Lee et al., 2019). Single laboratory study; goods, not chores.
- **Small amounts of control suffice in forecasting tasks.** Three incentivised experiments found people were considerably more likely to use an imperfect algorithm when they could modify its output, even slightly, and were more satisfied with the process (Dietvorst et al., 2018). Forecasting, not allocation.
- **Task type matters.** In an online experiment on managerial decisions, algorithmic and human decisions were seen as equally fair for "mechanical" tasks, but algorithmic decisions were seen as less fair and less trustworthy, and evoked more negative emotion, for tasks needing "human" skills, owing to a perceived lack of intuition and subjective judgement (Lee, 2018). Workplace vignettes. Whether dividing chores registers as a mechanical or a human task was not tested.
- **Reviews.** A systematic review of 58 empirical studies on fairness perceptions of algorithmic decision-making reports heterogeneous concepts and measures and evidence drawn almost exclusively from Western democracies (Starke et al., 2022). A systematic review of 80 studies on algorithm aversion groups its drivers into algorithm, individual, task and higher-level factors (Mahmud et al., 2022). Neither abstract gives a pooled direction of effect, so no general claim that people prefer or reject algorithms is made here.
- **Which fair solution is chosen matters to users.** A user study with users of a fair-division website found people judged the maximin envy-free rent division significantly fairer than arbitrary envy-free solutions (Gal et al., 2017; user-study finding read from the abstract of the conference extended abstract of the same title). Formal envy-freeness is not enough for an outcome to feel fair. Involves money and housemates.
- **Envy-free procedures are perceived as fairer in the laboratory.** An experiment found envy-free cake-cutting procedures, including cut-and-choose, were fairer and were perceived as fairer than proportional ones, even though participants often manipulated them, and that participants learned each other's preferences over repeated play and used that to improve their own share (Kyropoulou et al., 2022; findings taken from the abstract of the 2018 preprint of the same title). Laboratory setting with strangers. The manipulation result is relevant to any repeated two-person procedure.

## Implications for the participation protocol

Each item is tagged with how directly the evidence supports it.

1. **Make the set-up an explicit, joint decision.** Both partners take part in choosing the allocation; nothing is assigned by default or inherited from current habit. *Supported by couple evidence, moderate* (Hawkins et al., 1995; Taniguchi & Kaufman, 2020; Wiesmann et al., 2008).
2. **The algorithm proposes; the couple decides.** Present the algorithm's output as a draft that both partners can adjust before accepting. *Supported by HCI evidence on groups, weak for couples* (Lee & Baykal, 2017; Lee et al., 2019; Dietvorst et al., 2018).
3. **Do not rely on stating fairness guarantees to produce buy-in.** Explanation alone did not raise perceived fairness in the one study that tested it. *Weak, single study* (Lee et al., 2019).
4. **Let preferences be entered in forgiving ways and corrected later.** Errors in quantifying preferences were a named source of perceived unfairness. *Weak, single study* (Lee & Baykal, 2017).
5. **Elicit both partners' inputs independently and symmetrically before any discussion.** This removes the structure in which one partner must raise a grievance and the other can withdraw. *Inference from the conflict-structure evidence; not tested as an intervention* (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Kluwer et al., 2000; Holley et al., 2010).
6. **Either partner can trigger a review, and a trigger cannot be ignored.** Withdrawal is the status-quo holder's best response to a demand. Silence in unequal couples is not evidence of contentment. *Inference* (Kluwer et al., 1997; Adelson et al., 2021).
7. **Put cognitive labour on the list explicitly.** Anticipating and monitoring are invisible by default and a frequent conflict source. *Supported, moderate* (Daminger, 2019; Reich-Stiebert et al., 2023).
8. **Show outcomes alongside process.** A process that feels neutral can make a lopsided result easier to explain away. Report the resulting split and each partner's fairness rating at every review. *Supported by one qualitative study* (Daminger, 2020).
9. **Record expectations, then check them.** Unmet expectations about the division, not only the division itself, relate to dissatisfaction. *Moderate; mainly transition-to-parenthood samples* (Ruble et al., 1988; Lawrence et al., 2007).
10. **Review at transitions, plus a scheduled check-in.** Trigger a review at changes in work hours, a new child, a move or a change in health. Add a recurring check-in whose interval is a product decision, not an evidence-based figure. A plausible shape is more frequent while routines are forming and less frequent afterwards. *Weak; extrapolated* (Baxter et al., 2008; Wiesmann et al., 2008; Cordova et al., 2014).
11. **Build in acknowledgement.** Felt appreciation removed the satisfaction cost of unequal and unfair-seeming divisions in a large multi-sample study. *Moderate; one strong paper plus an older survey* (Gordon et al., 2022; Hawkins et al., 1995).
12. **Offer shared tasks as an option, not only split tasks.** *Moderate, cross-sectional* (Carlson, 2022).
13. **Pay most attention to the partner who comes out with the less favourable allocation.** Process quality affects reactions most when outcomes are poor. *Strong in organisations, untested in couples* (Brockner & Wiesenfeld, 1996).
14. **Expect asymmetric sensitivity.** In different-sex samples, fairness and process effects were reliably found for women and often not for men. A gender-neutral design should read this as: the partner carrying more load, or holding a grievance, is the one whose fairness perception moves. That reading is an interpretation, not a tested result (Mikula et al., 2011; Kluwer et al., 2002; Kluwer et al., 2009).

## Open questions and gaps

- **No causal test in couples.** No experiment was found that varies the deciding process for housework while holding the split fixed. Whether process effects are causal, and how large, is unknown.
- **No evidence on review cadence.** Interval, format and duration of chore check-ins are untested.
- **No couple-specific study of algorithmic chore allocation.** The closest evidence is from groups dividing goods, rent or mixed tasks in the laboratory, or from housemates.
- **Men, same-sex couples and non-Western samples are under-studied.** Null effects for men may reflect who carries the burden in these samples and not gender itself. No study was found that separates the two.
- **Voice without influence.** Whether being heard helps when it does not change the allocation was not verified in this search (Lind et al., 1990, unverified content). This matters if an algorithm takes inputs but partners cannot see their effect.
- **Strategic behaviour over repeated rounds.** Laboratory participants learned each other's preferences and exploited them (Kyropoulou et al., 2022). Whether intimate partners do the same, and whether it harms perceived fairness, is unknown.
- **Does perceived procedural fairness entrench unequal outcomes?** Daminger (2020) suggests it can. Since the project's objective is each couple's own perceived fairness, it needs a stated position on whether a split that both partners call fair, but that one partner might judge differently with better information, counts as success.
- **Abstract-level reading.** Measures of "procedural justice" and "decision-making" differ between studies and could not be inspected. A full-text pass on Kluwer et al. (2002), Mikula et al. (2011) and Mikula (1998) would firm up section 1.
