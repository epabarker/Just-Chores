# Existing chore-division systems and interventions

Research ticket: [#7](https://github.com/epabarker/Just-Chores/issues/7). Searched 2026-11-16.

Question: what existing systems and interventions aim to make the division of household labour fairer, and what evidence is there that they work?

## Short answer

These searches found no system or intervention that has been shown, in a controlled comparison, to make a couple's division of chores fairer in the couple's own eyes.

- **Fair Play** is the only commercial system for which any published evaluation was found. The evidence is one small open-trial pilot (87 mothers, no between-group comparison reported) and a non-peer-reviewed report prepared in coordination with the vendor's institute. Both show that mothers who completed more of the online course reported a more equal split afterwards. Neither shows the course caused the change, a second cohort showed no significant change, and only about a quarter of those invited finished [1][3].
- **Chore apps** (Nipto, Sweepy, Cozi and similar) have no evaluation of any kind that these searches could find. Their sites cite downloads, reviews and press quotes [20][21][22].
- **Academic fair-division tools** (Spliddit's chores app, Kajibuntan) have mathematical guarantees but no published evaluation of whether couples find the result fair or act on it [14][15][19]. The nearest evidence is a warning from studies of algorithmic "social division" (which tasks were divided in [17] was not verified; [18] used goods): about a third of participants judged a provably fair algorithmic allocation less than fair, and letting people adjust the result improved perceived fairness [17][18].
- **Couple and relationship education** has good trial evidence for communication and relationship quality, but division of labour is rarely an outcome [9][10][11]. The main studies found with a housework-sharing outcome gave one positive result (1994, design details unverified), one null randomised trial (2002, 151 couples) and one randomised trial where attitudes shifted but housework involvement, a secondary outcome, did not (2026, 262 fathers) [5][6][7].
- **The strongest causal evidence that sharing can be shifted** comes from settings far from this project: gender-norm programmes in sub-Saharan Africa [23][24][25] and a paid-leave policy reform [26]. These rely on changing gendered norms or on money and policy, not on a gender-neutral allocation procedure.

Almost every study measures the share of work or men's involvement. A few also measure satisfaction with the arrangement (positive in [5] and, for parenting tasks, [9]; null in [6]). None measures each partner's perceived fairness of an allocation procedure. That is the gap this project sits in.

## Findings by theme

### 1. Fair Play

**What it is.** A book and a deck of 100 task cards. The first-party pages describe four "easy-to-follow rules", 100 household tasks and a "figurative card game" played with a partner [29][30]. Each card covers both the Planning and the Execution of a task; the system uses the cards "to quantify and visualize couples' respective domestic labor burdens" and to reallocate them [3]. An eight-week online course (65 lessons in nine modules, with optional coaching) delivers it [3]. The first-party book page describes the aim as "rebalancing" and does not state a 50/50 target [29]. The four rules themselves are not spelled out on the pages retrieved, so they are not described here.

**Mechanism.** Visibility of cognitive labour, a shared vocabulary, and assigning whole tasks. Participants' free-text feedback in the report names the same things: a prompt for conversation, new vocabulary, and recognition of unseen work [3].

**Evidence, treated as one stream.** Three documents come from the same university research group, and the first two appear to describe the same first cohort (the pilot's 87 participants and 54% completion match the report's 86 starters and 47 finishers; this overlap is inferred from the numbers, not confirmed from the pilot's full text).

- *Peer-reviewed pilot* [1] (according to PubMed). Design: "small, open-trial pilot". Sample: 87 heterosexual, predominantly White US mothers of preschool-aged children. 54% completed the full intervention. Participants who completed more of it reported a significantly more egalitarian division at posttest. Mothers reporting a more egalitarian cognitive split afterwards reported lower depression and stress; a more egalitarian physical split went with better relationship functioning. The authors say results "warrant replication within a larger and more diverse sample". The pilot's funding and conflict-of-interest statements were not retrieved.
- *Final report, December 2024* [3]. Not peer reviewed; "prepared in coordination with the Fair Play Policy Institute". Two cohorts.
  - Cohort 1: 255 eligible and interested people were randomised, 167 to the intervention and 88 to a waitlist. The report's text gives **no between-arm comparison**. Its findings are within-group: people who completed more modules showed a more equal split, and larger shifts were associated with fewer depressive symptoms and lower stress.
  - Cohort 2 (employees of one company; 170 invited to the intervention): "We did not see a significant change in division of labor".
  - Hair cortisol: no effect.
  - Attrition: of 337 people invited to the intervention, 190 (56%) started and 89 to 92 (26% to 27%; the report gives both figures) finished. The step where cards are actually reallocated comes in session 5, which only 106 (31%) reached. The authors write that "it is important to interpret our results with caution".
  - Sample: over 90% women; no both-partner (dyadic) data are reported; mostly White and degree-educated.
- *Cross-sectional paper* [2] (according to PubMed). 322 mothers rated 30 of the cards. They reported doing about 73% of cognitive and 64% of physical labour. Cognitive share was associated with depression, stress, burnout and relationship quality; physical share only with relationship quality. This is descriptive, mother-report only, and the authors note the measure "has not been empirically validated". The deck's creator is a co-author. It says nothing about whether the method works.

**Vendor claims.** The institute's own summary says 61% of "participants who participated in at least some of the Fair Play Method" saw an increase in overall egalitarianism [4]. That figure does not appear in the text extracted from the report, has no stated denominator or comparison group, and should be read as a vendor claim.

**Verdict.** Promising but unproven. Dose-response among self-selected completers cannot separate the effect of the course from the kind of person (or partner) who finishes it.

### 2. Chore-allocation apps

- **Nipto**: tasks earn points scaled by effort, there is a weekly race, streak bonuses, rewards, reminders and rotation of turns according to who did a task last. Aimed at families, couples and roommates. Evidence offered: download counts, app-store ratings, press quotes and user reviews [20].
- **Sweepy**: cleaning tracker with effort points, a scheduling feature and a household leaderboard. Claims to have "helped over 1,000,000 households" with no source [21].
- **Cozi**: shared family organiser with real-time to-do and chore lists. Makes no fairness claim and offers no evidence on division of labour [22].
- Journalism-sourced only [31]: a technology magazine feature describes a shared-pool app (tasks are picked from a pool rather than delegated), a prototype that would crowdsource how households split chores, and an academic's pilot chore calendar. No results are reported for any of them. The same article gives one user's account of a points app shifting her household "from something like 90-10 to more like 60-40", which is a single anecdote, and reports sociologists' concerns that list-style apps leave one partner as the manager and that the partner doing less has to buy in.

No peer-reviewed or independent evaluation of any consumer chore app was found in these searches. This is absence of evidence, not evidence that they fail.

### 3. Academic fair-division tools for the public

- **Spliddit** launched in 2014 with rent, goods and credit division [14]. A chores (tasks) app was added in April 2015 after requests mainly from medical staff scheduling shifts [16]. For chores it "asks each participant to report how much they dislike one chore over another" and returns a split that is Pareto optimal and equitable, meaning everyone equally dislikes their own bundle [15]. The site reported over 100,000 users and 35,000 instances across all apps by 2017 [15]. Evidence of user satisfaction in the developers' own account is a selection of positive user comments [15]. The developers expected that imperfect one-off allocations "tend to balance each other out" when the process repeats; that is an expectation, not a finding [16]. Whether the site is still operating could not be confirmed (the fetch failed).
- **No evaluation specific to the chores app was found.** For rent division, a user study on users' own real instances found people rated the maximin solution significantly fairer than an arbitrary envy-free one [14a]. That setting involves money changing hands, which this project excludes.
- **Perceived fairness of algorithmic division** (Lee and Baykal, CSCW 2017) [17]. Two studies of "mathematically-proven fair division algorithms making social division decisions". In the qualitative study about one third of participants perceived the algorithm's decision as less than fair (30% for self, 36% for group), "often because algorithmic assumptions about users did not account for multiple concepts of fairness or social behaviors, and the process of quantifying preferences through interfaces was prone to error". In the experiment, algorithmic decisions were perceived as less fair than discussion-based ones, depending on interpersonal power and programming knowledge. A later survey paper describes the comparison as being with "outcomes prescribed by algorithms on Spliddit" [39]. Only the abstract was retrieved, so which division tasks were tested (and whether chores were among them) is **not verified**. If chores were included, this would be the only perception evidence on an algorithmic chore split and is worth reading in full.
- **Transparency and outcome control** (Lee et al., CSCW 2019) [18]. Within-subjects lab study on goods division using an interface the authors built. Explaining the algorithm's fairness properties alone "did not increase perceived fairness"; explaining outcomes had mixed effects; letting the group adjust the allocation "universally improved perceived fairness".
- **Kajibuntan** (Igarashi and Yokoyama, AAAI 2023) [19]. The only tool found that is built for exactly two partners and household chores. Couples enter the chores, their preferences and the current allocation; the app visualises the split and proposes allocations from fair-division theory, and partners can swap chores manually. It drew almost 13,000 unique visitors in its first month after a television feature. The paper is three pages; its abstract describes the system and reports no user-outcome evaluation (full text not retrieved).

### 4. Couple-focused interventions and relationship education

**Programmes that targeted housework directly.**

- Hawkins and Roberts (1992) reviewed earlier attempts and concluded that "interventions to help dual-earner couples share domestic labor are rare" and that related efforts to raise fathers' time in child care "have met with limited success" [8].
- Hawkins et al. (1994) evaluated a family life education programme built on that analysis. The abstract reports "small increases in husbands' involvement in both housework and child care" and "large increases in wives' satisfaction with domestic work arrangements", persisting 6 months after the programme [5]. Single study; sample size and comparison design were not retrievable and are **unverified**. Satisfaction with the arrangement is the outcome closest to this project's objective, and here it moved more than the share did.
- Gjerdingen and Center (2002), according to PubMed: randomised trial, 151 couples expecting a first child, support and work-planning intervention versus standard prenatal classes. Outcomes included housework sharing and satisfaction with housework sharing at 6 months postpartum. "No significant group differences on postpartum health or work outcomes"; fathers showed only "a trend toward less postpartum decline in housework sharing" [6]. A clear null.
- Terada et al. (2026), according to PubMed: single-blind randomised trial in Japan, 262 fathers, brief online motivation-based session. Traditional gender-role attitudes fell at 3 and 6 months, but there were "no significant differences" in mother-rated paternal empathy or childcare and housework involvement [7]. Attitude change without behaviour change.
- Nakajima et al. (2023), according to PubMed: non-randomised, 15 versus 15 participants, pregnancy programme in Japan; wives' satisfaction with husbands' housework support "did not decline" at 3 months [32]. Too small and uncontrolled to carry weight.
- A randomised trial of an information booklet for expectant fathers lists housework engagement as a secondary outcome, but only the protocol was found [33].

**Broader couple programmes.**

- Shapiro, Gottman and Fink (2019), according to PubMed: randomised trial, 136 pregnant couples, Bringing Baby Home. Intervention fathers reported more involvement in parenting tasks and more satisfaction with the division of parenting labour [9]. The outcome is parenting tasks, self-reported by fathers, not routine housework.
- Xiao and Loke (2021), according to PubMed: systematic review of 12 randomised coparenting trials. Positive effects on coparenting support, communication and maternal depressive symptoms, but "limited evidence was found on the overall effects on co-parenting, division of labor" [12].
- Suto et al. (2016), according to PubMed: systematic review of 11 trials of prenatal education for partners (some covering housework sharing). Interventions were too diverse to judge; no sufficient evidence of benefit for paternal depression or the couple relationship [13].
- Pinquart and Teubert (2010): meta-analysis of 21 controlled couple interventions around the birth of a child. Small effects on communication (d = .28) and wellbeing (d = .21), very small on couple adjustment (d = .09). The abstract reports no division-of-labour outcome [11].
- Hawkins et al. (2008): meta-analysis of 117 studies of marriage and relationship education. Effects of d = .30 to .36 on relationship quality and .43 to .45 on communication in experimental studies. The abstract reports no division-of-labour outcome [10].
- Family Foundations [27] and Couple CARE for Parents [28] report randomised-trial effects on coparenting, relationship and parenting outcomes; their abstracts report no housework outcome.

**Why communication may still matter.** In survey data on 487 couples, the quality of women's communication with their partner predicted how housework was divided, and men's communication quality mediated the link between division and women's relationship satisfaction [34]. This is observational.

### 5. Programmes and policies outside the project's scope (context only)

- Rwanda (Doyle et al. 2018), according to PubMed: randomised trial, 575 intervention and 624 control couples, gender-transformative couples programme. Higher reported men's participation in childcare and household tasks at 21 months, by both women's and men's report [23].
- Uganda (Kyegombe et al. 2014), according to PubMed: cluster-randomised community mobilisation; men more likely to report participating in household tasks (adjusted risk ratio 1.48) [24].
- Cote d'Ivoire (Hossain et al. 2014), according to PubMed: pilot cluster-randomised men's discussion groups; higher reported participation in gendered household tasks (adjusted risk ratio 2.47) [25].
- Quebec (Patnaik 2019): reserving paid leave for fathers produced "causal evidence that paternity leave reduces sex specialization long after the leave period" [26]. A Norwegian study of the causal effects of paternity leave was located but its findings could not be retrieved and are not reported here [35].

These show that reported sharing can be moved, but through explicitly gendered norm change or state policy, in other populations, with self-reported behaviour as the outcome.

### 6. What the wider literature says about why this is hard

- Cognitive labour (anticipating, identifying options, deciding, monitoring) is a distinct, largely invisible dimension of housework and "a frequent source of conflict" (70 interviews, 35 couples) [36].
- Couples who hold egalitarian ideals but live unequal arrangements often explain the gap by seeing their allocation *process* as gender-neutral, which "helps prevent spousal conflict, but it may also facilitate behavioral stasis" (64 interviews, 32 couples) [37]. This is a direct caution for any tool whose selling point is a neutral process.
- A 2026 observational study of postpartum couples ends by stating that "interventional evidence is required to optimize domestic labor" [38], which matches what these searches found.

## Implications for the allocation method and protocol

1. **There is no validated baseline to beat, and no validated outcome to copy.** No existing system has shown an effect on each partner's perceived fairness. The project will need its own outcome measure and, if it wants to claim effectiveness, its own controlled evaluation with a between-group, intention-to-treat comparison (the step the Fair Play evaluation did not report [3]).
2. **Measure both partners.** The Fair Play studies rely on one partner's report, almost always the mother's [1][2][3]. A method whose objective is the couple's perceived fairness has to elicit and evaluate from both people.
3. **Treat planning and execution as separate things to allocate.** The split between cognitive and physical work is where the largest reported imbalance and the stronger wellbeing associations sit [2][36]. A chore list that only counts execution will miss it.
4. **Engagement is the binding constraint.** Only 31% of invited participants reached the reallocation step of an eight-week course [3]. The protocol should reach an actual allocation in the first session and keep ongoing effort low.
5. **Make the algorithm's output a proposal that the couple can adjust.** Provably fair outputs were judged less than fair by about a third of participants [17], explanation alone did not help, and outcome control did [18]. Kajibuntan's manual swap feature follows the same logic [19].
6. **Preference elicitation is a known failure point.** Quantifying preferences through an interface "was prone to error" [17]. Keep the elicitation simple and let partners check that the numbers reflect what they meant.
7. **Do not assume a particular fairness criterion.** Spliddit uses equitability for chores [15]; Kajibuntan aims at envy-freeness [19]. No test of either against couples' own fairness judgements for chores was found. Which criterion best predicts perceived fairness is an empirical question for this project.
8. **Satisfaction can move without large changes in share.** One education programme found small changes in involvement and large changes in wives' satisfaction [5]. That supports the project's choice of perceived fairness over 50/50, with the caveat that it is a single 1994 study whose design is unverified here.
9. **Information and attitude change are not enough.** A planning tool produced no significant change [6] and a brief attitude intervention changed attitudes but not behaviour [7]. The method should produce a concrete allocation and a review point, not just awareness.
10. **A neutral procedure can legitimise an unequal status quo.** Couples already use "our process is neutral" to explain away unequal outcomes [37]. The protocol should show partners the resulting distribution of both planning and execution so that perceived fairness is judged with the outcome in view.
11. **Gender neutrality is untested ground.** The evaluated couple interventions found here were framed around mothers and fathers or sampled almost entirely from different-sex couples. None reports results for same-sex couples, and no evaluation was found of a mechanism that makes no reference to gender.

## Open questions and gaps

- Does any structured allocation procedure raise *both* partners' perceived fairness relative to no procedure? No study found.
- Does the Fair Play course outperform its waitlist? The data to answer this appear to exist for one cohort but the comparison is not reported [3].
- Do chore apps change anything? No evaluation found. Do points and leaderboards help or harm perceived fairness between two adults? Unknown.
- Which fair-division criterion (equitability, envy-freeness, something else) matches couples' own fairness judgements for chores? Unknown; the perception studies found covered rent [14a], goods [18] and an unverified set of tasks [17].
- How durable is any change? No in-scope housework programme found here reports follow-up beyond 6 months [5][6][7]; longer follow-up appears only in the out-of-scope trials [23].
- Do effects hold when the less-engaged partner did not choose to take part? Journalism suggests this is the main hurdle [31]; no study found.
- How should cognitive labour be elicited and weighed? The only quantitative measure found is unvalidated [2].

### Search limitations

- The Consensus search tool returned no results in this session (rate limit, then the monthly quota was exhausted), so the planned semantic literature search did not happen.
- PubMed covers biomedical journals only. Sociology, family studies, economics and human-computer interaction sources were found through web search and then checked against Crossref and OpenAlex records.
- Only [2], [3], [14] and [15] were read in full. Every other scholarly source was read as an abstract or database record only (publisher blocks prevented more), and the product and news pages were read through an automated page summary. Where an abstract is silent on division of labour, this file says so and does not infer a null effect.
- Every "none found" above means none found in these searches, not that none exists.
- Not covered: couple therapy models, books other than Fair Play, and non-English app markets beyond Kajibuntan.
