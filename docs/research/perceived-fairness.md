# What makes a division of chores feel fair

Research ticket: issue #3. Slug: `perceived-fairness`. Searched 2026-11-16.

## Short answer

A partner's sense that the division of household labour is fair is only loosely tied to the split itself. Doing a larger share does raise the odds of saying "I do more than my fair share" (Jansen et al. 2016), but majorities in several national samples call clearly unequal splits fair (Carriero 2011; Baxter 2000; Braun et al. 2008; Perales et al. 2015). The literature explains the gap with the distributive justice framework (Thompson 1991): a person feels unfairly treated only when they lack an outcome they value, hold a comparison standard that says they should have it, and see no acceptable justification for going without.

The predictors of perceived fairness with support are listed below. The order is not a ranking by weight of evidence: the split itself (item 4) has by far the largest evidence base, while item 1 rests mainly on one cross-sectional sample of 234 wives.

1. **Feeling appreciated by the partner, and deciding the division together.** These were the strongest direct predictors in the first empirical test of the framework (Hawkins et al. 1995), Later work shows appreciation moderating related links rather than replicating that result: it conditions how ideology relates to perceived unfairness (Lachance-Grzela 2012) and buffers the link between an unequal or unfair split and satisfaction (Gordon et al. 2022).
2. **Comparison referents.** Who a person compares themselves or their partner with shifts the fairness judgement, in surveys, in a vignette experiment and in qualitative work (Gager 1998; Carriero & Todesco 2016; Nakamura & Akiyoshi 2015; Esmail 2010). One longitudinal study did not support this (Grote et al. 2002).
3. **Justifications that offset housework against other contributions.** Paid work hours and earnings are accepted as reasons to do less housework, and fairness is judged against total workload (Koster et al. 2021; Urbina & Tisch 2025).
4. **The actual split, including who does which tasks.** It matters, but less than the above would suggest, and task allocation can matter more than hours (Baxter 2000; Jansen et al. 2016).

Each partner tends to over-count their own contribution. The effect is replicated in group settings (Kruger & Savitsky 2009; Caruso et al. 2006; Schroeder et al. 2016; the 1979 paper usually credited with it, Ross & Sicoly, could not be checked at source) and shows up in couples as reporting gaps and over-reported hours (Deutsch et al. 1993; Press & Townsley 1998; Yavorsky et al. 2015; Naujoks 2024).

On relationship satisfaction, perceived fairness is the more consistent predictor: in most studies that include both, the perception carries the association and the measured split adds little (Wilkie et al. 1998; Hiekel & Ivanova 2022; Smith et al. 2026), and the same holds for conflict over housework (Trübner 2022). Three cautions apply. Nearly all of this is correlational and same-respondent. One longitudinal study finds that marital distress predicts later perceived unfairness as well as the reverse (Grote & Clark 2001). And two recent studies find that equal sharing itself predicts the highest satisfaction (Carlson 2022; Petts et al. 2025). No meta-analysis of perceived housework fairness and relationship satisfaction was found.

Perceived fairness is almost always measured with a single self-report item. See "How perceived fairness has been measured" below.

## Findings by theme

### 1. The split alone is a weak predictor of perceived fairness

**Strength of evidence: strong (many large cross-national surveys, consistent direction).**

- In 29 countries (ISSP 2012, N = 16,633) people who do a larger share of household tasks are more likely to say they do more than a fair share (Jansen et al. 2016). So the split does matter.
- Yet unequal splits are routinely judged fair. In a national Australian survey 59% of women and 68% of men called the division fair although women reported doing the bulk of the work (Baxter 2000). Among 404 dual-earner couples in one Italian city, 55% of both wives and husbands reported fairness and satisfaction while wives did about two-thirds of the chores; 13.6% of wives and 5.7% of husbands reported both unfairness and dissatisfaction (Carriero 2011). Australian panel data give the same picture with women doing about 70% (Perales et al. 2015). Dutch data extend it to childcare, where unequal splits are even more often seen as fair (Koster et al. 2021).
- In the Australian study the division of tasks between partners predicted fairness more strongly than time spent, and gender attitudes, paid work time and financial power had little effect (Baxter 2000).
- Country context moves the threshold: an unequal split is more likely to be called unfair in more gender-egalitarian countries (Jansen et al. 2016; Greenstein 2009), and country-level wage ratios and average inequality also predict perceived equity (Braun et al. 2008).
- Both spouses' answers agree poorly. In US national data (NSFH wave 1) agreement between wives and husbands on fairness was low, and the data fitted a two-dimensional model in which one dimension was relative fairness and the other a general tendency to answer "fair to both" whatever the balance (Smith et al. 1998).

### 2. Equity theory: under-benefit hurts; evidence on over-benefit is mixed

**Strength of evidence: moderate (large samples, mostly cross-sectional).**

Equity theory predicts distress whenever contributions and benefits are out of proportion, in either direction.

- German panel data (FReDA wave 1, 2021, N = 11,012 respondents cohabiting with a different-sex partner): perceived unfairness goes with lower relationship satisfaction both for those who feel they contribute more and those who feel they contribute less than their fair share. The authors read this as support for the distributive justice model over a self-interest model. Which direction hurt more differed by gender: lower satisfaction was tied more strongly to perceived under-contribution among women and to perceived over-contribution among men (Adriaans et al. 2026).
- A US online convenience sample (N = 10,236): feeling the housework split is unfair to oneself is a clearly stronger predictor of lower relationship satisfaction than feeling it is unfair to the partner (Gillespie et al. 2019). As read from the full text, the adjusted estimate was about -0.78 on a 1 to 7 scale for unfair-to-self and about zero (0.02, not significant) for unfair-to-partner. That is a null result for over-benefit and conflicts with Adriaans et al. (2026).
- Three samples during the COVID-19 pandemic (N = 2,193, including 476 couples): doing more and perceiving unfairness predicted lower satisfaction, and there were also costs of doing less (Gordon et al. 2022).
- An Australian sample of 373 husbands and wives: marital satisfaction was predicted both by relative benefit and by how close the exchange was to strict equity, with the balance between the two varying by life stage (Feeney et al. 1994).
- US panel data (1,500 couples): the only subjective inequity measure associated with later divorce was women's sense of being under-benefited (DeMaris 2007). A US and West German comparison using hours of paid and unpaid work found departures from equity most destabilising in the US when the wife under-benefits; in West Germany equity was less salient (Bellani et al. 2017).

**Disagreement:** whether over-benefit carries a real cost is unsettled. Adriaans et al. (2026) and Gordon et al. (2022) find one, and the strict-equity result in Feeney et al. (1994) is consistent with one; Gillespie et al. (2019) do not.

### 3. The distributive justice framework: outcomes, referents, justifications

**Strength of evidence: moderate for the framework as a whole. Individual components vary, and most tests sampled wives only.**

The framework (Thompson 1991) is a theoretical review. It argues that researchers should look at valued outcomes other than time and tasks, at comparison referents within and between genders, and at the justifications offered for a small contribution.

**Empirical tests**

- First direct test, 234 dual-earner wives in the western US, cross-sectional: qualified support. Feeling appreciated was the strongest predictor of fairness and deciding together how things would be divided was also strong. Other elements worked indirectly, through the division of work itself (Hawkins et al. 1995).
- Interviews with 25 dual-earner US couples: both spouses valued some aspects of household tasks, justifications helped maintain inequality, and comparisons with peers strongly shaped fairness judgements (Gager 1998).
- 404 dual-earner Italian couples: the framework's elements were operationalised and tested, and the author describes the result as a "critical reassessment" (Carriero 2011). The abstract does not say which elements held up.
- Australian panel data: after the first birth women's perceived fairness rose at first and fell in the long run, net of the actual split, time availability and relative resources. The authors attribute this to changing identity and entitlement (Perales et al. 2015).

**Valued outcomes**

- Appreciation is the most consistent valued outcome. Beyond (Hawkins et al. 1995), among 141 Canadian mothers an egalitarian ideology went with a stronger sense of unfairness only when they felt little appreciated by their partner (Lachance-Grzela 2012), and feeling appreciated removed the negative link between an unequal or unfair division and satisfaction in the pandemic samples (Gordon et al. 2022).
- Enjoyment and competence: in a three-wave study of couples expecting a first child, wives saw a lopsided division as fairer when they enjoyed family work and when both spouses believed them especially competent at it (Grote et al. 2002).

**Comparison referents**

- Italian probability sample with a vignette experiment: referent effects were strong but limited to the domestic behaviour of male referents, and unfavourable comparisons moved fairness more than favourable ones. The vignette design showed the effect holds when self-serving choice of referent is controlled (Carriero & Todesco 2016).
- Japanese married women (2014): social comparison was the key mechanism, and reference groups were people in similar life circumstances rather than others in general (Nakamura & Akiyoshi 2015).
- 30 countries (ISSP 2012): comparison with one's partner and comparison with other women in the same society each affect women's family life satisfaction independently (Hu & Yucel 2017).
- 22 dual-earner lesbian couples (qualitative): partners used comparisons with heterosexual families they knew and with their own former relationships to justify what they considered fair (Esmail 2010).
- **Null finding:** in the three-wave study above, the prediction that women see more fairness when they compare with other women rather than with their spouse was not supported. Prospective analyses suggested the reverse direction: perceiving inequity led people to make more comparisons with the spouse (Grote et al. 2002).
- Spanish dual-earner couples: only women used social comparisons linked to how housework was divided (Martínez et al. 2010).

**Justifications**

- US vignette experiment, nationally representative, N = 1,502: respondents made trade-off calculations. A spouse's higher relative earnings and longer work time were treated as contributions that justify doing less housework, for men and for women alike. Contrary to the authors' expectation, arrangements where the woman did more were rated less fair than those where the man did more (Urbina & Tisch 2025).
- Dutch data: fairness of the household division is judged in relation to total workload, not housework in isolation (Koster et al. 2021).
- Cross-national survey data (ISSP 2002, N = 8,556): time availability, resource dependence and gender ideology shape perceived equity through their interaction with the actual split (Braun et al. 2008).
- Spanish couples: both men and women used more justifications when the split was unequal, and justifications were tied to traditional ideology (Martínez et al. 2010).
- Chinese and South Korean families (qualitative, 24 families): zero participation was always unfair, but unequal splits were justified by time availability and tiredness, applied in gendered ways and without specifying how much each should do. Many participants thought "fairness" should not apply to family at all and preferred "understanding and agreement" (Midgette 2020).

**Procedure**

- Deciding together was a strong predictor in Hawkins et al. (1995).
- Dutch daily diary study (157 parents, 1,235 daily conversations): on days with more conversation about household tasks, people reported higher perceived fairness of, and satisfaction with, the task division, and higher relationship quality. Daily fairness and satisfaction, but not how egalitarian the day's split was, were linked to relationship quality. Effects barely carried over to the next day (Riedijk et al. 2023).
- Japan: joint decision-making predicted marital quality for both spouses (Taniguchi & Kaufman 2020).

### 4. Egocentric over-claiming of one's own contribution

**Strength of evidence: strong that the bias exists in general. Moderate for couples and housework specifically. Thin on whether correcting it helps couples.**

**General mechanism**

- The original 1979 paper reported egocentric biases in recall and in allocation of responsibility across several experiments, including one with married couples (Ross & Sicoly 1979). *Its abstract could not be retrieved from a primary source in this session; this description comes from a search summary and is unverified at source.*
- People focus on their own contributions and give less weight to collaborators'. This inflates relative claims when one's own absolute contributions are many and deflates them when they are few, so over-claiming is "not as ubiquitous as once thought" (Kruger & Savitsky 2009) (abstract from the preprint version).
- Over-claiming grows with group size, in two field studies and two experiments, and shrinks when people explicitly consider others' contributions first (Schroeder et al. 2016). (Inference, not a finding: a two-person household is the smallest group, so by this account the bias should be smaller there than in larger groups.)
- **A cost of debiasing:** across experiments, having people consider others' contributions reduced egocentric claims but left high contributors less satisfied and less interested in future collaboration, especially in cooperative groups. "Some members who look beyond their own perspective may not like what they see" (Caruso et al. 2006).

**In couples**

- 268 married US couples estimating each spouse's percentage contribution to 32 child-care tasks: a consistent credit-taking bias, with each spouse giving themselves more credit than their partner gave them. **Null finding:** the size of the bias was not correlated with marital adjustment (Deutsch et al. 1993).
- Comparison of US survey reports with time-diary data: both husbands and wives over-report their housework hours, and the size of the over-report varies with gender attitudes, class, education and family size (Press & Townsley 1998).
- Dual-earner US couples followed across the transition to parenthood with both diaries and surveys: the birth magnified over-estimation in survey reports, enough that survey data alone would have hidden the gender gap the diaries showed (Yavorsky et al. 2015).
- 500 US families, survey estimates against experience sampling: hours estimates differ substantially by method; the husband's share differs somewhat less (Lee & Waite 2005). Shares are more robust than hours.
- German couples (pairfam, n = 414): partners' reports of the division disagreed in 45% of couples for housework and 38% for childcare (Naujoks 2024).
- Swedish couples (N = 1,057, linked to register data): when a man reports that his partner does less housework than she reports, her relationship satisfaction is lower and the union is more likely to dissolve (Ruppanner et al. 2017a). Here the disagreement itself, not the bias, is what tracks the outcome.
- 235 heterosexual cohabiting romantic partners, dyadic response surface analysis across physical, cognitive and emotional load: larger discrepancies between partners' perceptions of how equally the work is shared went with poorer relationship quality. Discrepancies in equity perceptions did not (Coundouris & Henry 2026).
- Small US study (33 couples): agreement between partners about who does what went with relational certainty, and only marginally with satisfaction (Le & Aune 2011).

### 5. Perceived fairness versus the actual split as a predictor of relationship satisfaction

**Strength of evidence: moderate that perceived fairness is the stronger correlate. Weak on causal direction. No meta-analysis found.**

**Evidence that the perception carries the association**

- 382 two-earner US couples, path analysis: the division of labour and role preferences affected marital satisfaction mainly through perceived fairness, and what counted as fair differed between husbands and wives (Wilkie et al. 1998).
- German panel (pairfam), across the transition to parenthood: differences in the change in relationship satisfaction followed perceived fairness, not the actual division. This held for women, not men (Hiekel & Ivanova 2022). Per the institute's press release, women who saw their unfair load as stable lost almost a full point on a 0 to 10 scale, and several subgroup contrasts were not significant.
- 1,597 German couples with children, actor-partner models: "it is not the division of labor that is decisive for conflicts in relationships, but what is perceived to be fair" (Trübner 2022).
- US couples including same-sex couples (N = 3,455, with time diaries): satisfaction with the housework division predicted relationship and sexual satisfaction in every couple type, and results were stronger for the subjective report than for diary time (Smith et al. 2026).
- 29 countries: perceived fairness mediates the link between the division and how often couples disagree about housework (Tai & Baxter 2018).
- US national sample of dual-earner marriages: perceived housework inequity goes with lower marital happiness for both spouses and with higher odds of divorce among wives only (Frisco & Williams 2003).
- A review of over 200 studies from 1989 to 1999 concluded that more balanced divisions go with women perceiving fairness, less depression and higher marital satisfaction (Coltrane 2000). This is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis.

**Counter-evidence and nulls**

- **Reverse causation.** Three-wave US study across the transition to parenthood: marital conflict and wives' dissatisfaction at time 1 predicted perceived unfairness at time 2, controlling for earlier unfairness. Perceived unfairness at time 2 also predicted later conflict and dissatisfaction for wives (Grote & Clark 2001). The relation runs both ways, so perceived unfairness is partly a symptom of distress.
- **Null under fixed effects.** 1,315 German couples: within-couple changes in the division or in perceived fairness had no effect on sexual satisfaction or frequency. The author argues earlier between-person findings may reflect unobserved confounders (Hajek 2019). This concerns sexual outcomes only, not overall relationship satisfaction.
- **Null actor effects for men.** 389 dual-earner couples in Austria, Germany and Switzerland: perceived justice mattered for wives' own satisfaction but not husbands'. Wives' perceived justice did affect husbands' satisfaction through conflict (Mikula et al. 2011). Japan: perceived fairness predicted marital quality for wives only (Taniguchi & Kaufman 2020).
- **The split itself can matter.** In two US datasets the number of tasks shared equally predicted relationship quality for both men and women, while the man's overall proportion of housework related positively to women's and negatively to men's (Carlson 2022). Among US parents, equal division of cognitive housework went with the highest relationship satisfaction for both mothers and fathers (Petts et al. 2025).
- **Typology view.** Swedish couples sort into six classes on split and fairness; the modal class (33%) shares equally and both call it fair. The authors suggest allocation, conflict and fairness mark different types of couple rather than a single chain of association (Ruppanner et al. 2017b).

**Moderators**

- Appreciation buffers the cost of an unequal or unfair split, for whoever does more (Gordon et al. 2022).
- The fairness and satisfaction link was present in same-sex couples but weaker than in different-sex couples (Smith et al. 2026). This is the only quantitative same-sex evidence found and it used satisfaction with the division, not a fairness item.
- The link is stronger in more gender-egalitarian countries (Greenstein 2009; Hu & Yucel 2017), and depends on gender-role attitudes (Hiekel & Ivanova 2022).

**Shared-method caveat.** In nearly every study the fairness rating and the satisfaction rating come from the same person at the same time, which inflates their correlation compared with a diary-measured split. "Perception beats the split" is partly a measurement artefact of unknown size. The partner effects in (Mikula et al. 2011) and the diary comparison in (Smith et al. 2026) are the designs least exposed to this.

### 6. Cognitive labour

**Strength of evidence: thin for fairness specifically.**

- Cognitive labour (anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, monitoring) is often invisible to both the person doing it and their partner, and is a frequent source of conflict (70 interviews, 35 US couples) (Daminger 2019).
- A systematic review of 31 studies finds women do more mental labour and report more negative consequences, including lower relationship satisfaction (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023).
- Equal sharing of cognitive housework goes with the highest relationship satisfaction among US parents (Petts et al. 2025), and discrepancies between partners' perceptions exist for cognitive load as for physical load (Coundouris & Henry 2026).
- No study was found that measures perceived fairness of cognitive labour separately and tests what predicts it. Invisibility plausibly worsens egocentric under-counting of the partner's contribution, but that is inference from Kruger & Savitsky (2009) and Daminger (2019), not a tested result.

## How perceived fairness has been measured

| Approach | Example wording or design | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Single item, bipolar, who it is unfair to | "How do you feel about the fairness of household chores in your relationship?" with 5 options from "very unfair to you" through "fair to both" to "very unfair to (spouse)". Parallel items for paid work, spending money and child care. | US NSFH codebook [M1]; analysed in (Smith et al. 1998; DeMaris 2007) |
| Single item, bipolar, own fair share | 5 options from "I do much more than my fair share" through "I do just about my fair share" to "I do much less than my fair share", plus "doesn't apply". | German pairfam [M2]; a "fair share" item is also used in the ISSP (Jansen et al. 2016) and FReDA (Adriaans et al. 2026) (exact wording not retrieved) |
| Single item with an explicit reference to constraints | "Considering work/commute schedules, would you say the division of your household/childcare chores is fair?" Options: I do way too much / somewhat more than I should; fair enough; partner does way too much / somewhat more. | (Gillespie et al. 2019) (read from full text) |
| Satisfaction with the division, as a proxy or alongside fairness | Rating of satisfaction with the housework division | (Carriero 2011; Smith et al. 2026; Riedijk et al. 2023) |
| Multi-component operationalisation of the framework | Separate measures of valued outcomes, referents and justifications | (Hawkins et al. 1995; Carriero 2011) |
| Vignette or factorial survey | Respondents rate the fairness of hypothetical couples' arrangements with attributes varied at random | (Carriero & Todesco 2016; Urbina & Tisch 2025) |
| Both partners' reports, analysed as a couple | Each partner answers the same item separately; latent class or dyadic models | (Naujoks 2024; Smith et al. 1998; Mikula et al. 2011; Coundouris & Henry 2026; Ruppanner et al. 2017b) |
| Separate equity and equality perceptions per load | Physical, cognitive and emotional load rated separately | (Coundouris & Henry 2026) |
| Distributive versus procedural justice | Both rated for the division of domestic labour | (Mikula et al. 2011) |
| Daily diary | Fairness of the day's task division rated each day | (Riedijk et al. 2023) |

Known problems with the standard single item:

- "Fair to both" attracts answers regardless of the real balance, and spouses agree poorly (Smith et al. 1998).
- Surveys rarely define "fairness", and respondents' reasons go unrecorded (Midgette 2020).
- Bipolar items merge two things: whether the arrangement is unfair, and in whose favour. Studies that split them find the two directions behave differently (Adriaans et al. 2026; Gillespie et al. 2019).
- Combining housework and childcare in one item hides differences between them (Koster et al. 2021; Gillespie et al. 2019).
- No validated multi-item scale in wide use was verified in this session. One questionnaire aimed at dual-earner wives' sense of fairness was located by title only (Hawkins et al. 1998).

## Implications for the allocation method

1. **Target each partner's perceived fairness directly, and measure it separately for each.** The split is a weak proxy (Carriero 2011; Baxter 2000; Smith et al. 1998), so a method that only equalises hours or task counts can miss its own objective.
2. **Ask about direction, not just fairness.** Use an item that distinguishes "I do more than my fair share" from "I do less", per partner. The two directions carry different costs (Adriaans et al. 2026; Gillespie et al. 2019), and a couple-level "both say fair" answer is the cleanest success signal available (Ruppanner et al. 2017b).
3. **Do not treat "fair to both" at face value.** It is partly a response habit (Smith et al. 1998). Pair it with a second signal, such as satisfaction with the division or frequency of disagreement (Tai & Baxter 2018).
4. **Let the couple set what counts as a contribution.** Paid work hours, commute and total workload are widely accepted offsets (Koster et al. 2021; Urbina & Tisch 2025). The method should let partners declare such offsets explicitly rather than assuming housework is judged in isolation. Because these justifications are also how lopsided splits get rationalised (Gager 1998; Martínez et al. 2010; Midgette 2020), they should be visible and agreed by both, not implicit.
5. **Build the procedure in.** Deciding together and talking about tasks predict perceived fairness independently of the split (Hawkins et al. 1995; Riedijk et al. 2023). A mechanism in which both partners take part in the allocation should outperform one that hands down a computed answer. The diary result suggests the benefit decays within a day or so (Riedijk et al. 2023), which favours a recurring light check-in over a one-off negotiation.
6. **Make contributions visible to counter over-claiming, with care.** Each partner over-counts their own work and partner reports disagree in roughly four in ten couples (Deutsch et al. 1993; Naujoks 2024). A shared record addresses the availability mechanism (Kruger & Savitsky 2009; Schroeder et al. 2016). But debiasing made high contributors less satisfied in group experiments (Caruso et al. 2006), and discrediting a partner's reported contribution tracks worse outcomes (Ruppanner et al. 2017a). A shared record should therefore come with acknowledgement of what it reveals, not just a corrected ledger.
7. **Prefer shares to hours when eliciting estimates.** Reported hours are inflated and method-dependent; shares are somewhat more stable (Lee & Waite 2005).
8. **Treat appreciation as part of the outcome, not decoration.** It is the strongest single predictor of perceived fairness in the first framework test and it buffers the cost of an unequal split (Hawkins et al. 1995; Lachance-Grzela 2012; Gordon et al. 2022). Since no money changes hands, acknowledgement is the main non-task currency the method has.
9. **Track task allocation, not only totals.** Who does which tasks predicted fairness more than time did (Baxter 2000), and the number of equally shared tasks predicted relationship quality for both partners (Carlson 2022).
10. **Include cognitive labour as its own line.** It is invisible by default (Daminger 2019), which makes it the component most exposed to under-counting by the partner.
11. **Expect fairness ratings to drift with relationship mood.** Distress raises perceived unfairness (Grote & Clark 2001). A fall in a couple's fairness rating is not on its own evidence that the allocation got worse.
12. **Gender-neutrality check.** The predictors above (appreciation, referents, justifications, procedure, visibility) are not inherently gendered and the fairness and satisfaction link appears in same-sex couples (Smith et al. 2026). Most effect-size differences by gender are best read as failure modes: one partner's fairness rating mattering less to their own satisfaction (Mikula et al. 2011; Taniguchi & Kaufman 2020) does not make it safe to ignore, since it affects the other partner through conflict (Mikula et al. 2011).

## Open questions and gaps

- **No meta-analysis** of perceived housework fairness and relationship satisfaction was found. One 2025 systematic review of equality in housework and childcare among dual-career couples exists (7 studies) but it has no DOI and was not used.
- **Causal direction** is largely untested. Only Grote & Clark (2001) and Hajek (2019) use designs that address it, and they point to bidirectionality and to possible confounding.
- **Does over-benefit hurt?** Adriaans et al. (2026) and Gillespie et al. (2019) disagree.
- **Does correcting over-claiming help or harm couples?** The debiasing evidence is from work groups and student samples (Caruso et al. 2006; Schroeder et al. 2016), not from partners sharing a household. The one couple study found no link between credit-taking bias and marital adjustment (Deutsch et al. 1993).
- **Comparison referents** have one clear longitudinal null (Grote et al. 2002) against several cross-sectional positives (Carriero & Todesco 2016; Nakamura & Akiyoshi 2015).
- **Cognitive labour:** no study of what predicts its perceived fairness.
- **Same-sex couples:** one quantitative study (Smith et al. 2026) and one small qualitative study (Esmail 2010).
- **Interventions:** no trial was found that changes an allocation procedure and measures perceived fairness afterwards. The closest evidence is observational daily diary data (Riedijk et al. 2023).
- **Measurement:** no widely used validated multi-item fairness scale was verified. Exact ISSP and FReDA item wording was not retrieved.
- **Which elements of the framework failed** in the Italian reassessment (Carriero 2011) is not stated in its abstract and needs a full-text read.
- **Foundational paper:** the content of (Ross & Sicoly 1979) needs checking at source.
