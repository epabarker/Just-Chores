## Synthesis

## What makes a partner see the allocation of chores as fair: synthesis, pass 4

**Basis and limits.** Thirty-five papers were read in full: five narrative reviews (Mikula 1998, Shelton & John 1996, Coltrane 2000, Thompson 1991, Major 1993) and thirty single studies. No meta-analysis of perceived fairness and relationship satisfaction was found in the searches. The reviews report most findings secondhand, and each secondary extract says so. Seventeen of the papers are scans or have a scanned text layer; a quote from one carries a conclusion only where a second machine reading of the page agrees, and the quotes where the readings differ sit under Leads. Nearly all the evidence is cross-sectional and self-reported. A partner's report of the split and that partner's perceived fairness often come from the same respondent. Gender findings below are evidence about failure modes the method must design against; the method takes no gender input.

### Conclusions

**C1: the actual split explains only a small part of a partner's perceived fairness (decisive). Supported in direction; the best figures are small, but the one large figure comes from a measure that overlaps the split.** Mikula's review concludes that the actual division of household labour is not the main determinant of perceived justice [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E1] and that variance explained by quantitative workload measures is relatively small [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E2]. The reviews' own figures are secondhand and sit under Leads, so the numbers come from the single studies. In Sánchez's NSFH analysis (single study, matched couples), both partners' hours together give an adjusted R² of .03 in each partner's equation [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E1], and .04 with every control [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E2]. Greenstein's whole fairness model, with 13 predictors including the share, has an adjusted R² of .044 for wives (single study) [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E1]. Koster's models of the three labour shares plus controls give R² of .047 for men and .134 for women on housework unfairness (single study) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1]. Blair and Johnson's whole models explain about 15% of wives' variance (single study) [10.2307_353243#E1], and Wilkie's explain .19 and .11 for housework fairness (wives, husbands; single study) [10.2307_353530#E1]. In Mikula's 2009 dyadic model the division and hours controls explained 8% to 10% of women's justice variance beside 21% to 33% for the entitlement predictors (single study) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E1]. Each of these is a whole-model figure, so it is an upper bound on the split's own share. Two things work against "small". Mederer's full model with time, task and management allocation explains 54% of the variance in wives' fairness [10.2307_352964#E2]. It comes from one sample of employed wives, with the split and the fairness rating reported by the same woman. Mikula 2009 found an unadjusted correlation of -.47 for women's child-care share (about 22%) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E3]. The split is also a steep predictor per unit even when its explained share is small [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2]. Braun's odds ratios and z statistics show it as the strongest individual-level predictor but give no variance explained [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2008.00556.x#E2]. For design: the split is worth measuring, but a method that equalises counts or hours alone should expect to leave most of a partner's perceived fairness unexplained.

**C2: comparison referents predict perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for (decisive). Weakly and inconsistently supported, by two single studies and only for some referents.** Mikula's 2009 dyadic model controls the division and hours. The outcome of the man-versus-other-men comparison predicts justice (beta .181), and the woman-versus-other-women outcome does not (beta -.041) (single study) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E4]. The frequency of both referential comparisons was also not significant [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E5]. Carriero's survey (single study, Italy) controls the respondent's reported share. For husbands comparing themselves with a male friend, the predicted probability of rating the split fair falls from 0.56 (same amount of housework) to 0.40 (he thinks he does less) [10.5153_sro.3871#E7]. Wives comparing their husband with their father show a fall from 0.63 to 0.45 [10.5153_sro.3871#E8]. Referent effects are null for comparisons involving women [10.5153_sro.3871#E6], and the authors suspect self-serving choice of referents inflates the survey associations [10.5153_sro.3871#E9]. Against this, Hawkins's wives model finds the between-gender comparison's direct effect gone once the wife's perceived division is added, with the referent working only through the split [10.1177_019251395016006002#E8] (single study; the division there is a perception, not the actual split). Grote and colleagues' panel finds that comparisons did not predict later fairness and that the dominant direction was inequity driving comparisons [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E9], and same-sex referents went the opposite way from the hypothesis [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E5]. Mikula's review notes the causal direction between comparison and perceived injustice is undetermined [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E7]. Two narrative reviews present the referent account as the author's own proposal and not as a tested adjusted association [10.1111_j.1540-4560.1993.tb01173.x#E3] [10.1177_019251391012002003#E4]. Several studies stand in a proxy for the referent (gender ideology, a country mean of inequality) [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E3] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2008.00556.x#E4], and Hu's referential index is built from the split it adjusts for [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E3]. For design: ask each partner whom they compare with, but do not expect the comparison to add much once the allocation is known, and do not assume the effect is the same for both partners.

**C3: the justifications a partner accepts for an unequal split predict that partner's perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for (decisive). No direct test; only proxies.** No study read measures the justifications a partner accepts and enters them with the split. Perales states that psychological mechanisms can be tested only by an indirect residual approach [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E9], and Greenstein says justifications are discussed as theory and not measured [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E6]. Major says no study has examined whether a voice justification affects satisfaction with an unequal split in intimate relationships [10.1111_j.1540-4560.1993.tb01173.x#E4]. The proxies are objective compensating inputs and ideology. In Mikula's 2009 model the woman's weekly professional hours predict both partners' justice ratings net of the division (beta -.087) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E7], and only the woman's hours and status do so, not the man's [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E8]. DeMaris & Longmore find that the husband's relative contributions to sporadic chores, child care, health and paid hours predict lower perceived unfairness net of the routine split [10.2307_2580392#E7], and that endorsement of housework sharing does too [10.2307_2580392#E9]. These are objective inputs and attitudes, not reasons a partner reports accepting. Braun's gender ideology interacts strongly with the split, but its main effect is non-significant net of the split [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2008.00556.x#E7] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2008.00556.x#E8]. Lennon & Rosenfield's ideology proxy shows no association net of the split [10.1086_230545#E7]. Urbina's randomised vignettes show third-party raters judging a focal person with more income or more work hours as more overburdened, so inputs shift judgments of others' splits [10.1177_08912432251337416#E6] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E7]. The authors say it does not show that a partner's accepted justifications predict that partner's own fairness [10.1177_08912432251337416#E9]. Gager's interviews (qualitative) report that the most overburdened wives could not justify their outcomes and called the split unfair [10.1177_019251398019005007#E4]. Ruppanner's classes show a reduced employment of the woman does not act as a justification [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E5]. In Hawkins's combined model the Standards construct (the wife's higher standards as a justification) had no significant direct effect, and Deciding Together was removed for multicollinearity [10.1177_019251395016006002#E13]. For design: a method should record the reasons each partner gives for a difference in chores, because the literature has not tested whether they matter and cannot be cited against doing so.

**C4: a partner's perceived fairness is more strongly associated with that partner's relationship satisfaction than the actual split is (decisive). Supported for women in the studies that compare them; not for men, and with exceptions.** Coltrane's review says that for men, split and fairness are typically unrelated to well-being or marital satisfaction [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E10]; the reviews' secondhand reports that fairness is the stronger correlate, more so for women, are under Leads. Among single studies, Wilkie's path models say the split's effect on satisfaction runs only through perceptions and is small [10.2307_353530#E10]. Hu's models (single study, women, family-life satisfaction) show the actual share no longer predicting satisfaction once both fairness measures are in, against 0.092 and 0.261 for the fairness measures [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6]. Lavee & Katz's pooled models show fairness the stronger correlate of marital quality for both sexes [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9] (values read from a figure; check by eye), but by group the fairness path is .76 for Christian Arab women and .03 and .38 (not significant) for Jewish and Moslem women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E7]. Greenstein's wives have fairness coefficients of 1.463 and 0.941 on happiness and stability against a non-significant split, but the two are on different scales and not formally compared [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E8]. Adriaans finds the split index significant for women's satisfaction and not for men's, with fairness categories significant for men [10.20377_jfr-1377#E8]. Coundouris found the discrepancy in reported split related to poorer relationship quality while the discrepancy in fairness was not [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8]. For design: measure perceived fairness in addition to the split, and do not treat satisfaction as following from the split for either partner.

**C5: the association between the actual split and relationship satisfaction is mediated by perceived fairness (supporting). Suggestive for women; not shown for men; no formal test.** Mikula's review says whether perceived injustice mediates between the division and marital quality has had little systematic study [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E15]. Lavee & Katz find that for women pooled across groups the direct path becomes non-significant once fairness is added, read as full mediation [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E11], and that for men fairness did not mediate, because the split did not move fairness [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E12]. Hu reports a 31% reduction in the share coefficient when relational fairness is added, read as partial mediation [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E8], and says the data are cross-sectional and support association only [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E10]. Wilkie's models say the split's effects are largely explained by perceived unfairness and empathy [10.2307_353530#E11], with the causal order admitted to be partly speculative [10.2307_353530#E13]. Greenstein tests moderation by ideology, not mediation [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E10]. Gillespie notes that relationship quality might drive perceived fairness [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E9]. For design: do not build a method on the assumption that equalising the split improves satisfaction by way of perceived fairness.

**C6: each partner over-claims their own contribution, so the two partners' self-reported shares of chores sum to more than the whole (decisive). Supported on average in four samples, but not for wives in the one paired test of hours, and nothing is tested for chore shares at couple level.** Kamo's paired analysis (single study) finds husbands' own reports of their weekly housework exceed their wives' reports of it by 2.67 hours [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E1] and finds no gap for wives' contribution [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E2]. The author concludes that over-claiming holds for husbands and not wives [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E4]. Against that, Wilkie finds the mean wife-minus-husband discrepancy in the wife's percentage of domestic hours is 4.5 points, so the spouses' own shares sum to about 104.5% if each respondent's shares total 100 [10.2307_353530#E16], and the authors state that both spouses report more contribution by themselves than the partner attributes to them [10.2307_353530#E15]. Coundouris asked each person to divide 100% of the load and found women's own shares of 59.5% and men's 44.4% on average, a sum of about 104% (group means, no per-dyad test) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1], with the largest excess, 107%, for the cognitive load in that sample [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2]. Ross and Sicoly's couples over-estimated on 16 of 20 activities and none were significantly under-estimated, with a small magnitude [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E5] [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E6]. A time-diary comparison points the same way: both sexes report more weekly hours in a direct-question survey than in a diary survey, wives by 13.4 hours and husbands by 3.5, in two different samples [10.1177_089124398012002005#E2]. Several studies cannot test it. Carriero's average shares sum to about one [10.5153_sro.3871#E2], Grote's ratings are relative scales that cannot be summed [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E13], and Hu and Blair use one respondent's report of both partners [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E12] [10.2307_353243#E10]. For design: both partners' reports of the same chores will not agree, and a method should expect their summed shares to exceed the whole; the one sample that split the load by type showed the largest excess for cognitive labour.

**C7: which chores a partner does predicts that partner's perceived fairness beyond the time the chores take (decisive). Indirect evidence only; no study tests it.** The evidence is about types of work and mostly about the husband's contribution. Blair and Johnson (single study) find that husbands' hours in female-typed tasks predict wives' fairness with the wife's total labour in the model [10.2307_353243#E12] [10.2307_353243#E13]. They note male tasks take little time compared with female tasks, so type and time are entangled [10.2307_353243#E14]. Mederer finds that allocation of task and of household management each add to fairness beyond the wife's share of time (single study) [10.2307_352964#E4]. DeMaris & Longmore find that for wives the husband's sporadic-hours ratio has a stronger effect than the routine-hours ratio [10.2307_2580392#E10]. Against this, Koster finds housework and child care divisions not differently related to unfairness [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E7]. Carlson's task-specific results are for relationship quality and not for fairness [10.1177_2378023118765867#E3]. Ciciolla finds responsibility for child adjustment and for finances, but not for routines, is associated with partner satisfaction, and time was not controlled [10.1007_s11199-018-1001-x#E2]. Many studies pool chores into one time measure [10.2307_353530#E18]. For design: chore type and who holds the management of a chore are worth recording as separate inputs to the burden model, but the literature does not show that type adds to time.

**C8: studies measure perceived fairness of the division of chores mainly with a single self-report item (decisive). Supported.** Mikula's review identifies the single NSFH item as the basis of the most common database of recent studies [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E22] and says one-item measures of questionable reliability are common [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E21]. Of the 25 studies whose own measure was quoted, 19 use one item: for example Perales [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E1], Frisco [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E5], Koster [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E9], Ruppanner [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E8] and Gillespie [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E1]. Five of them draw on the same NSFH item (Blair & Johnson, DeMaris & Longmore, Greenstein, Lavee & Katz, Gager), so they are not independent measures. The exceptions are multi-item or composite scales: Mederer's three items [10.2307_352964#E5], Grote & Clark's five and eight items [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E13], Kamo's four-item composite, which has a low alpha [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E7], and Grote's panel indexes at Times 1 and 2 [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E16]. Hawkins reports a two-item scale [10.1177_019251395016006002#E19], Wilkie a two-item dichotomous housework measure [10.2307_353530#E20]. The item asks about fairness to self or the other partner on a five-point scale; Sánchez scores it 1 to 5 with higher meaning more unfair to the woman [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E9]. Grote asked fairness per task but analysed an index per domain [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E15], and Coundouris is the one study read that rated perceived equity of the cognitive load, with a single item per load [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E6]. For design: a method should not take the single item as sufficient, and should collect a partner's judgement per chore, since no study read analyses fairness chore by chore.

**C9: a partner over-benefited by the allocation reports lower relationship satisfaction than a partner in an equitable allocation (supporting). Not supported for housework; the data barely cover the over-benefited.** Gillespie's two analyses (single study) find that housework "unfair to partner" against "mostly fair" is associated with relationship satisfaction at -0.00 with covariates [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E4] and 0.02 for men [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E5], and the authors state there is no association for housework [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E6]. Coltrane's review summarises that for men, the usual over-benefited partner, split and fairness are typically unrelated to marital satisfaction [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E23]. Wilkie finds overreward measures not significantly related to marital satisfaction in any model [10.2307_353530#E22]. In the other direction, Adriaans finds that respondents who do "a bit less" than their fair share report satisfaction 0.39 points lower, but the estimate for "much less" is not significant and men in that group show no lower satisfaction [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E2] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E4]. Lennon & Rosenfield's wives who see the split as unfair to their husband have more depressive symptoms, a U-shape in a fitted quadratic for a different outcome [10.1086_230545#E18]. Few partners fall in this group: 2.1% of women and 3.9% of men in Lavee & Katz [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E16], Hu codes over-benefit as the high end of fairness, so it is never contrasted with an equitable allocation [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E16], and Frisco cannot separate it from other answers [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E6]. For design: do not rely on over-benefit lowering a partner's satisfaction. Treat a partner's own report of an unfair split to them as the signal to design on.

### Disagreements

**How large the split's share is (C1).** Coltrane's review, reporting other studies, calls the husband's share of routine housework the single most important predictor of a wife's fairness evaluation [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E3], and Mederer's full model explains 54% [10.2307_352964#E2]. Mikula's review concludes the split is not the main determinant [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E1], and the whole models of Sánchez, Greenstein, Blair & Johnson and Koster explain 3% to 15% [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E2] [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E1] [10.2307_353243#E1] [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1]. The two readings differ in what is ranked and what is measured: Coltrane ranks predictors, Mikula and the single studies report variance explained, and Mederer's sample is wives who were full-time state employees, whose fairness scale and split both come from the wife. The sources do not conflict if "most important" means the largest single coefficient and "small" means a small share of the total variance.

**Whether comparison referents add to the split (C2).** Mikula 2009 and Carriero find referent effects net of a measure of the split [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E4] [10.5153_sro.3871#E7], and Hawkins finds the direct effect lost once the wife's perceived division enters [10.1177_019251395016006002#E8]. Grote's panel finds the direction runs from inequity to comparison [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E9]. They differ in the referent (male versus female referents), in the control (a reported share versus the wife's perception of the division) and in the design (cross-sectional versus prospective).

**Whether fairness outranks the split for satisfaction (C4, C5).** Wilkie, Hu and the pooled models of Lavee & Katz favour fairness [10.2307_353530#E10] [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9]. Mikula's review reports, from other studies, an exception in Perry-Jenkins and Folk's working-class wives [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E13], and Lavee & Katz's group-level paths show fairness outranking the split only for Christian Arab women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E7]. For men Coltrane's review reports fairness unrelated to satisfaction, and Shelton and John report one such study [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E10] [10.1146_annurev.soc.22.1.299#E7], while Adriaans finds fairness categories significant for men [10.20377_jfr-1377#E8]. The differences follow the sex of the partner, the country or group, and the outcome (family-life satisfaction, marital quality, marital happiness).

**Whether both partners over-claim (C6).** Kamo's paired data show over-claiming by husbands only [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E4]. Wilkie and Coundouris show summed own shares above 100% [10.2307_353530#E16] [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1], and Carriero's rounded unpaired averages show no excess [10.5153_sro.3871#E2]. The studies measure different things: weekly hours, percentage shares, or relative-share ratings, and only Wilkie and Coundouris have both partners' own shares. The sums in Wilkie and Coundouris are the reader's arithmetic on group means.

**Whether over-benefit costs satisfaction (C9).** Adriaans's "a bit less" group is lower [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1], Gillespie's "unfair to partner" group is not for housework [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E6], and Coltrane's review summarises men as not less satisfied [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E23]. Adriaans's fairness item mixes housework and paid work and counts any "less than my fair share" answer, whereas Gillespie asks about housework and about unfairness to the partner.

### Leads

Two kinds of extract sit here. The first group cannot carry a conclusion because the two machine readings of the scanned page differ; those quotes are to be checked by eye against the page. The last group is findings a review reports from other studies.

- **C1, Mederer 1993** (single study): adding the wife's reported proportion of housework time to the controls raised adjusted R² from .052 to .42, and the full model reached .535 [10.2307_352964#E1] [10.2307_352964#E3]. This is the strongest evidence against "small", from one same-source sample, and the page readings differ.
- **C1, Grote & Clark 1998** (single study): the actual division alone explains about .04 of women's housework-fairness variance, about 0 for child care, and about .13 for men, derived as R² minus the step-2 change from a scrambled Table II [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E1] [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E2] [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E3].
- **C1, others:** Lennon & Rosenfield's adjusted R² of .18 for wives [10.1086_230545#E1] and Wilkie's housework beta of -.34 for wives and .22 for husbands [10.2307_353530#E2]. Greenstein's Table 2 R² for fairness is .044 [10.1093_sf_74.3.1029#E2]. Lavee & Katz's split-to-fairness paths are -.36 to -.39 [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E1].
- **C2:** Lennon & Rosenfield's perceived-options and poverty-potential terms predict fairness net of the split, but they are exchange-theory options, not referents [10.1086_230545#E4]. Hawkins dropped the Coprovider Orientation referent measure for low reliability [10.1177_019251395016006002#E9].
- **C3:** in Grote & Clark's analyses of norm use, women's communal-norm use (housework self b .30, spouse .35) and an equal-sharing effort by the spouse (b .24) predict fairness beyond the split, and men's exchange-norm use predicts lower fairness (b -.22 and -.28) [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E6] [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E8] [10.1023_a-1023234732556#E11]. These are norms, not accepted justifications, and the authors say unfairness may drive the exchange norm. ### What would change this

- **C1:** a study that reports the share of fairness variance due to the split alone, entered first, in a dyadic sample with the split measured independently of the fairness rating. Mederer's .42 would then be tested against the 3% to 15% of the other studies.
- **C2:** a study that measures each partner's own referent and enters it with a time-use measure of the split. Within-person or prospective data would show whether comparison drives fairness or fairness drives comparison.
- **C3:** a study that asks each partner which reasons for an unequal split they accept and enters them with the split. Any such result would settle a claim the literature has not tested.
- **C4, C5:** a longitudinal dyadic study with the split, perceived fairness and satisfaction at each wave and a formal indirect-effect test, reporting men and women separately.
- **C6:** couple-level own shares of the same chores, both partners reporting as percentages of the whole, with a test of whether the sum exceeds 100%, against a time-diary baseline.
- **C7:** a study that enters chore identity or chore type with the doer's own time, for the partner who does the chores.
- **C8:** a systematic count of fairness measures across studies. The present count is a count of studies in this read, and five of the single-item studies share one NSFH item.
- **C9:** a measure that separates "unfair to me" from "unfair to my partner" in housework and relationship satisfaction in a sample with enough over-benefited partners.
- **Everything:** the by-eye check of the page readings that differ. The Mederer and Ross and Sicoly figures, if the page confirms them, would move C1 and C6.

### C2. Comparison referents predict a partner's perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for.

Current reading: Weakly and inconsistently supported. Referent effects net of the split appear for male referents in Mikula 2009 and Carriero 2016 (single studies) and are null for female referents; Hawkins's direct referent effect is lost once the wife's perceived division enters, and Grote's panel runs from inequity to comparison.
