## Synthesis

## What makes a partner see the allocation of chores as fair: synthesis, pass 1

**Basis and limits.** Nine papers were read in full; no review or meta-analysis was among them. Two searches for a meta-analysis on perceived fairness and relationship satisfaction found none, and the reviews that exist (Mikula 1998, Shelton & Daphne 1996, Coltrane 2000) are paywalled and requested. Every conclusion below therefore rests on **single studies**, each flagged as one. The nine are the open-access ones, so all date from 2016 or later (the 1990s papers that founded the field, including most sentinels, are not yet read). Most were cross-sectional surveys of different-gender couples or of one partner only. The method never takes gender as an input, so gendered findings are read as failure modes to design against. A verdict of "partial" below means the verifier judged the extract to support the claim only with the caveat named.

### Conclusions

**C1: the actual split explains a small part of perceived fairness (decisive).** The support is thin but points one way. In a Dutch survey of 462 men and 638 women (one study), the model with the three shares of labour plus controls has an R² of 0.047 to 0.139 for housework unfairness [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1]. In a panel of 178 couples across the first year of parenthood (one study, infant care rather than general chores), the whole final model explains about 19% of the variation in fairness, which is an upper bound for the split alone [10.1111_jomf.12331#E1]; the split was nevertheless the strongest single predictor [10.1111_jomf.12331#E2]. In Swedish couples (one study) a semi-equal split sits in both a "both fair" and a "she unfair, he fair" class, so perceived fairness differs at similar splits [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E7]. A small share of variance does not mean a small slope: the same Dutch study calls the effect of the housework share large (standardised 0.83 and 1.33) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2]. The Italian survey is consistent but reports no variance explained: 62.8% of women and 57.4% of men call their share fair although women do about two thirds of the chores (partial) [10.5153_sro.3871#E1].

**C2: comparison referents predict perceived fairness after the actual split (decisive).** One study, the Italian survey of 828 dual-earner couples, supports this for some referents only. Favourable or unfavourable comparisons predict fairness for men comparing themselves with men and for women comparing their partner with men, with the respondent's own share controlled [10.5153_sro.3871#E5]. For husbands comparing themselves with a male friend, the predicted probability of "fair" falls from 0.56 to 0.40 [10.5153_sro.3871#E7]; for wives comparing their partner with their father it falls from 0.63 to 0.45 [10.5153_sro.3871#E8]. The effect is null for comparisons involving women [10.5153_sro.3871#E6]. The authors suspect self-serving bias in choosing referents, so the survey estimate may be too high [10.5153_sro.3871#E9], and the control for the split is a self-reported estimate, so residual confounding is possible [10.5153_sro.3871#E4]. The Swedish study ties a traditional childhood referent to the classes where she calls the split unfair, but it models class membership, not fairness with the split held constant [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E3]. The 30-country study finds referent favourability associated with satisfaction, not fairness, and builds the referent index from the split's own rank [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E3]. Direct evidence is one survey, with one confirming vignette outcome that is behavioural, not fairness [10.5153_sro.3871#E10].

**C3: accepted justifications predict perceived fairness after the actual split (decisive): no direct test in the papers read.** The one study that touches it is mixed. Reduced employment did not work as a justification: women in the unfair classes called the inequality unfair [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E5], and for one class no distributive-justice predictor, referent or justification, was significant [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E6]. In the 30-country study the justifications appear only as moderators of the fairness-satisfaction slope, not as predictors of fairness (partial) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E4]. Gager 1998 and Perales 2014 are the direct tests and are requested.

**C4: perceived fairness is more strongly associated with satisfaction than the actual split is (decisive): the three direct tests disagree (see Disagreements).** The best-fitting evidence is the 30-country study (one study; women only; family-life satisfaction, not relationship satisfaction): adding the fairness item cuts the split's coefficient by 31% [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E5], and with both fairness measures the split coefficient is 0.001 (partial: coefficients are unstandardised) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6]. Two other studies do not reproduce this [10.20377_jfr-1377#E7] [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8], and none of the three compares the strength of the two associations directly.

**C5: perceived fairness mediates the split-satisfaction association (supporting): not formally tested in any paper read.** The only support is a change in coefficients (the split's coefficient shrinks when fairness enters), which the authors call partial and then full mediation (partial) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E8] [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E9]; the authors state the data are cross-sectional and show association only [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E10]. Two other studies run no mediation test and say so: one acknowledges that relationship quality may drive perceived fairness [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E9], the other says longitudinal data are needed [10.20377_jfr-1377#E10]. The abstract of Lavee & Katz 2002 claims mediation by structural equation models (abstract only, see Leads).

**C6: each partner over-claims, so shares sum to more than the whole (decisive): weak support, one dyadic study.** Among 235 UK couples (one study, preregistered, Prolific), the partners' own-share means sum to 103.9% across three loads (partial: arithmetic on group means; the authors do not discuss over-claiming) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1]. The excess is largest for cognitive labour, at 107.0% [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2], then 102.5% for physical [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E3] and 102.3% for emotional chores [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E4]; each participant divided 100% across self, partner and others, so a sum above 100 indicates over-claiming [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E5]. The Italian survey's group means sum to about one, with no sign of over-claiming on average, but they are rounded and unpaired [10.5153_sro.3871#E2]. The Dutch study shows women report a higher female share (0.80) than men do (0.74) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E5], in the direction of over-claiming but in separate samples. The 30-country study surveyed one partner only, so cannot test it [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E12]. The size of any over-claim is therefore small in the one test, a few percentage points, and unconfirmed.

**C7: which chores a partner does predicts perceived fairness beyond time (decisive): no direct test in the papers read.** Housework and childcare were not differently related to unfairness (Wald p = 0.79 for men, 0.61 for women) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E7], but the measures are relative task frequencies, so time and chore type are not separated [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E8]. In mothers, responsibility for child adjustment and for finances, but not routines, was linked with lower partner satisfaction; the outcome is satisfaction, and no time control exists [10.1007_s11199-018-1001-x#E2]. Carlson 2018 (task-level) and Petts 2025 (cognitive labour) are the direct candidates; one is requested.

**C8: perceived fairness is mainly measured with a single self-report item (decisive).** Of the eight papers read that measure perceived fairness, seven use one self-report item: the Swedish couples [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E8], the volunteer web survey with a three-category item [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E1], the German panel [10.20377_jfr-1377#E6], the Italian survey (partial) [10.5153_sro.3871#E13], the Dutch survey (partial) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E9], the 30-country study (partial) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E13] and the UK couple study (partial) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E6]. The eighth, the infant-care panel, builds its outcome from nine task items (see Leads for the one quote, which the quote check places in the abstract). This is a convenience sample of recent open-access papers, not a survey of the field, so it shows the practice but not its prevalence; the review-level answer is in Mikula 1998 and Shelton & Daphne 1996, both requested. The item wording also differs between studies: a "fair share" item is used in some, a fair-to-partner item with a different reference in others.

**C9: an over-benefited partner reports lower satisfaction than one in an equitable allocation (supporting): contradicted for chores in one study, supported in another.** In the German panel (N = 11,012, one study), partners who say they do "a bit less" than their fair share report satisfaction 0.390 points lower than those who say they do a fair share [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1]; the effect is not significant for "much less" (−0.279, SE 0.201) [10.20377_jfr-1377#E2], and men who do much less show no drop while the few women in that group show a strong one [10.20377_jfr-1377#E4]. In the volunteer web survey (N = 10,236, one study), "unfair to partner" versus "mostly fair" shows no association with satisfaction for chores: β −0.03 unadjusted and −0.00 adjusted (partial: unadjusted β is significant) [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E4], b = 0.02, not significant [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E5]. The penalty shows for shared expenses, not chores [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E8]. In the German study "over-benefited" means self-perceived under-contribution across housework and paid work together [10.20377_jfr-1377#E5]; the UK couple study cannot tell over- from under-benefit [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11]. The 30-country study codes over-benefit as the fairest end of a linear scale, so it cannot test the claim [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E16].

**Design implications (from what is read so far, each a failure mode, not a result).** (1) A method cannot infer perceived fairness from the allocation: at similar splits partners land in different fairness classes [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E7], and the split's explained share is small [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1]. Each partner's own judgement has to be collected. (2) Referent effects differ by who the partner compares with [10.5153_sro.3871#E6] [10.5153_sro.3871#E5], so referents are worth recording rather than assuming. (3) Own-share reports can add to more than 100%, most for cognitive labour [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2], so the allocation should not rest on each partner's unaided recall of who does what. (4) Over-benefit and under-benefit are different positions and need an item that records direction [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11].

### Disagreements

**C4, fairness versus the split for satisfaction.**
- *Fairness absorbs the split:* the 30-country study, women only, family-life satisfaction; the split's coefficient falls 31% with one fairness item and to 0.001 with two [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E5] [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6]. It is a preprint, cross-sectional, one study.
- *The split keeps its association beside fairness:* the German panel, the chore-split index has a .365 coefficient (women .523, men .129 n.s.) in the same model as the fairness categories [10.20377_jfr-1377#E7] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E8]; its outcome is single-item satisfaction.
- *The reverse pattern:* in the UK couple study the discrepancy in the reported split related to poorer relationship quality, while the discrepancy in fairness did not [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8].
- *Why they may differ:* the outcome (family-life versus relationship satisfaction on the Dyadic Adjustment Scale), the unit (one partner versus couples), a fairness item that mixes housework with paid work in the German study, and coefficients on different scales, so no paper compares the strength of the two associations directly. The disagreement is open.

**C9, over-benefit.** The German panel finds lower satisfaction for those who say they do a bit less than their share [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1]; the volunteer web survey finds none for chores [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E6]. They differ in sample (a panel of cohabiting people versus a self-selected volunteer sample), in the item ("fair share" in five categories that mixes two domains versus three categories for housework alone), and in the contrast ("fair share" versus "mostly fair"). Neither tests over-benefit against an externally judged equitable allocation.

**C2, referents.** Effects for male referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E5] against nulls for female referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E6] within one study; the authors suggest self-serving bias for some of the former [10.5153_sro.3871#E9].

### What would change this

- **A review or meta-analysis** on fairness and satisfaction would replace the single-study basis of C1, C4, C5 and C9. Mikula 1998 and Shelton & Daphne 1996 are the nearest candidates, and Coltrane 2000 follows them.
- **A study that enters the split and perceived fairness together and compares standardised effects**, ideally with both partners' reports and a relationship satisfaction scale, would settle C4 and C5, where three studies currently disagree.
- **Gager 1998 and Perales 2014 in full text**, or any study that measures justifications and referents and keeps the split in the model, would turn C3 from "no direct test" into a finding or a null.
- **A matched-couple test of the sum of own shares** (Ross & Sicoly 1979, Kamo 2000, Press & Townsley 1998) would settle whether the 2 to 7 point excess in C6 is over-claiming or sampling noise.
- **A study analysing individual chores and cognitive labour against perceived fairness with time held constant** would settle C7; none read so far does.
- **A survey of how fairness is measured** (a systematic count of items across studies) would turn C8 from an observed practice into a prevalence.
