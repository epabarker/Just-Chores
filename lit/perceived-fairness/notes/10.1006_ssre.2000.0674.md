# Kamo 2000: "He Said, She Said", husbands' and wives' reports of the division of household labor

## Design
Secondary analysis of a two-wave national panel survey (NSFH, Time 1 1987-88, Time 2 1992-93). Descriptive paired comparison of husbands' and wives' own and spouse-estimated weekly hours on nine household tasks (paired t tests, percent of couples where own estimate exceeds spouse's estimate), then OLS regressions of the interspouse discrepancy (self estimate minus spouse estimate) on attitudes, perceived fairness, length of marriage and controls (Table 2), and of several measures of the division of labor (husband's time, wife's time, husband's relative share; by husband, by wife, by both) on the same predictors (Table 3). Time 2 outcomes with the Time 1 value of the same measure as a covariate. Not randomised; not preregistered. Perceived fairness is a predictor of discrepancy and of share, never an outcome, and relationship satisfaction is not measured. The "true" division is unknown; the author uses over- and underestimation only relative to the other spouse's report.

## Sample
Married couples from the NSFH main and oversample: 6877 couples at Time 1; 4554 still married to the same person at Time 2; restricted to non-Hispanic white or African American couples, giving 4122 couples (mean ages 43.6 husbands, 41.1 wives; married about 17 years at Time 1). Regressions use N = 1934 (listwise). Attrition: 20.2% of couples not contacted at Time 2, plus separations, divorces and widowhood. Hours above 35 per task recoded to 35; totals above 140 recoded to 140.

## Key results
- Husbands report more of their own hours than wives report for them (total 19.79 vs 16.86; mean paired discrepancy 2.67 h, significant; 62% of couples have the husband's own estimate higher). Wives' own and husbands' estimates of wives' hours are about the same (37.72 vs 37.01; mean discrepancy 0.63 h, not significantly different from zero; 50% of couples). Author: Hypothesis 1 (both over-claim) is supported for husbands, not for wives.
- On the husband's relative share, husbands' own mean is 35.86 and wives' report of the husband's share is 31.64 (Table 3 means; N = 1934). Taking the wife's share as 100 minus the husband's share, the implied self-claimed shares sum to about 104 (35.86 + 68.36), but this is my arithmetic, not a result the paper states, and it rests on the husband's-share measure being the complement of the wife's share. The paper does not test whether self-reported shares sum to more than 100.
- Perceived fairness: a four-item composite (household chores, working for pay, spending money, child care), averaged, alpha .56 (husbands) and .51 (wives) as a sum. Not a single item, and chores are one of four domains.
- Wife's perceived fairness is related to the discrepancy in the husband's share (Table 2); the direction is perceived fairness predicting reporting discrepancy, which is not the claims' direction.
- Claims C1-C5, C7 and C9: no passage bears on them (no regression of fairness on the split, no satisfaction measure, no task-type analysis). Table 3 regresses the husband's share on perceived fairness (the reverse direction) and is not extracted.

## Limitations
Authors': R2 in Table 2 is .02 to .08 because discrepancies are largely random; R2 is an external criterion that depends on the predictors chosen; the true division is unobserved so over- and underestimation are relative. Mine: reports are recalled weekly hours (known to be inflated relative to diaries); capped values; fairness alphas of .51 to .56 mean the fairness composite is weakly reliable; the sample is married, white or African American couples who stayed together 5 years (survivor selection); the study does not address same-sex or unmarried couples; the pooled-chores totals cannot speak to chore type; the sum-over-100 reading for C6 is derived, not reported.
