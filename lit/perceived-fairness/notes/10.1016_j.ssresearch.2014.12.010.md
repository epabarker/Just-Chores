# Perales, Baxter & Tai 2015: Gender, justice and work: a distributive approach to perceptions of housework fairness

## Design
Observational panel study (HILDA, Australia, waves 2001-2011). Outcome: a single self-report item ("Do you think you do your fair share of work around the house?", 5 categories), dichotomised to "I do more than my fair share" (1) versus fair share or less (0). Estimator: person fixed-effects conditional logit (Chamberlain), run separately for women and men, so only within-person change over time identifies the odds ratios. Exposures are life course transitions (parenthood, relationship status change), not the actual split or referents. The actual split (couple weekly housework hours and the respondent's share) enters as a control, together with time-availability and relative-resource variables. Robustness: person-specific-threshold and blow-up-and-cluster ordered fixed-effects logit (Table 4), plus linear models (footnote 4). Not preregistered. The "distributive justice" mechanisms (comparison referents, valued outcomes, entitlement, identity) are not measured; they are inferred only as a residual: any transition effect left after controls is read as suggestive of subjective change.

## Sample
HILDA married or cohabiting opposite-sex couples, both partners aged 18-64, no prior partnership, no missing key variables. Final analytical sample 54,936 observations (27,468 per gender) from 5790 couples. Fixed-effects models use only individuals whose outcome varies: Table 3 Model 3 has 2034 women (14,732 observations) and 1296 men (9244 observations), after dropping 3756 and 4494 individuals. Nothing about satisfaction, referents, justifications or the type of chore is collected.

## Key results
- Outcome measure: one self-report item on own fair share, dichotomised (C8). Descriptively, 60.2% of women and 14.8% of men report doing more than their fair share (Table 2); women do a mean 73% of couple housework hours (Table 1).
- Net of the actual split (couple housework hours and own share), time availability, resources and person fixed effects, parenthood still predicts women's perceived unfairness: odds ratios 1.96 (one child), 2.79 (two or more), 0.75 (recent birth) in Model 3. The authors say the effect "weakens only slightly" once these objective factors enter. This is evidence that something other than the measured split moves perceived fairness (relevant to C1 in direction only; no variance explained is reported) but it is a residual inference, not a measurement of referents or justifications (C2, C3).
- The actual split matters strongly within person: odds ratio on own share of housework hours 5.98 (women) and 7.13 (men), per unit of a 0-1 share (Table 3). No R-squared or variance decomposition is reported, so C1 ("explains only a small part of the variation") is not tested.
- Relationship transitions: raw effects among women fade in the final model, which the authors take to mean they work only through time and resources.
- Not addressed: relationship satisfaction (C4, C5, C9), self-reported shares summing past the whole (C6; the table reports only respondent shares that sum to one by construction) and which chores are done (C7; only hours are measured). No extracts for C4, C5, C6, C9.

## Limitations
Authors' own: the residual test attributes leftover transition effects to subjective dispositions only if every instrumental factor is controlled; omitted factors would bias that reading. Also, childcare may be mentally included in the housework judgment after a birth (footnote 7), gender-role attitudes are available in only four waves and are not in the main models, and outsourcing of tasks is unmeasured. Wald tests across gender are caveated because logit effect sizes are not comparable across samples (Mood 2010).
Mine: no direct measure of comparison referents or accepted justifications, so C2 and C3 are untested here; the estimand is the effect of a life course transition, not of referents. The fixed-effects estimator drops individuals with no change in the outcome (about 65% of women and 78% of men are dropped in Model 3), so results come from a selected subsample. The binary outcome cuts at "more than fair share", so it cannot separate under-benefit from over-benefit, and the single self-referenced item confounds perceived fairness with perceived own workload. The "share" and "hours" controls are survey self-reports, with no check on over-claiming. Cross-sectional variance explained is not reported, so C1 cannot be assessed on this paper.
