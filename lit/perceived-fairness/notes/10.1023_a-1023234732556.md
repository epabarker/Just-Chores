# Grote & Clark 1998: Distributive justice norms and family work

## Design
Cross-sectional, within-couple questionnaire study (second wave of a longitudinal study of the transition to first parenthood). Not experimental, no identification strategy beyond statistical control. Estimand: for each spouse separately, the standardized regression coefficient of reported norm use (communal, exchange, equality; self and spouse; housework and child care domains) on perceived fairness of the division in the same domain, with the respondent's own report of the actual division entered at step 1 and the norm at step 2 (hierarchical OLS regression, 24 equations per gender in Table II, one norm at a time). The paper reports mean ratings of norms (Table I, repeated-measures MANOVA with Bonferroni paired t tests) and these regressions. No preregistration. Analysis population: 120-121 per equation, each gender analysed separately (couple non-independence not modelled across the two partners' equations). Relationship satisfaction is not measured.

## Sample
121 married couples assessed 6 months after the birth of their first child, from 142 who agreed initially (12 couples lost: 2 miscarriages, 3 separations, 6 declined, 1 moved). Recruited from prenatal childbirth classes in three urban hospitals in the US (Pittsburgh) and from university electronic bulletin boards. Mostly Caucasian, well educated (41% of women and 47% of men with a graduate degree). At 6 months 40% of women and 87% of men worked full-time. Both partners completed identical questionnaires separately.

## Key results
- Actual division and perceived fairness: women who did more housework perceived it as less fair (b = -.21 in the final equation, p < .05); men who did less housework (own share low) saw it as less fair (b = .36, p < .001). In Table II the share of variance in fairness from the actual division is not reported directly; it can be backed out as total R2 minus the step-2 change. For women: housework about .04 (R2 .13 minus change .09), child care about .00 to .01. For men: housework about .13, child care about .13 to .14.
- Norm use after controlling for the actual division, women: self communal (housework b = .30, child care b = .19), spouse communal (housework b = .35, child care b = .42) and spouse equality (housework b = .24, child care b = .19) predicted higher fairness; self exchange (housework b = -.18) predicted lower fairness. Step-2 R2 change up to .16 (spouse communal, child care).
- Norm use after controlling for the actual division, men: self and spouse communal and equality did not predict fairness; self and spouse exchange predicted lower fairness (housework b = -.20 and -.24; child care b = -.22 and -.28; R2 change .04 to .06).
- The authors read the communal norm as a basis on which an unequal division can still be seen as fair because needs may differ. Norm-use items are a general rule the respondent says they or the spouse follow, not an explicit justification for an unequal split.
- Fairness was measured with multi-item scales (5 housework items alpha .80; 8 child care items alpha .81), not a single item. The actual division was a respondent-rated relative share on a 5-point scale (spouse mostly to you mostly), not time or proportions of the whole.

## Limitations
Authors: social desirability may have influenced reports of norm use and fairness (they judge it unlikely); exchange-norm and equality-norm findings may reflect reverse causation, since the authors themselves argue that "perceptions of unfairness may lead to use of an exchange norm"; unexplained null for women's exchange norm in child care.
Reader: cross-sectional and same-respondent reports of norm use and fairness, so shared method variance and reverse causation (fairness feelings shaping reported norms) are not separable; the actual division is the respondent's own report, not an independent measure, and is in a bounded rating scale, so fairness and division are not independent measurements; norms entered one at a time with no correction for the many equations (24 per gender); men's and women's equations are not jointly tested for gender differences; no tests of dyadic non-independence in the regressions; recoding of "I don't think about it this way" to strongly disagree; task-level data were collapsed into two scales so which chores are done is not examined; sample is a narrow, well-educated, first-time-parent US sample measured at one time point; the full set of t statistics in Table I is available only on request. The paper does not measure relationship satisfaction, comparison referents, or whether partners over-claim.
