# Mikula 1998: Division of household labor and perceived justice, a growing field of research

## Design
Narrative (non-systematic) review, selective by the author's own statement: only studies that explicitly measured fairness or justice of the division of household labor; child care and other family work excluded; no distinction between household types. No search strategy, no inclusion counts, no effect-size pooling, no risk-of-bias appraisal. Almost all cited findings are from observational, cross-sectional regression studies (mostly OLS on NSFH survey data). Every result below is therefore secondhand: the review reports other papers' findings and mostly gives direction and significance, not coefficients, intervals or the covariates in each model. Part 2 of the review treats perceived justice as the outcome; part 3 treats it as a predictor of marital quality and well-being.

## Sample
No sample of its own. Reviewed studies are mainly of US wives and husbands (NSFH), plus student shared households (Mikula et al. 1997a), employed wives, and couples at the transition to parenthood. The author notes that most studies were of women and that men's views are neglected.

## Key results
- C1: The author concludes that the actual division is "by no means the main determinant" of perceived justice, on three grounds: most people rate the division as fair despite gross inequality; variance explained by quantitative workload measures is small; and the best-correlating measures are symbolic (men's share of female-type tasks). Most studies explained no more than 20% of the variance in fairness ratings with all predictors; Hawkins et al. (1995) explained 66% of wives' fairness variance with a model rich in cognitions; Mederer (1993) explained over 50% with three labor measures plus controls, but the author calls her fairness measure problematic and semantically overlapping the predictors. No R-squared is given for the split alone.
- C2 and C3: Evidence is correlational and reported without the adjustment set. Hawkins et al. (1995): wives rated the division less fair the more they compared husbands' contributions with their own (between-gender referents). Grote and Clark (1998b): both between-gender and within-gender comparison frequency were negatively related to fairness. The author raises reverse causation (perceived injustice motivating comparison) and says no evidence decides between the directions. Justifications: Hawkins et al. found more fairness when the division was "worked out together"; Freudenthaler and Mikula (1998) found perceived lack of justification acted indirectly via blame. The review does not say whether the actual split was controlled in these models.
- C4: Studies that included both the division and perceived justice "typically found" justice the more important indicator of marital quality (Blair 1993, 1998; Greenstein 1996; Ward 1993). One stated exception (Perry-Jenkins and Folk 1994, working-class wives, conflict outcome). Associations are stronger with women, or significant only for women, and vary by gender ideology, class and wives' employment status.
- C5: The author says mediation has received little systematic attention. Only two pieces of evidence: Robinson and Spitze (1992), well-being (not relationship satisfaction), women only; Reichle's work, injustice perceptions acting through emotional responses to restrictions on marital satisfaction (new parents).
- C6: Not addressed. The only related point is a general caveat that perceived injustice may distort respondents' estimates of own and partner contributions.
- C7: Female-type (routine) task contributions are better correlated with perceived justice than male-type, neutral or global measures; relative size beats absolute hours. These are comparisons of correlations across task types, not tests of task type beyond time; the author infers symbolic meaning is more relevant than "mere amount of time and effort".
- C8: The most common database (NSFH) uses one fairness item, and the author names one-item measures of questionable reliability as a shared problem. The review does not count studies by measure type, so "mainly" is supported qualitatively only. Other studies used direct single justice items, composites mixing fairness and satisfaction, or indirect measures.
- C9: Not tested for relationship satisfaction. Equity theory predicts under-benefit is more distressing than over-benefit; men perceive the division as fairer; in student households a smaller own share predicted more satisfaction with the division (not with the relationship).

## Limitations
Authors' own (the review's): one-item fairness measures of questionable reliability; skewed fairness distributions (about two thirds rate the division as just) analysed with OLS; mostly additive models; unclear causal ordering, including that perceived injustice may distort reported contributions and that predictors may be effects of fairness; fairness and satisfaction conflated in some studies; ideal or appropriate division rarely measured; scope limited to household labor and mostly to women.
Mine: narrative and selective, with no search or appraisal, so direction-counting of results cannot be weighed; no effect sizes, intervals or covariate sets for any cited finding; no extractable evidence on over-claiming (C6) or on over-benefit and relationship satisfaction (C9); the "66% variance" and "20% of variation" figures compare models of unlike content and cannot be read as the share due to the actual split; published 1998 so it predates later work; several cited sources are unpublished or submitted manuscripts.
