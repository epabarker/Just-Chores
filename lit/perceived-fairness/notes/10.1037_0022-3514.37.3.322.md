# Ross 1979: Egocentric biases in availability and attribution

## Design
Five-experiment report (Ross & Sicoly 1979, JPSP 37(3), 322-336) on egocentric bias in memory availability and in attributions of responsibility for joint products. Only Experiment 1 bears on this question: a cross-sectional, within-couple questionnaire study of married couples. Each spouse separately marked, for each of 20 activities, a 150-mm line labelled "primarily wife" / "primarily husband". The two spouses' marks were converted to "units of responsibility" and summed per activity; a sum above 150 means at least one spouse over-claims. The couple's composite is the mean of (sum - 150) over the 20 activities (19 if childless), tested against zero with the couple as the unit of analysis. Experiment 1 also had a free-recall measure (examples of own vs. spouse's contributions), correlated with the over-claiming score. No preregistration (1979). Experiments 2-5 are laboratory or basketball-team studies of recall and responsibility and are not about household division. The paper does not measure perceived fairness, relationship satisfaction, comparison referents, justifications, equity or over-benefit anywhere.

## Sample
Experiment 1: 37 married couples living in student residences (20 with children), recruited by door-knocking by two female research assistants, paid $5, tested in their apartment; questionnaire completed individually, anonymity assured. Preliminary interviews with six other couples shaped the questionnaire. Degrees of freedom in the tests (F(1,35); F(1,32)) imply 36 couples in the composite and 33 in the per-item test, so some couples had missing data (not explained in the text). Other samples (not married couples): pilot seminar students and 13 dyads in cafeterias/lounges (speaking time), Experiment 2 (22 student dyads, 37 men and 7 women), Experiment 3 (158 intercollegiate basketball players, 12 teams), Experiment 4 (40 male undergraduates), Experiment 5 (29 psychology graduate students). No household-task data outside Experiment 1.

## Key results
Samples that were married couples: Experiment 1 only. Household tasks: yes, but only inside a 20-item list of couple activities (see the first extract), of which cleaning dishes, cleaning house, shopping for groceries, washing the clothes, taking out the garbage, making breakfast and making the house messy are chores; the rest are childcare, decisions, social and affective items. The composite pools chores with non-chores and the text reports no chore-only or item-level result.
- Composite over-claiming: M = 4.67 units (of 150) above zero, F(1,35) = 12.89, p < .001; 27 of 37 couples over-estimated (sign test p < .025); over-estimation on 16 of 20 items on average, none significantly under-estimated (supports C6 in direction; the effect is small, about 3% of the scale length, and the scale was used in a restricted range).
- Availability: more examples of own (M = 10.9) than spouse's (M = 8.1) contributions, F(1,35) = 36.0, p < .001; correlated with over-claiming r(35) = .50, p < .01 (not a causal test; the authors say so in the General Discussion).
- Nothing in the paper bears on C1-C5, C7, C8 or C9.

## Limitations
Authors' own: the magnitude of over-estimation was small and subjects used a restricted range of the scale; no definitive evidence that the availability bias causes the responsibility bias. Mine: the 150-mm line is a bipolar scale, so sum > 150 is the definition of over-claiming and carries no information about the distribution of true shares; there is no behavioural criterion for the actual split, so "over-claim" is inferred from the two reports exceeding the whole, not from a measured truth; the composite averages over 19-20 heterogeneous items including negative ones (causing arguments, irritating spouse), so it is not a chores-only estimate; no per-item values or intervals are reported; unit of analysis is the couple, no sex-specific results (husband vs. wife over-claim not separated); convenience sample of student-residence couples in 1970s Canada; the item set is not the modern chores set. The paper was read from a PDF text layer, not a scan. Some OCR-like artefacts exist in the text (e.g. "ISO" for 150).

## Follow up
Papers this one cites that the question needs: none identified; the cited literature is on memory availability and attribution (Tversky & Kahneman 1973; Jones & Nisbett 1971). Later studies of chore-specific over-claiming in couples (not cited here) would be needed for chores alone.
