# Grote 2002: Perceiving the division of family work to be unfair (social comparisons, enjoyment, competence)

## Design
Observational, three-wave longitudinal panel (pregnancy, about 6-7 months postpartum, about 12-15 months postpartum) of married couples expecting a first child; mailed self-report questionnaires plus phone interviews, each spouse completing separately. Not an experiment, no identification strategy beyond (a) concurrent regression and (b) prospective hierarchical regressions that control the earlier value of the outcome. Not preregistered (none stated). Hypotheses were derived from the distributive justice model (Thompson 1991, Major 1993): same-sex comparison referents, enjoyment, own competence and spouse-perceived competence should each buffer perceived unfairness. Analyses: mixed-model MANOVAs on means (Tables 1-2), zero-order correlations with perceived fairness (Table 3), multiple regression of perceived fairness on comparisons and outcome values (Table 4; standardised betas, R2 given in text), and prospective hierarchical regressions in both directions (Figure 1 and text). Analysis population: complete-data wives and husbands, N 177-181 per analysis, wives and husbands analysed in separate models. Estimand: conditional associations, not effects. The actual split ("total division of tasks") is a 5-point self-rated relative scale, not hours or shares. Marital satisfaction was not measured in this paper.

Reading caution: in the text layer of this PDF the minus signs, "=" and "<" symbols are dropped (for example "F(5, 173)  17.4, p  .01; R2  .33"). Every coefficient in Tables 3 and 4 therefore appears unsigned. Directions below come from the authors' prose, not from the table cells. Figure 1 (cross-lagged betas) was not viewed.

## Sample
181 married couples (first-time expectant parents), recruited mainly from prenatal childbirth classes in three urban hospitals, others through two university electronic bulletin boards. 210 couples agreed, 200 completed Time 1, 185 completed Time 2, 181 completed Time 3 (overall attrition 9.5%; dropout differed only by race, with proportionately more African American women and men leaving after Time 1). Mean age 31 (women) and 32 (men); mostly Caucasian (171 women, 166 men); 77% of women and 75% of men held college or graduate degrees; family income range on average $51,000 to $75,000; married on average 3 years at Time 1. Employment: 76% of women and 92% of men full-time at Time 1; 40% and 90% at Time 2. Measures: perceived fairness per task (4 housework tasks at Times 1-2; 8 child care tasks at Time 2; single items at Time 3), 5-point scale, averaged to indices.

## Key results
- C1: Table 3 "total division of tasks" correlates with perceived fairness at |r| = .00, .16, .15 for wives (housework Time 1, Time 2, child care Time 2) and |r| = .33, .30, .32 for husbands. Squared, that is at most about 11% of variance (husbands) and 0 to 3% (wives); this squaring is my calculation, signs are not recoverable from the text layer, and the split is a self-rated relative scale. By contrast the regression models of comparisons plus outcome values (no split term entered) reach R2 .23 to .46 (Table 4 text). So the split explains little of the variation in fairness here, more for husbands than for wives. Table 4 does not enter the split, so the paper gives no R2 for the split adjusted for the other predictors.
- C2: Spousal comparison frequency predicts lower perceived fairness concurrently for both spouses (Table 3 r .17 to .49; Table 4 betas .33, .25, .19 for wives, marginal .13, .13, .08 for husbands, signs per prose). Footnote 2 says that when the division of housework or child care was controlled, all Table 3 correlations but one stayed significant; the controlled coefficients and the exception are not reported. Same-sex comparison frequency was hypothesised to raise fairness; instead it was correlated with less fairness (Table 3), and in Table 4 it adds little (wives, other women .00, .02, .09; husbands, other men .15 at Time 1 then .10, .01). Prospectively (controlling the earlier value of the outcome, not the split) spousal comparisons did not predict later fairness, while perceived unfairness predicted later spousal comparisons. So referent frequency predicts fairness in cross-section, the direction is contested by the authors' own prospective data, and the hypothesised buffering by same-sex referents is not found.
- C3: Justification rules (the first component of the model) were not measured here. The paper cites Grote and Clark 1998 for justice rules. No evidence on C3 from this paper.
- C4, C5, C9: Not testable from this paper: no relationship satisfaction or marital quality outcome, no mediation test, no over-benefit versus equitable grouping. The paper only motivates the question from earlier work. The satisfaction follow-up on the same sample is Grote and Clark 2001.
- C6: Self-rated division means (1 = spouse mostly, 3 = both equally, 5 = self mostly): housework Time 1 wives 3.68, husbands 2.49; Time 2 wives 3.89, husbands 2.36; child care Time 2 wives 4.02, husbands 2.10. If both reports were consistent each pair would sum to 6; they sum to 6.17, 6.25 and 6.12. That is the direction of mutual over-claiming, but the difference is 0.12 to 0.25 scale points against SDs of 0.58 to 0.87, ordinal scale points are not shares of the whole, no test of the sum or the couple-level discrepancy is reported, and the sums are my arithmetic. Both spouses agree the wife does more.
- C7: The chore split and fairness are rated per task but analysed as 4-task and 8-task indices, with housework and child care analysed separately; no analysis by chore identity or against time spent. The authors list the lack of raw hours or proportion of time as a limitation. Chore-level qualities (enjoyment, competence) predict fairness (Table 4), but they are averaged across chores, not a test of which chores are done. So the paper cannot support "beyond time".
- C8: Perceived fairness here was multi-item per domain (alpha .63 to .83) at Times 1-2, and single-item only at Time 3 because of the shorter survey; the authors list the single items as a limitation. This paper is a counterexample to "mainly single item", not a survey of the literature.

## Limitations
Authors' own: self-report bias; categorical hours worked and income; two one-item fairness measures at Time 3; subjective rather than raw-hours division; fairness measure did not specify fair to whom; generalisability beyond middle and upper-middle class couples and beyond the transition to parenthood.
Mine: observational, single sample, no experimental or instrumental identification; all predictors and outcomes are same-respondent self-reports (shared method variance), and many alphas are low (.53 to .70 for several composites). The authors themselves find a reverse path (perceived inequity prompts comparisons), so concurrent betas for comparisons are not effects on fairness. Table 4 does not adjust for the actual split; the footnote 2 control is reported only in prose, without coefficients. Wives and husbands analysed separately without a test of equality. Many correlations and models with no multiplicity adjustment; prospective null results at N about 180 have no intervals in the text. Coefficient signs are unreadable in the text layer (see Design).
