# Lennon 1994: Relative fairness and the division of housework, the importance of options

## Design
Cross-sectional, observational secondary analysis of the 1987-88 National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH), a weighted national probability sample. No randomisation, no preregistration (1994 sociology paper). Survey-weighted OLS regressions with balanced-repeated-replication (BRR) standard errors, all coefficients unstandardized. Husbands and wives are independent respondents, not couples (the sample "restricted ... to employed individuals ... married and living with their employed spouses ... these individuals are not married to one another"). Three stages: (1) descriptive gender comparison of chore shares and fairness (Tables 2-4); (2) OLS of relative fairness on options, relative income, gender ideology and relative paid hours, among employed wives, adjusting for the actual split (Table 5); (3) OLS of CES-D depressive symptoms (log) on fairness and fairness squared, among employed wives (Table 6). The outcome in stage 3 is psychological well-being (12-item CES-D), not relationship satisfaction. The actual split is a covariate in Table 5 and is absent from Table 6.

Fairness is one item recoded -1 (very unfair to me) to +1 (very unfair to my spouse), with 0 = fair to both. "Actual split" is respondent-reported weekly hours on tasks, split into "female" tasks (meals, shopping, cleaning, laundry, dishes) and "male" tasks (bills, outside work, car), and each respondent's proportion of the couple's hours per category.

## Sample
N = 3,236 employed, married individuals aged 18-65 living with an employed spouse (NSFH 13,017 interviewed, 74.4% response). Table 2 N: 1,126 men and 1,057 wives (a third of cases missing task data, 32.5% after imputation). Table 3 N: 1,545 men, 1,691 women. Table 5 (fairness regression, wives): unweighted N = 789. Table 6 (depression regression, wives): unweighted N = 1,485. Median age 38, 86.3% white, 82.1% full-time. Single wave, so no attrition.

## Key results
- Descriptive: women report doing 33.2 h/week (68.2% of the couple's chores), men 18.2 h (34.5%); men report their wives do 65.5%. 60.8% of women and 67.5% of men call the split fair to both; 35.3% of women and 4.3% of men call it unfair to self. Mean fairness -.19 (women), .13 (men).
- Table 5 (wives, N=789, adjusted R2 = .18, adjusting for the actual split): % female tasks done by respondent b = -.007 (SE .001, p<=.001); % male tasks done b = -.001 (SE .001, ns); marital happiness .048*** (SE .010); perceived options -.044* (SE .019); poverty potential .107* (SE .049); conventional gender ideology .002 (SE .015, ns); % of paid hours -.001 (ns); % of earnings .000 (ns). The adjusted R2 is for the whole model; no R2 is given for the split alone or for the other predictors alone.
- Table 6 (wives, N=1,485, adjusted R2 = .10): fairness b = .086 (SE .070, ns), fairness squared b = .188 (SE .084, p<=.05); marital happiness -.070***. Authors read this as a U shape, with symptoms lowest when the split is seen as fair and higher when seen as unfair either way. Only 3.9% of women say unfair to spouse (N=52 in Table 4), so the over-benefited arm is estimated off a small group.
- Authors' reading of C2/C3-related tests: "no statistically significant association" for gender ideology and for relative paid hours; options (perceived, and poverty potential) are the only significant social-exchange predictors; relative income is not.

Mapping to claims: C1 and the R2 point are only indirectly addressed (no partition of variance); C2 is addressed through "options" and "reference group" theory, but no comparison referent is measured; C3 is not measured (gender ideology is the nearest proxy and is null); C4 and C5 are not tested (the outcome is depressive symptoms, and the split is not in the outcome model); C6 is only addressed in aggregate from independent samples; C7 is partly addressed by the task-category shares; C8 is an instance (one item); C9 is addressed for depressive symptoms, not satisfaction.

## Limitations
Authors' own: cross-sectional, so "only prospective studies can help resolve questions of causal direction" (options may follow fairness, and depression may colour perceived unfairness); perceived options may partly reflect marital quality (handled by a marital-happiness control, which could itself be a mediator); spouses' reports not matched; 34% of respondents missing task data and imputation by means; analysis of fairness restricted to wives, with husbands only in footnoted parallel analyses.

Mine: (a) The version is a vision model's reading of a scan (MinerU OCR of a JSTOR scan); table cells, symbols and minus signs are machine-read and the Table 4 footnote asterisks are garbled in the text (a "**" printed for "*** P<=.001"). Quoted numbers should be checked against the page images if they decide anything. (b) The fairness item is a single self-report item, so measurement error is unquantified. (c) Time is respondent-reported hours, which is crude; the authors note that "female" tasks are more time-consuming, so chore type and time are not separable. (d) Marital happiness (a covariate in Table 5) is a strong predictor of fairness (b = .048***) and is conceptually near relationship satisfaction; it is a control, so it cannot be read as an outcome. (e) The U-shape rests on a quadratic term; the linear term is null and the unfair-to-spouse arm is about 4% of wives. (f) Table 5 includes many collinear covariates (shares and couple hours by task type); 789 of 1,057 wives in the sample. (g) The social-exchange reading of options is not distinguished from reverse causation or cognitive dissonance, as the authors concede.
