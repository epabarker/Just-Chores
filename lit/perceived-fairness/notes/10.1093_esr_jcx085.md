# Hu and Yucel (2018): What fairness? Gendered housework division and family life satisfaction across 30 countries

Note on relevance: the outcome throughout is the woman's family life satisfaction (one item), not relationship satisfaction in a narrow sense, and perceived fairness is always a predictor or mediator, never an outcome. The paper never regresses perceived fairness on the actual split, so C1 and C3 (as worded) are not tested and C2 is only indirectly addressed. It never records which chores a woman does (C7) and never compares the two partners' reports (C6). Only the "women do less than their fair share" cells touch C9. It bears most directly on C4, C5 and C8.

## Design
Cross-sectional, observational, multilevel (two-level mixed-effects linear regression, individuals nested in 30 countries), 2012 ISSP "Family and Changing Gender Roles" plus UNDP country indicators. Outcome: single 7-point item on family life satisfaction (reversed so high = satisfied). Key predictors: women's share of the couple's housework time (respondent's own report of her hours and her partner's hours; actual split proxy, 0-1); relational comparison (single 5-point item on whether she does her fair share; the "perceived fairness" measure, coded as continuous); referential comparison (a derived index: country percentile rank of her housework share, reversed, minus country percentile rank of her gender-ideology score; compares her with other women in the same country). Model 1 has the share and controls; Model 2 adds relational fairness; Model 3 adds referential comparison; Model 4 adds both; Model 5 adds their interaction; Models 6-9 add random slopes and cross-level interactions with country gender equality. "Mediation" is the proportional change in the share coefficient when a fairness measure is added (no formal mediation test, no bootstrap CI). No preregistration mentioned. No causal identification strategy; authors say results are association only. Analysis population: 10,467 women in co-residing heterosexual (married or cohabiting) couples.

## Sample
Women only. 12,785 women co-residing with partners in 30 of the 37 ISSP 2012 countries (China, India, Spain, South Africa, Taiwan, Turkey and Venezuela dropped for lack of comparable measures); 2,318 (c. 18%) dropped for missing key variables (family life satisfaction, gender ideology, housework division), leaving 10,467. Dropped women were older (mean 50.57 vs 47.30), so older women are slightly under-represented. ISSP is a (multi-stage) stratified random household sample with one respondent aged 16+ per household; only one partner per couple is interviewed. Means: satisfaction 5.64 (SD 1.02) on 1-7; share of housework 0.71 (SD 0.19); weekly housework 19.88 hours. About 20% lack income data (coded as a missing category). Women's relational fairness answers: 29.6% much more than fair share, 26.8% more, 38.6% about fair, 3.3% less, 1.7% much less.

## Key results
- Model 2: relational fairness predicts satisfaction, B = 0.093 (SE 0.011), beta = 0.091 [0.071, 0.111], controlling the actual share (B = -0.397 (SE 0.056)). Predictive margin 5.911 at relational fairness = 5 vs 5.530 at 1.
- The share coefficient falls from -0.577 (M1) to -0.397 (M2) with relational fairness (31.11% "mediated"), to -0.111 (M3) with referential comparison (81.76%), and to 0.001 (SE 0.138) in M4 with both. In M4 relational B = 0.092-0.093 and referential B = 0.261 (beta = 0.112 [0.042, 0.182]; relational beta = 0.093 [0.072, 0.114]). The authors say both dimensions together "explained almost all" of the share-satisfaction link.
- The two dimensions barely mediate each other (14.04% and 1.86%). Interaction (M5): referential x relational -0.044 (SE 0.022); the referential slope is steeper where relational fairness is low.
- Country gender equality moderates the relational slope (B = 0.030, SE 0.014, 24.12% of the random-slope variance) but not the referential slope (B = 0.005, SE 0.029; 0.48%).
- Individual-level "justification" interactions with relational fairness (gender ideology beta = -0.011 [-0.029, 0.007]; resource dependence beta = -0.012 [-0.030, 0.006]) are small and the intervals include 0; paid work time beta = -0.015 [-0.033, -0.004].
- Robustness (Supplementary Tables S3-S5, read in full): excluding women who did less than their fair share and coding relational fairness as ordinal gives fair vs very unfair B = 0.275 (SE 0.024) and unfair vs very unfair 0.150 (SE 0.025) in the first model; results hold for ages 30-60 and with extra controls.

## Limitations
Authors' own: cross-sectional, so "only be interpreted in terms of association, not causality"; one randomly selected partner per couple; women only; only two comparison referents; the time measure is a generic recall estimate, less accurate than time diaries.

Mine: (1) Outcome is satisfaction, so nothing here estimates what predicts perceived fairness (C1-C3). (2) Fairness and satisfaction are reported by the same woman at the same time, so reverse causation, common-method bias and an unmeasured mood or relationship-quality confounder can all produce the fairness-satisfaction coefficient; the "mediation" percentages are coefficient-change comparisons that assume no confounding of fairness and satisfaction, with no sensitivity analysis. (3) The referential index is computed from the same housework share and gender-ideology variables that are also controls; its coefficient rests on a rank-based nonlinear transformation and is highly collinear with them (share and ideology coefficients flip from significant in M1 to near 0 in M3), so its large "mediation" share is partly an artefact of construction. (4) Relational fairness is coded 1 = much more than fair share to 5 = much less than fair share and treated as linear "higher = fair", so over-benefited women (5.0% of the sample) are scored as fairer than those who do their fair share; the linear slope cannot reveal any dip for over-benefit (C9). The coding makes the label "fair" for value 5 a misnomer. (5) The share is a single respondent's report of her own and her partner's hours; whether men's and women's reports sum above 100% cannot be seen (C6). (6) Standardised beta for the share is not reported, so "fairness is more strongly associated than the split" (C4) rests on the share coefficient dropping to 0.001 once fairness is controlled, not on a direct standardised contrast. (7) No multiplicity adjustment across nine models and several hypotheses; significance reported at p < .05. (8) Figures 1 and 2 were not viewed; no claim here rests on them.
