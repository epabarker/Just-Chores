# Sanchez 1994: Gender, labor allocations, and the psychology of entitlement within the home

## Design
Cross-sectional secondary analysis of matched couple reports in the National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH; the abstract says 1988, the Data section says 1987). Seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR), a two-equation system: women's and men's perceived fairness regressed on both partners' reported weekly housework hours and employment hours, with cross-equation equality constraints tested by Wald statistics. Observational; no identification strategy beyond covariate adjustment; not preregistered. The outcome is a single five-point item scored 1-5 with higher meaning more unfair to the woman, so men's and women's perceptions are both coded in the woman's direction. The paper tests whether own and partner's hours affect fairness differently by gender (the entitlement hypotheses of Major 1987 and Thompson 1991). Comparison referents and justifications are not measured; relationship satisfaction is not measured.

## Sample
Couple sample N = 2,618: primary-respondent women (main sample and oversample) who are black or white, currently married with spouse present or cohabiting, and whose partner returned the self-administered questionnaire. NSFH response rates: primary respondents 74% (main) and 77% (oversample); secondary respondents 83% (married) and 77% (cohabiting). Interracial couples are 3.5%. Hours are self-reported by each partner about self and partner; child-care time is excluded. Mean housework hours per week: woman 37.87, man 16.49 (Appendix A).

## Key results
- Table 1: 30% of women and 4% of men say the division is unfair to themselves; 66% of wives and 72% of husbands say fair; 13% of couples agree it is unfair to the woman, 1% agree it is unfair to the man. Women's mean 3.32 (SD .65), men's mean 3.22 (SD .56).
- Table 2 (hours only, no controls or constraints): adjusted R2 .03 for women's and .03 for men's equation. Table 3 (all controls, all constraints): adjusted R2 .04 and .04. Appendix C (controls, no constraints): .04 and .05. Authors: "less than 5% of the incremental variance"; "the low explained variance also suggests that fairness perceptions are a far more subtle, complex phenomena than can be represented by these indicators."
- Table 3: man's housework -.006 (SE .001) in both equations, woman's housework .002 (SE .000) in both; woman's employment hours .002 (SE .001) for women, -.000 for men; man's employment hours .001, ns. The coefficients are unstandardised per hour on a 1-5 scale.
- Only the total of nine household tasks is used. The paper does not test which chores a partner does. It reports Blair and Johnson (1992) finding husbands' participation in feminine tasks the strongest predictor of wives' fairness.
- Relationship satisfaction, mediation, over-claiming (the two partners' shares are not summed), over-benefit and justifications are not analysed. Comparison referents are explicitly not measured.

## Limitations
Authors' own: the survey could not capture contradictory or unresolved feelings; child-care time is omitted; low explained variance; few cohabiters, possible measurement error; cannot determine which referents respondents use. Ours: cross-sectional, so no causal reading of the hours-fairness association; hours are self-reported by each partner, and the paper does not report partner-report discrepancies; the fairness outcome is one item coded in the woman's direction, so a man's "perceived fairness to himself" is not analysed as such; R2 is adjusted R2 on a 1-5 ordinal item treated as continuous; the explained share is of hours (not a share-of-chores measure) and controls; the sample is restricted to black and white couples whose partner returned the questionnaire.

Re-read note: footnote 7 (p.20, 'flat, simplistic, nonmanipulable, non-reinterpretable questions', see Smith 1987) is a feminist methodological critique tied to low explained variance; it is not a description of this paper's fairness item and is not used as an extract for C8.
