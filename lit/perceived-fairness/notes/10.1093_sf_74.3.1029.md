# Greenstein 1996: Gender ideology and perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor: effects on marital quality

## Design
Cross-sectional secondary analysis of wave 1 of the National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH), married wives only. Three weighted OLS regressions on the same 13 predictors plus interaction terms: (1) perceived fairness of the division of chores, (2) wife's marital happiness, (3) wife's perceived marital stability. Theory is relative deprivation (Crosby 1976; Major 1987: outcome values, comparison referents, justifications). Gender ideology (wife's traditionalism) is treated as a moderator and, in the author's words, as a proxy for the wife's comparative referent. Hypothesis 1: ideology x actual split predicts perceived fairness. Hypothesis 2: ideology x perceived fairness predicts marital quality. No preregistration. There is no mediation analysis: the marital-quality equations contain both the split and perceived fairness, but no model without perceived fairness is reported, so no total effect of the split is shown. Coefficients are unstandardised. The perceived-fairness measure is a single NSFH item; the split is wife's share of total household hours (wife, husband, children, others) from self-reported hours on nine tasks.

## Sample
NSFH wave 1, national probability sample of 13,017 adults interviewed 1987-88; 4,960 married couples (of 5,640) in which both spouses responded and gave codable answers to all items analysed. Couple weights applied. The tables report unweighted N = 4,960 for all models. Analyses are of wives. The text does not say whether the hours of each task are the wife's report, the husband's report or an average; the study reports total hours "by wife" and "by husband" without saying whose report. Hours over 100 per week in any activity were recoded to 100. Wives averaged about 37 hours per week of chores and husbands about 16; wives did about 64 percent of household labour. Mean perceived fairness is -0.14 on a -1 to +1 scale (Table 1).

## Key results
- C1: The full fairness model (13 predictors including the wife's share, hours of both spouses, ideology and the ideology x share interaction) has adjusted R2 = .044. The split is at most a part of this, so the actual split explains little of the variation (upper bound; the share alone is not separated out, and wives only).
- C2 and C3: There is no direct measure of comparison referents or accepted justifications. Ideology is used as a proxy for both. Its main effect on perceived fairness is -0.0014 (not significant) but it significantly moderates the split's effect (interaction 0.004*): the more traditional the wife, the weaker the link between the split and perceived fairness. This is not a test of referents or justifications as such. Because ideology is uncentered (mean 100), the "main effect" is evaluated at ideology = 0 and is not interpretable on its own.
- C4: In the marital-happiness equation, perceived fairness has coefficient 1.463* and the wife's share of domestic labour -0.046 (not significant); in the stability equation 0.941* and 0.039 (not significant). The fairness coefficients are conditional on ideology = 0 (interaction -0.005 for happiness, -0.006* for stability), so at the mean ideology of 100 the fairness slope is roughly 0.96 for happiness. Fairness and share are on different scales (-1 to +1 and 0 to 1), so unstandardised coefficients are not strictly comparable. The pattern is consistent with C4, but there is no formal comparison of the two and the author only reports "better predictors" from other studies (Blair 1993; Perry-Jenkins & Folk 1994) for marital conflict.
- C5: Consistent in pattern with full mediation (share has no significant coefficient once fairness is in the happiness and stability equations) and the author states the theory, but no mediation model, indirect effect or total effect is reported. Cross-sectional data, wives only.
- C8: Perceived fairness is measured with one NSFH item about household chores (five response categories). One study's instance of the single-item practice; the paper does not survey other studies' measures.
- C9: Fairness is coded -1 (very unfair to me) to +1 (very unfair to her/him) and entered linearly, with no category dummies or non-linear term, so there is no comparison of over-benefited wives with equitable ones. The linear positive coefficients (1.463, 0.941) imply higher happiness and stability toward the "unfair to her" end, the opposite of an over-benefit penalty, but this is forced by the linear form and is not tested. Wives only; the author doubts that the model suits husbands.
- Not addressed: C6 (spouses' reported shares are never summed or compared; the only related passage is that many respondents reported more than 168 hours per week across domestic and market work, which the author attributes to invalid self-reports or concurrent tasks) and C7 (tasks are summed into total hours; which tasks are done is not related to fairness).

## Limitations
Author's own: the model is tested on wives only, and the author doubts that relative deprivation applies to husbands; the happiness interaction is not significant at the conventional level (significant only one-tailed at .07), and support rests on the stability equation and a multivariate Wilks test (lambda .998, F = 4.60, p = .011); the author notes a conflict with Blair and Johnson (1992), who found little role for ideology in a smaller sample of 778. Mine: cross-sectional, single-item outcome for fairness, with a skewed distribution (mean -0.14, SD 0.32); the one-item fairness measure and the happiness measure come from the same respondent, so shared-method variance and reverse causation (unhappy wives judging the split unfair) are not addressed; no mediation analysis, so any mediation claim is an inference from coefficient patterns; unstandardised coefficients on unlike scales; ideology is uncentered, so the reported main effects are at ideology = 0; multiple outcomes and tests without correction; the split depends on self-reported hours with an ad hoc cap at 100 hours; ideology is only a proxy for referents and justifications, which were not measured, so the C2 and C3 estimand is not what was estimated. The paper was read from a scan: the text layer of a JSTOR scan with OCR errors (for example "Faimess", "4367*" for the constant), and the cells of Table 2 are in scrambled column order, so the columns of Table 2 were taken from the row reconstruction (fairness, happiness, stability) and from the text's description of which coefficients are significant.
