# Mikula 2009: Roots and correlates of perceived injustice in the division of family work

## Design
Cross-sectional, correlational, paper-and-pencil survey; each partner of a dual-earner couple completed the questionnaire independently. Four correlated outcomes (each partner's 1-6 justice rating of domestic work and of child care) modelled in one dyadic structural equation model (Mplus), predicted by (a) social-comparison frequencies and outcomes (woman vs man, woman vs other women, man vs other men) and their frequency x outcome interactions, (b) gratification and appreciation by the partner, (c) "justifications" operationalised as each partner's weekly professional-work hours and SES, with (d) the actual division of family work and both partners' hours entered as control variables. Estimand: conditional (adjusted) associations (standardised betas) of entitlement predictors with justice ratings, net of the division. Equality constraints across country, sex and domain were tested and a final constrained model fitted. Alpha set at p < .01. Not preregistered. No satisfaction outcome, no longitudinal data, no causal identification; the authors say the design "does not permit any conclusions about the causal direction". The "division" controls are self-reported hours converted to percentages, from the same respondent as the justice rating (see Limitations). Relationship satisfaction was not measured as an analysed variable.

## Sample
632 dual-earner couples (Austria 213, Germany 216, Switzerland 203), convenience samples, no sampling frame; both partners employed at least 15 hr/week, at least one preschool child and none older than 12. Women mean 35, men 37 years; 85% married; 56.6% of women and 59.7% of men with a university degree. Analyses on n up to 630 per sex (sample sizes vary 613-632 across variables). No attrition (single wave). Data collected in the FamWork project (year of collection not stated in the text).

## Key results
- Variance explained (final model): entitlement predictors 29% (women, domestic work), 33% (women, child care), 21% (men, domestic work), 24% (men, child care). Control variables (division and hours) explained a further 8% and 10% for women's domestic work and child care justice, and no significant share for men. Whether the control block was entered first or last is not stated; the figures are reported as non-overlapping.
- Zero-order correlations of the division variables with justice are larger than the adjusted estimates: for women, r ranges -.14 to -.47 across DW/CC (e.g. CC-w with women's CC justice -.47); for men -.13 to -.35 (Table 1).
- Division in the final model (women only): woman's share of DW/CC beta = -.183 (t = -4.447, p < .001); partner's workload beta = .098; woman's own workload not predictive. For men, no control variable significant (division beta = .017).
- Referents (C2): outcome of the man-vs-other-men comparison beta = .181 (t = 7.585); frequency x outcome for man-vs-other-men beta = .070; woman-vs-other-women outcome beta = -.041 (n.s.); frequencies of both referential comparisons about zero (.004, .003). Relational (woman vs man) frequency beta = -.252, outcome beta = -.090. All net of division. Effects constrained equal for women and men.
- Justifications (C3): woman's professional workload beta = -.087 (t = -3.658) and woman's SES beta = -.080 (t = -3.360) significant; man's workload (-.026) and man's SES (n.s.) not. Higher woman's SES and longer woman's hours predict LESS perceived justice.
- Appreciation by partner beta = .159 (t = 8.041); gratification n.s.
- Measurement (C8): justice is a single 6-point self-report item per domain.
- Not examined: relationship satisfaction, over-benefit/under-benefit, mediation, task type within a domain (C4, C5, C7 largely, C9). The introduction asserts, with citations, that perceived justice relates to relationship satisfaction, and Mikula, Riederer & Bodi (2008, reference title) concerns a mediating role of perceived justice; neither is tested here.
- C6: the table gives each partner's estimate of the woman's share; own-claim sums are not reported (see extract E11 for what the printed means imply).

## Limitations
Authors' own: convenience samples, homogeneous and above-average education and SES, relatively high relationship satisfaction of participating couples; limited number of variables per construct; correlational cross-sectional design, with bidirectional links plausible; gratification measure "rather crude".
Mine: (1) Predictors and outcome come from the same respondent at one time, so shared-method variance and reverse causation (people who feel unfairly treated report more frequent comparison, less appreciation) inflate the entitlement-block R2 relative to the division block. (2) The "actual split" is each respondent's own report of hours, not an independent measure; its R2 (8-10%) is therefore a self-report-based, adjusted figure, and any variance shared with the comparison outcomes (the WM comparison outcome is itself a perceived-split item, and correlates .73 with the woman's share, Table 2) is credited to the entitlement block or dropped. The "small share" for the split is thus partly a function of how the blocks were partitioned. (3) "Justifications" are proxies (hours, SES), not the justifications a partner accepts; the direction found for women's SES is a legitimacy reading imposed by the authors. (4) Dyadic model with equality constraints across sexes and domains: pooled coefficients, so claims of equal effects rest on non-significant modification indices at p < .01. (5) Final model fit is moderate (CFI .916). (6) Single-item justice measure. (7) The mapping of Table 1's "m"/"w" division rows to respondents is ambiguous (see E11).
