# Coltrane 2000: Research on household labor, modeling and measuring the social embeddedness of routine family work (review)

## Design
REVIEW, not a primary study. A narrative (non-systematic, no search protocol, no inclusion criteria, no quality appraisal) review of "more than 200" articles and books on household labor published 1989 to 1999, which the author calls "a cross-section of influential social science works". No estimand, no pooled effect sizes, no sample of its own, no preregistration. Every empirical statement about fairness, satisfaction or measurement is the reviewer's summary of other studies (mostly US cross-sectional survey work, e.g. NSFH, plus some small-sample and qualitative work). The fairness section (pp.16-18) and outcome section (p.18) are the parts that bear on this question. Direction and strength of associations are asserted in prose; no coefficients, R-squared values, intervals or adjusted-versus-unadjusted comparisons are reported, and the review mostly does not say which covariates the cited studies held constant.

## Sample
None of its own. More than 200 works, 1989-1999, with the review's emphasis on US married or cohabiting couples and, within fairness, mostly on wives.

## Key results
What the review says, with no numbers except where noted:
- Perceived fairness and the actual split (C1): past studies "found little relation" between the actual split and perceived fairness, because unequal splits are accepted as normal (citing Lennon and Rosenfeld 1994: men see the split as fair at a 36% contribution, women at 66%). The review then says that 1990s studies found variation in fairness explained by participation, and that "the single most important predictor of a wife's fairness evaluation is what portion of the routine housework her husband contributes". No variance explained is reported, so "only a small part" is neither confirmed nor refuted.
- Referents and justifications (C2, C3): reports the distributive-justice framework (Thompson 1991, Major) and, from Hawkins et al. 1995, Coltrane 1990 and Gager 1998, that cross-gender referents go with less perceived fairness and same-gender referents with more; housekeeping standards act as a justification. These are reported as associations; the review does not say whether they hold after adjusting for the actual split.
- Fairness versus split as predictors of satisfaction (C4): no head-to-head comparison. Both are reported as associated with marital satisfaction, mainly for women; for men the review says both are typically unrelated.
- Mediation (C5): says other studies "consider fairness as a mediating, or intervening, variable" and lists eight; it does not report whether the mediation was found or how large it was. It does say marital conflict rises "only when women perceive the division of labor to be unfair".
- Over-claiming (C6): says both sexes overestimate their own contributions in direct-question surveys (men possibly more) and that many studies average the spouses' reports. The review does not state that the two reports sum to more than 100%.
- Task type (C7): reports that men's low contribution to routine cooking, cleaning and washing is what matters, and that doing more occasional tasks raises perceived fairness (DeMaris and Longmore 1996). No test against time spent is described.
- Measurement (C8): does not say fairness is mainly measured with a single item. It notes one NSFH item "lacks unidimensionality and is difficult to interpret" and lists new multi-item instruments (Hawkins et al. 1998).
- Over-benefit (C9): equity theory is stated as predicting discomfort among over-benefitted partners, then challenged by evidence that men who do little see the split as fair; for men, split and fairness are typically unrelated to satisfaction.

## Limitations
Authors' own: the review is selective ("do not exhaust research on the topic"), notes that "problems associated with frequent reanalysis of the same data also emerged" (NSFH), that cross-sectional correlations "can reflect spurious associations", that the fairness literature has measurement problems, and that findings on fairness "vary considerably".
Mine: (1) a secondary source: no claim here can be stronger than the cited studies, and none of them can be checked from this text. (2) No effect sizes or adjustment sets are given, so the review cannot support or refute claims worded as "after the actual split is accounted for" (C2, C3), "only a small part" (C1), "more strongly" (C4), "mediated" (C5), or "beyond time" (C7). (3) Most fairness findings reported are about wives; findings about men are summarised as null or inconsistent, which matters for C9 and C4. (4) Cross-sectional dominance and the NSFH fairness item mean fairness, split and satisfaction may share reporting bias. (5) Narrative selection: no search or inclusion rule, so absence of a topic (for example any direct test of single-item measurement, or any decomposition of variance) is not evidence of absence.

## Follow up
Primary studies the review cites that the question needs, as printed:
- Hawkins, A. J., Marshall, C. M., & Meiners, K. M. (1995). Exploring wives' sense of fairness about family work: An initial test of the distributive-justice framework. Journal of Family Issues, 16, 693-721. (referents, justifications)
- Gager, C. T. (1998). The role of valued outcomes, justifications, and comparison referents in perceptions of fairness among dual-earner couples. Journal of Family Issues, 19, 622-649. (referents, justifications)
- Thompson, L. (1991). Family work: Women's sense of fairness. Journal of Family Issues, 12, 181-196.
- Smith, H. L., Gager, C. T., & Morgan, S. P. (1998). Identifying underlying dimensions in spouses' evaluations of fairness in the division of household labor. Social Science Research, 27, 305-327. (C8: measurement)
- Hawkins, A. J., Marshall, C. M., & Allen, S. M. (1998). The orientation toward domestic labor questionnaire. Journal of Family Psychology, 12, 244-258. (C8)
- Lennon, M. C., & Rosenfield, S. (1994). Relative fairness and the division of housework: The importance of options. American Journal of Sociology, 100, 506-531. (C1, C5, C9)
- DeMaris, A., & Longmore, M. A. (1996). Ideology, power, and equity: Testing competing explanations for the perception of fairness in household labor. Social Forces, 74, 1043-1071. (C7)
- Greenstein, T. N. (1996b). Gender ideology and perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor: Effects on marital quality. Social Forces, 74, 1029-1042. (C4, C5)
- Wilkie, J. R., Ferree, M. M., & Ratcliff, K. S. (1998). Gender and fairness: Marital satisfaction in two-earner couples. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 60, 577-594. (C4, C5)
- Press, J. E., & Townsley, E. (1998). Wives' and husbands' housework reporting: Gender, class, and social desirability. Gender & Society, 12, 188-218. (C6)
- Marini, M. M., & Shelton, B. A. (1993). Measuring household work: Recent experience in the United States. Social Science Research, 22, 361-382. (C6)
- Kluwer, Heesink, & Van de Vliert (1996), Perry-Jenkins & Folk (1994), Pin~a & Bengtson (1993), Robinson & Spitze (1992), Suitor (1991), Ward (1993), Dancer & Gilbert (1993): the studies the review lists as treating fairness as mediator (C5); see reference list on pp.20-26 of the text.
No supplement is involved. No figures or tables are in the paper.
