# Lavee & Katz 2002: Division of labor, perceived fairness and marital quality (gender ideology)

## Design
Cross-sectional interview survey, observational, no randomisation and no preregistration. Structural equation models (LISREL 8, weighted least squares on a polyserial/asymptotic covariance matrix) fitted separately for men and for women, then a multigroup nested-model comparison for women across three ethnic-religious groups (Moslem Arabs, Christian Arabs, Jews) treated as natural proxies for traditional, transitional and egalitarian gender ideology. Mediation is tested with the Baron and Kenny (1986) steps: segregation to marital quality without fairness, then the model with fairness as mediator; no indirect-effect estimate or interval is reported.

The "actual split" is a respondent-reported degree of segregation, not a share: 15 tasks, each checked as done by wife only, mainly wife, both equally, mainly husband, or husband only, recoded 0 (shared) to 2 (done by one spouse only). Direction of imbalance is therefore discarded (wife-only and husband-only both score 2), so over-benefit and under-benefit cannot be told apart in the split measure. Segregation is a latent variable with three indicators (routine housework, child-care tasks, other household tasks), alphas .87, .69, .70. Perceived fairness is one item, recoded to three categories (very unfair to me, unfair to me, fair), entered as a single indicator with its error variance fixed at .20 from other studies' reliability estimates. Marital quality is the 10-item short ENRICH with the division-of-labor item removed, alpha .84, modelled with two random split halves (r = .76). Estimand: standardized structural paths among the three latent variables.

## Sample
1,124 married respondents with at least one child at home: 450 Moslem Arabs (225 men, 225 women), 214 Christian Arabs (106 men, 108 women), 460 Jews (228 men, 232 women). Area probability sampling from Central Bureau of Statistics sampling map across Arab and Jewish communities in Israel; home interviews by research assistants. Ages: men 22 to 59, women 19 to 56. Jewish women were somewhat older and more often employed full time (63.4% vs 42% Christian and 18% Moslem). The text does not say whether men and women are spouses of the same couples (the group sizes are not equal, which suggests mostly not), and no attrition or non-response figures are given. Date of fieldwork not stated.

## Key results
- Total (unmediated) effect of segregation on marital quality: men gamma = -.17 (t = -2.48), women gamma = -.30 (t = -5.56) (p.8).
- Mediated model, women (Figure 1, p.9): segregation to fairness -.36, fairness to marital quality .45, direct segregation to marital quality -.13 (dashed, not significant). Fit: chi2(7) = 6.89, AGFI .99, RMSEA .02. Authors conclude mediation for women.
- Mediated model, men (Figure 1): segregation to fairness -.25 (dashed, not significant), fairness to marital quality .51, direct path -.04 (not significant). No mediation for men; fairness still predicts marital quality.
- By group, women only (Table 3, common metric standardized): segregation to fairness -.36 Moslem (ns), -.38 Christian (p < .01), -.39 Jewish (p < .01); segregation to marital quality -.04, -.10, -.46 (only Jewish p < .01); fairness to marital quality .38, .76, .03 (only Christian p < .01). So fairness mediates for Christian women only; for Jewish women segregation has a direct effect and fairness is unrelated to marital quality; for Moslem women neither segregation path is significant. Structural invariance across groups was rejected (delta chi2(6) = 35.12, p < .01).
- Variance explained in fairness by the split is not reported. A standardized path of about -.36 implies roughly 13% of latent fairness variance for women, and -.25 about 6% for men (my arithmetic, from the figure; the paths are on a latent fairness variable whose error is fixed by assumption).
- Fairness distribution (Table 1B): only 2.1% of women and 3.9% of men judged the division unfair to their spouse; Christian women were least likely to say "fair" (64.2%).
- Nothing in the paper measures comparison referents or accepted justifications directly (C2, C3), chore content beyond three subscales or time spent (C7), or partners' summed self-reported shares (C6). Group membership stands in for ideology and, in the authors' interpretation, for referents.

## Limitations
Authors': fairness and the egalitarian attitude item are single items "which may have somewhat limited validity"; groups are internally diverse; the sample is Israeli.
Mine: cross-sectional, so the order fairness to marital quality cannot be separated from marital quality colouring fairness reports, and the Baron and Kenny steps carry the usual sequential-ignorability assumptions with no covariates (education, employment, age, hours worked differ strongly by group and are not controlled). The mediator and outcome are both self-reported by the same person. The fairness error variance is fixed rather than estimated, and the three-category recode is data-driven (chosen after a sensitivity analysis), so the path sizes depend on those choices. Segregation ignores direction and hours, so "actual split" is a reported, non-directional index; it cannot address over-benefit (C9) except through the self-perceived fairness category. Mediation inference for the group comparison rests on significance of separate paths with small Christian cells (106 men, 108 women), with no formal test of the indirect effect. The multigroup test shows structural paths differ but does not isolate which. Group is a proxy for ideology, referents and justification, so the discussion of referents is untested interpretation. Fairness mediation is estimated within each group's own metric, and "Divison" in the title is a typo in the source.
