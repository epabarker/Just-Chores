# Braun 2008: Perceived equity in the gendered division of household labor

## Design
Cross-sectional, observational, comparative multilevel logistic regression (individuals nested in 25 countries, Stata 10, full maximum likelihood), ISSP 2002 "Family and Changing Gender Roles" module. Not randomised, not preregistered. Outcome: a single self-report item on whether the woman does her fair share of the housework, dichotomised (1 = roughly fair share or less than fair share; 0 = a bit or much more than fair share). Exposure ("actual inequality"): the woman's own report of her weekly housework hours minus her report of her partner's, divided by the sum (range -1 to 1). Models: 1 = inequality only; 2 = plus individual main effects (absolute hours, household aid, paid-work hours, resource dependence, gender ideology, controls); 3 = plus inequality x (paid hours, dependence, gender ideology); 4 = plus country gender-wage ratio, its interaction with inequality, and the country mean of inequality. Estimand: conditional (log-odds) association of each predictor with a woman reporting equity, not a variance share. No R-squared or pseudo-R-squared is reported; fit is compared by likelihood-ratio tests only. Analysis population: women aged 18 to 65 who are married or cohabiting.

## Sample
N = 8,556 women in 25 countries (country n from 171 in Portugal to 662 in France). Response rates 20.6% (France) to 82.8% (Mexico), four countries by substitution. Survey modes: 6 mail, 14 personal interview, 5 self-completion with interviewer present. Nationally representative samples of private households. Only women, so partner-reported data do not exist. Missing-data handling is not described.

## Key results
- C1: No variance-explained figure for the split. The paper says only that in Model 1 the odds ratio for inequality is .107 (z 29.9), that "if the woman does all the household work, the chance for her to perceive equity is reduced by nearly 90% compared to equal participation", and that in Model 2 the split "remains by a wide margin the largest (as measured by the z statistic)". On the logistic scale the country-level share of variance is 5.1% in the empty model. These are conditional odds and z statistics, so they neither support nor contradict a small R-squared; they do show a strong association with a dichotomised outcome. The split here is a woman's own report of both partners' hours, so it can share reporter bias with the outcome.
- C2: The only referent-type variable is the country mean of the inequality (odds ratio 7.334, z 2.0 in Model 4, net of the individual's own inequality). It is an ecological proxy for a referent that the authors infer ("the comparison referent is likely to be more negative"); no individual's referent is measured. The estimate is imprecise (z about 2, 25 country units).
- C3: The three "legitimizing principles" are structural or attitudinal proxies (paid hours, relative earnings, a five-item gender-ideology scale), not a measure of what the woman accepts as justification. Gender ideology main effect: odds ratio .959 (z 1.3, ns) in Model 2, 1.272 (z 4.9) in Model 3, 1.213 (z 3.8) in Model 4. The interaction with the split is the strongest: .546 (z 7.4) in Model 3, .593 (z 6.3) in Model 4, meaning egalitarian women react more strongly to inequality. Interactions with paid hours (.989 / .991) and dependence (1.205 / 1.189) are significant; main effects of paid hours and dependence become null once interactions enter (Model 3: .999, z 0.4; .989, z 0.4).
- C6: Reports in the whole ISSP data set (all respondents with a partner, not matched couples): men give their own contribution as 9.0 hours and their partner's as 21.3; women give their own as 21.4 and their partner's as 7.5. By my arithmetic (not the paper's), women's own share is 21.4/28.9 = 74% and men's own share is 9.0/30.3 = 30%, which sum to about 104%; the over-claim sits with men (9.0 vs 7.5 hours attributed to the man), while women's self-report (21.4) matches men's report of them (21.3). Different people, mean hours, no couple-level sums.
- C7: A model using a count of female-typed tasks the man does (laundry, cleaning, meals, shopping, coded always / usually / equal / third person), in place of the hours measure, is said to give fundamentally the same results for the central variables, but "not presented here". No test of chores beyond time.
- C8: Perceived equity is measured by one item with five response options, then dichotomised. The authors note previous analyses used such a measure as a quantitative variable or dichotomised. Only one study, so it shows the practice rather than its prevalence.
- C4, C5, C9: not measured; relationship satisfaction does not appear. The Discussion only speculates that low tolerance of inequality "could consequently influence the quality of their marriage and its stability".

## Limitations
Authors': both division measures are the woman's subjective estimates, so "it cannot be excluded that in particular those women who perceive inequity have corrected their estimate for their spouses' share in household work somewhat downward"; no time-budget data; few countries for macro-level variables; other proposed explanatory variables unavailable.
Mine: cross-sectional, so no temporal order among the split, the legitimizing variables and fairness. The outcome lumps "roughly fair" with "less than fair share" (4.1% of the sample), and is a single item. Dichotomisation discards the 28.2% / 27.2% much-more / a-bit-more split. Model 3 and 4 "main effects" are conditional on zero values of the interacting variables (e.g., zero gender ideology, outside the 1 to 5 scale), so the authors' remark that "the causal structure of the model has changed completely" reads conditional coefficients causally; the shrinking of the main effect of inequality (OR .099 to .340 to 2.379, ns) is mostly an artefact of centring and the interaction terms. No R-squared. Women only; the 20.6% response rate in France. The legitimizing principles are assumed, not measured, as justifications; gender ideology and dependence are also determinants of the split itself, so they are confounded with it. Minus signs are lost in the text layer (the inequality range reads "1 to 1"; z ratios are unsigned); odds ratios below 1 are therefore read by the direction the authors describe.

## Follow up
Papers this one cites that the question needs (as printed):
- Mikula, G. (1998). Division of household labor and perceived justice: A growing field of research. Social Justice Research, 11, 215 - 241. (review: only 20% to 30% of women regard the split as unjust)
- Freudenthaler, H. H., & Mikula, G. (1998). From unfulfilled wants to the experience of injustice: Women's sense of injustice regarding the lopsided division of household labor. Social Justice Research, 11, 289 - 312. (comparison referents)
- Lennon, M. C., & Rosenfield, S. (1994). Relative fairness and the division of housework: The importance of options. American Journal of Sociology, 100, 506 - 531.
- DeMaris, A., & Longmore, M. (1996). Ideology, power, and equity. Social Forces, 74, 1043 - 1071.
- Blair, L. S., & Johnson, M. P. (1992). Wives' perception of the fairness of the division of household labor. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 54, 570 - 581.
- Greenstein, T. N. (1996). Gender ideology and the perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor: Effects on marital quality. Social Forces, 74, 1029 - 1042. (satisfaction link, C4/C5)
- Frisco, M. L., & Williams, K. (2003). Perceived housework equity, marital happiness, and divorce in dual-earner households. Journal of Family Issues, 24, 51 - 73. (C4/C5/C9)
- Lavee, Y., & Katz, R. (2002). Division of labor, perceived fairness, and marital quality: The moderating effect of gender ideology. Journal of Marriage and Family, 64, 27 - 39.
- Klumb, P., Hoppmann, C., & Staats, M. (2006). Division of labor in German dual-earner families: Testing equity theoretical hypotheses. Journal of Marriage and Family, 68, 870 - 882.
- The results for the female-typed-task variant (C7) are "not presented here" and are available only from the authors.
