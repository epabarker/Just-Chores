# DeMaris et al. 2016: Equity Dynamics in the Perceived Fairness of Infant Care

## Design
Longitudinal observational panel of married couples across the transition to first parenthood (waves 2-4 used as outcome waves). The outcome is a binary indicator that infant care is fair to the wife, modelled with a population-averaged logistic regression (GEE) in a multilevel dyadic-discrepancy framework, pooling spouses and waves (6 records per couple). Predictors are husbands' relative contributions (logged ratios) to infant care, housework, paid labour and the sexual relationship, plus moderators and controls. Not randomised, not preregistered; some predictors are lagged one wave, but infant-care contributions, fussiness and coparenting are contemporaneous with the outcome. The domain is infant care, not household chores in general. The estimand is the association of each predictor with the odds (geometric mean of his and her odds) that infant care is perceived as fair to the wife.

## Sample
178 married couples expecting a first biological child, Midwestern US city and surrounding areas, recruited via childbirth classes, notices, word of mouth and direct mail (convenience sample). Four waves (third trimester, then 4, 7 and 13 months later). 169 couples completed three waves and 164 (92%) all four. Only respondents with valid outcome data analysed; at most 2% missing on any predictor; key analyses repeated with multiple imputation (10 datasets).

## Key results
- Perceived fairness is a binary indicator built from nine infant-care tasks, each rated on a 1-5 "fair share" item per task. It is multi-item, not single-item, and the authors contrast it with single-item measures in other studies (E3).
- The final model (Model 4, all predictors including the contribution ratios, moderators, controls and two interactions) accounts for about 19% of the variation in fairness perceptions (E1). The paper does not say which R-squared-type statistic this is, and does not report what the infant-care contribution ratio alone explains. 19% is an upper bound on what the whole predictor set explains, so it is compatible with, but does not isolate, a small share for the actual split.
- The authors state that fathers' relative contributions to infant care had the strongest effect of any factor in the models (E2). The Table 2 coefficient is not in the text file; the text gives the Model 4 average effect of the infant-care ratio as .611 (+ .917 x male child) on the log-odds scale.
- No measure of relationship satisfaction as an outcome, no comparison-referent measure, no measure of accepted justifications, no self-report share-sum (over-claiming) analysis, no chore-type analysis, and no over-benefit versus equitable comparison on satisfaction. Those claims (C2 to C7, C9) are not addressed. Perceived relative advantage (overbenefit) was a control with non-monotonic spline terms nonsignificant; it predicts fairness, not satisfaction.

## Limitations
Authors' own: convenience sample of well-adjusted, short-duration marriages; married couples only; some predictors of questionable causal priority (sex-role traditionalism measured at wave 4, coparenting solidarity and infant fussiness not lagged); repeated measurement may sensitise couples to fairness.
Reader's: the outcome is dichotomised (fair to wife = no task where she reports doing more than her fair share, no task where he reports doing less), so it conflates fairness with absence of any wife-overburden and cannot register over-benefit of the wife; the authors report that several effects significant under the binary outcome were not significant with the continuous version. Contribution to infant care is the average of both spouses' reports (frequency, not time), so the "actual split" is itself self-reported. Variance explained is not decomposed by predictor. Alpha reliabilities of the relative-advantage scales are low (.54, .62). Binary outcome with GEE gives population-averaged, not couple-specific, effects.
