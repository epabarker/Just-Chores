# Shelton & John 1996: The Division of Household Labor (review)

## Design
Annual Review of Sociology chapter (narrative, non-systematic review; no search strategy, inclusion criteria, effect sizes or pooled estimates). Covers measurement of housework, explanations of the division, and consequences. Perceived fairness gets one short subsection ("Perceptions of Housework", about 1.5 pages, p.15-17) and a paragraph inside the satisfaction section. Every finding relevant to the claims is another paper's finding, reported without numbers, designs or intervals. No passage here is original evidence.

## Sample
None. The studies cited are US/Canadian marital samples of the late 1980s to mid 1990s (for example the 1988 NSFH is named as the data set for the conflict studies). Sample sizes, response rates and designs of the cited studies are not given.

## Key results
All are the reviewers' summaries of other papers, and none give a magnitude.
- C2, C3: the review presents Thompson (1991) as arguing that a wife sees an unequal split as unfair only if she wants more from her husband, compares him with someone who does more, and sees no justification. The comparison-referent and justification claims are stated as an argument and cited to Thompson (1991), Major (1994), LaRossa & LaRossa (1981) and McKee (1982). The review does not say they were tested with the actual split held constant. It closes the subsection by saying ideological factors "may partially account for the inconsistent findings", so this is not a settled result.
- C1: the review says findings on the division-fairness link "are inconsistent" and gives no variance explained. It neither supports nor contradicts "explains only a small part".
- C4, C5: the review reports (from Pina & Bengston 1993) mediation of the division-satisfaction effect by perceived fairness, and says wives' satisfaction is higher when they feel the husband does a fair share while few studies find a consistent division-satisfaction relation. For men it reports (Robinson & Spitze 1992) the reverse pattern: housework time matters, fairness perceptions do not. No comparative effect sizes are given.
- C6: only the general statement that respondents overestimate their own housework time and underestimate others' (Marini & Shelton 1993). The sum of partners' shares exceeding 100% is not reported, and no share is reported.
- C7: Blair & Johnson (1992), Lennon & Rosenfield (1994) and John et al (1995) are reported as finding that men's time or proportional share on female-typed tasks predicts perceived fairness. Whether task type adds anything beyond total time is not reported.
- C8: the review's measurement section covers how housework time and shares are measured, not how fairness is measured. It does not say a single item dominates. No extract is recorded for C8.
- C9: Yogev & Brett (1985) are reported as finding men's satisfaction highest at their own "fair share" (consistent with over-benefit lowering satisfaction), while Robinson & Spitze (1992) are reported as finding men happier when they do less housework (the opposite for an over-benefited man). The review does not classify respondents as over-benefited, under-benefited or equitable.

## Limitations
Authors' own: they say the literature is "cumulative in some respects, but not as much as it should be", that measurement varies between studies, and that the fairness findings are inconsistent. Mine: this is secondary reporting, a narrative review with no study-selection method, no quality appraisal and no effect sizes, so it cannot support any claim about size ("only a small part", "more strongly") or about adjusted associations. Most of the cited work is cross-sectional, so the causal ordering behind the mediation claim (C5) is unaddressed here. The review's "women" and "husbands" framing means evidence for same-sex couples and non-married partners is absent. Cited findings date from 1996 or earlier. The text was extracted from a JSTOR PDF with pymupdf and was read as plain text, not from a scan.
