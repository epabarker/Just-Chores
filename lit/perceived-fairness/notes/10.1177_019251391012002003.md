# Thompson 1991: Family work, women's sense of fairness

## Design
Narrative, conceptual review in the Journal of Family Issues (Vol. 12, No. 2, 181-196). No data are collected or analysed and no estimand is defined. Thompson organises prior (mostly qualitative and interview-based) work on the division of family labour around Major's (1987) three factors for a sense of fairness: outcome values, comparison referents and justifications. Her propositions about what would raise or lower women's sense of unfairness are theoretical suggestions ("would have a stronger sense of ... if"), not tested effects. Fairness is defined broadly as "a judgment that one is unjustly treated". The paper is about women only, and about the sense of fairness of the division of family work as the outcome of interest. Relationship satisfaction is not an outcome.

## Sample
None. The review draws on about 35 cited works, chiefly Hochschild (1989), Komter (1989), Luxton (1980), Haavind (1984), Hood (1983), DeVault (1987), Ferree (1988, conference paper) and Major (1987), and cites a few survey findings (Benin & Agnostinelli 1988; Berk 1985; Pleck 1985; Yogev 1981) as secondary reports. No search strategy, inclusion criteria or sample sizes are given.

## Key results
- The paper states, as a secondary report, that most women do more than two thirds of family work but fewer than a third of these wives feel it is unfair; it also states that "Researchers have not had much luck predicting women's sense of fairness about family work" (citing Ferree 1988). This is a statement about the mismatch between the actual split and perceived fairness, not a variance-explained figure. It is consistent with C1 in direction but gives no effect size.
- Comparison referents: the paper argues, from qualitative studies, that within-gender referents (the "superwoman" ideal; comparing husbands with other men) are associated with lower felt entitlement and that between-gender comparisons are associated with a stronger sense of injustice. This is a theoretical claim resting on interview studies (Hochschild, Luxton, Ferree); no analysis adjusts for the actual split. It bears on C2 as a hypothesis, not as a test.
- Justifications: the paper argues, from Komter, Hochschild, Haavind and LaRossa & LaRossa, that wives who accept gendered justifications for husbands' small contribution (more time, talent, pleasure, greater need for clean house, joint decision-making) sense less injustice. Again a theoretical proposition from qualitative work; no control for the actual split. It bears on C3 as a hypothesis.
- Which chores: the paper reports, from Crosby (1982), that women feel resentful about the amount of housework they do if they do chores they dislike, and argues that time and task counts do not capture valued outcomes. This weakly supports the idea behind C7 (task type matters beyond time), via secondary reporting and without a quantitative test.
- The paper says the assumed scenario in which unfair splits make women miserable in their marriages has largely failed to find support in research (citing Pleck 1985 and others). This touches C5 and C4 only obliquely and gives no mediation or association estimate.
- Nothing in the text bears on C6 (over-claiming sums), C8 (measurement of perceived fairness) or C9 (over-benefit and satisfaction).

## Limitations
Authors' own: only one rendition of distributive justice is used; the focus is on women, and the author says both women's and men's sense of fairness should be studied; power is acknowledged as central but not modelled. Mine: it is a non-systematic narrative review, so there is no way to judge selection of the cited work; most cited evidence is small-sample qualitative and several sources are books or an unpublished conference paper (Ferree 1988); no effect sizes, intervals or adjustment sets are given, so none of the claims about referents or justifications is a conditional association net of the actual split; causal language ("would have a stronger sense") is hypothetical. The paper was read from a scan (OCR version `unknown-ocr`), so characters may be misread; quotes are copied as the OCR text has them.
