# Hawkins 1995: Exploring wives' sense of fairness about family work (distributive justice framework)

## Design
Cross-sectional mail survey, wives only, 1993. Four covariance-structure (LISREL VII) models with latent variables: three domain models (valued outcomes, comparison referents, justifications), then a combined model of the constructs that were significant (directly or indirectly) in those three. The outcome is the wife's sense of fairness of family work (housework plus child care; two items, 7-point, alpha .84). "Division of Family Work" is a wife-reported global perception (three items, 5-point, "who does more", alpha .80), entered in every model as a mediator between the cognitions and fairness. The authors say they chose not to use the time estimates available in the data. No randomisation, no preregistration, no causal identification beyond the theory-assigned ordering; the authors state that direction of effect "is a theoretical issue, not a statistical one". Demographic variables (age, employment hours, education, years married, children, race, religion) had no significant bivariate association with fairness and were left out of all models.

Relevance to the claims: Division is the perceived split, not the actual split, so C1 to C3 are tested only for a perception-based proxy. The paper has no relationship-satisfaction measure and no husband data, so it does not bear on C4, C5, C6 or C9. It does not test chore type against time (C7) beyond combining housework and child care in one measure.

## Sample
234 dual-earner wives (employed 15 or more hours a week; 159 full time) out of 405 responding wives, from a random sample of 1,500 intact-marriage households with an employed husband and at least one child at home, five western US metropolitan areas, 1993. Household response rate 31 percent (wives 28 percent). 91 percent Anglo-American; mean age 39.6; wives report 37.4 hours a week of family work (text: husbands 20). Table 1: fairness M = 4.6, SD = 1.44; Division M = 4.2, SD = 0.73. Demographics were compared with a 1990 Public Use Microdata sample of comparable wives and judged similar.

## Key results
- Fit of all four models was reported adequate (chi2/df 1.47, 1.75, 2.62, 1.71; GFI .91 to .96).
- Valued outcomes model: Division is significantly related to fairness (the more wives do relative to husbands, the less fair); Feeling Appreciated has the stronger direct effect (coefficient .664). The Division-to-Fairness coefficients are printed only in the figures, which the text does not carry; the products given in the text imply -.357 (valued outcomes), -.535 (comparison referents) and -.279 (justifications), but the text does not say whether they are standardised, so treat these as inferred.
- C2 (comparison referents beyond the split): Invisible Work Recognition has no direct effect on fairness, only an indirect one through Division. Between-Gender Comparisons has a direct coefficient of -.229 with Division in the model, but in the combined model that direct effect is no longer significant. Coprovider Orientation (alpha .51) was dropped as unreliable. Net: weak and unstable support for C2, and the one referent construct that held up in one model is a single, post hoc chosen item.
- C3 (justifications beyond the split): Standards has a direct coefficient of -.124 (opposite to the hypothesised sign) and Deciding Together .512 with Division in the model. In the combined model the direct effect of Standards is no longer significant and Deciding Together had to be dropped because it correlated .68 with Feeling Appreciated (multicollinearity), so its independent contribution beside appreciation was not tested. Off the Hook (alpha .55) was dropped as unreliable. Deciding Together is a one-item measure of process rather than of an accepted excuse for inequality.
- C1: the full combined model accounts for 66 percent of the variance in fairness, driven mainly by appreciation. The share attributable to Division alone is not reported, so the text neither confirms nor refutes "small". The authors conclude the allocation is "not the only or primary predictor", but again for a perceived allocation.
- C7: not tested. Fairness and Division both pool housework and child care; the authors argue pooling matters because of trade-offs between the two but do not model chore type.
- C8: the paper describes fairness measures in other work (single item in Blair and Johnson from the NSFH; a three-item index in Mederer) and builds its own two-item measure.

## Limitations
The authors' own: 28 to 31 percent response, so "significant room for some unknown selection biases"; western metropolitan, mostly Anglo-American, middle-class sample; cross-sectional data so direction of effect is open ("wives may make between-gender comparisons because husbands are more involved"); several scales had low reliability (alphas .51 to .63) and three constructs were excluded, so "we are unable to draw conclusions about these important constructs"; the between-gender comparison construct is a single item chosen post hoc after the two planned items were uncorrelated; modification indexes were used to add some correlated errors; sample size forced the framework to be tested in three separate pieces.

Mine: (1) Division is a wife-reported global perception on a 5-point "who does more" scale, not a measure of the actual split in hours or chores, and it comes from the same respondent as the fairness outcome, so shared-reporter bias between Division and Fairness is likely; the claims C1 to C3 speak of the actual split. (2) Because Division is a mediator for most constructs, a direct effect "after the split" is the direct path in a model with Division as a mediator and as a covariate; the indirect effects through Division mean that adjusting for Division removes part of what the cognitions do, and the cognitions and Division are measured at the same time by the same wife, so the adjustment is not clean. (3) No coefficient table is in the text: path coefficients, standard errors and the model's R-squared per equation are in the figures only, and no intervals are given. (4) Many tests across four models without correction. (5) Wives only: nothing on husbands, satisfaction, over-claiming, or over-benefit. (6) This version is a vision model's reading of a scan (version unknown-ocr), so numbers, symbols and table cells could contain reading errors and the quote check marks every extract machine-read; the figures' contents were not in the text and the figure pages were not viewed.

## Follow up
- Mederer, H. J. (1993). Division of labor in two-earner homes: Task accomplishment versus household management as critical variables in perceptions about family work. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 55, 133-145. (Fairness measure; task allocation predicting fairness, bears on C1, C7, C8.)
- Blair, S. L., & Johnson, M. P. (1992). Wives' perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor: The intersection of housework and ideology. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 54, 570-581. (Single-item NSFH fairness measure, C8; ideology and fairness.)
- Thompson, L. (1991). Family work: Women's sense of fairness. Journal of Family Issues, 12, 181-196. (The framework tested here; C2, C3.)
- Figures 1 to 4 (path coefficients for Division and the cognitions) are not in the text of this version; if the source PDF is available, the figure pages could be read to check the inferred Division coefficients.
