# Frisco & Williams 2003: Perceived housework equity, marital happiness and divorce in dual-earner households

## Design
Secondary analysis of a US telephone panel (Marital Instability Over the Life Course, waves 1980, 1983, 1988). Individual respondents, one spouse per couple, analysed separately for men and women. Two outcomes: (a) marital happiness (1980, 11-item scale, alpha .86) regressed by OLS on 1980 perceived housework fairness plus controls (Table 2); (b) divorce or permanent separation by 1988 regressed by logistic regression on 1980 perceived fairness plus controls, with and without marital happiness added (Table 3). Observational; fairness and happiness are measured at the same wave, so the happiness result is cross-sectional. No preregistration. The estimand is the adjusted difference in happiness (or divorce odds) between respondents who say they do more than their fair share and those who say the split is fair.

The actual split was NOT in the happiness or divorce models. The only exposure is the fairness item (dummies: "does more than fair share", "other", reference "fair"); controls are race, education, income, age, marital duration, remarriage, children, gender-role attitudes and wife's part-time status. The respondent's reported proportion of chores (all, most, half, less than half, none) is used only in a descriptive cross-tabulation by perceived fairness (Table 4), with chi-square tests and no adjustment. Footnote 5 says the data held no hours measure. Marital happiness mediation is tested only for fairness to divorce, not for split to satisfaction via fairness.

## Sample
779 dual-earner respondents (484 women, 295 men) from 2,033 married respondents first interviewed in 1980 (married, under 55, contiguous US, telephone); one spouse per couple randomly selected. Dual-earner defined as the respondent saying the husband works full-time and the wife works for pay. Retained those who reported correct marital status in 1988. 14% divorced or separated 1980-1988. Attrition left African Americans, Hispanics, the young, renters and non-college-educated slightly under-represented. Few men report doing more than their share (17%), against 28% of women (Table 1).

## Key results
- Table 2 (OLS, 1980 happiness, scale mean about 42-43, SD about 4): doing more than one's fair share vs fair is -6.734 (SE 1.719) points for men and -3.342 (SE .418) for women, one-tailed p < .001, net of controls. R2 is .089 (men) and .174 (women). Standardised effect about 1.6 SD for men and 0.8 SD for women, computed from Table 1 SDs (4.10 and 4.40) and not stated by the authors. Gender difference in the fairness coefficient: t = -1.918, called similar at p <= .05. These R2 values are for models with fairness in them and no actual-split term, so they do not give the share of variance the split explains.
- Table 3 (logistic, divorce by 1988): women, doing more than fair share OR 2.851 (Model 1) falling to 2.089 (Model 2, happiness added; happiness OR .928 per point); men, OR 3.041 (SE 1.021, not significant) falling to 1.187 once happiness is added. The mediation tested here is from fairness to divorce via happiness, and is not the mediation the claim names.
- Table 4 (descriptive, no controls): among women who say the split is fair, 62.5% report doing all or most of the housework; among men who say it is unfair, 81.5% report doing half or less. Fairness thresholds in terms of actual split differ by gender.
- The Background (p.4) attributes to Perry-Jenkins and Folk (1994) and Wilkie, Ferree and Ratcliff (1998) the finding that perceived equity matters more to satisfaction than the proportion of housework done; this paper does not test that.

## Limitations
Authors': men's null for divorce is tentative (small n, few unfair men); the "other" fairness response cannot be interpreted; no hours-of-housework measure (fn. 5); attrition.
Mine: (1) the actual split is absent from the models, so nothing here separates the effect of fairness from the effect of the split, and C4, C1 and C5 as worded cannot be tested; (2) the fairness item is "fair vs I do more than my share", asked right after the respondent states their own proportion, so it is under-benefit only and is conditional on the respondent's own report; the over-benefited group is not identified (spouse-does-more is folded into an uninterpretable "other", n about 2%); (3) happiness and fairness are measured at once, so reverse causation (unhappy people judge the split unfair) is open; (4) happiness is a single-respondent scale that includes an item on the spouse as someone who takes care of things around the home (item g), which overlaps with the exposure (item g is not among the four items the text says were removed after factor analysis, so it appears to be retained); (5) one-tailed tests, many controls with small n; (6) one spouse per couple, so no over-claiming or couple-level comparison; (7) data from 1980-88 US.
