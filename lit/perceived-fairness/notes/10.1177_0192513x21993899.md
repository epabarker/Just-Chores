# Koster 2022: Fairness perceptions of the division of household labor, housework and childcare

## Design
Cross-sectional survey (New Families in the Netherlands, NFN, Wave 2, 2015-2016). Separate OLS regressions for men and women. Outcomes: housework unfairness and childcare unfairness, each a single self-report item recoded to 0-3 (0 fair for both, 3 very unfair, direction to self or to partner collapsed). Predictors: women's relative share of housework, of childcare (both from reported frequency of tasks, not time) and of paid hours, plus controls (education, gender ideology, union type, number of children, age). Model 2 adds pairwise interactions among the three shares. Wald tests (Suest) compare coefficients across equations and genders, with household-clustered SEs. Association only, no causal identification, no preregistration mentioned. Unfairness to self vs to partner (under- vs over-benefit) could not be analysed because of small cells. Relationship satisfaction, comparison referents and accepted justifications were not measured.

## Sample
1,100 respondents (462 men, 638 women) from 756 households; heterosexual married or cohabiting parents with a youngest child under 18, Dutch, random sample drawn with Statistics Netherlands. Wave 2 response 70% of persons; both partners took part in about half of households (46% in the analytic sample). Men, people of non-Western descent and people on low incomes were underrepresented; lower educated and younger people dropped out more after Wave 1. Respondents with missing data (n = 20) were excluded.

## Key results
- Single-item fairness measures: one item for housework and one for childcare (C8).
- Table 4, Model 1 (shares plus controls): R2 = 0.047 (men) and 0.134 (women) for housework unfairness; 0.065 and 0.139 for childcare unfairness. Adjusted R2 0.028 to 0.127. Model 2 with interactions: R2 0.074 to 0.155. So the model with the division of labor and controls explains roughly 5-14% of variance (C1). The authors nonetheless call the standardised effects large (b = 0.64 men, 1.11 women for housework share on housework unfairness, effect sizes 0.83 and 1.33 in SD units of Y), so "small part of the variance" and "large slope" coexist: the outcome is coarse and mostly 0 (fair), with R2 low.
- Controls were essentially null, so the R2 is almost entirely the division of labor (the paper reports that none of the controls had a significant impact on housework unfairness in Model 1).
- Housework and childcare divisions were not differently related to unfairness (Wald tests p = 0.792 men, p = 0.609 women). Relevant only loosely to C7: the paper compares types of chores but only by relative frequency of tasks, with no control for time (C7).
- Self-reported shares differ by gender of reporter: women report women's relative housework share 0.80, men report 0.74 (Table 1). This is directionally what over-claiming would produce, but the samples are not matched couples (partner pairs in 46% of households), the measure is a relative-share scale that cannot sum above 1 by construction, and no sum of the two partners' shares is computed (C6).
- Introduction (secondary): the authors state that studies show perceived fairness, not the actual division, explains well-being and relationship outcomes (C4). Not tested here.
- No relationship-satisfaction outcome, no mediation, no referents, no justifications, no over-benefit vs equitable comparison on satisfaction: C2, C3, C5, C9 are not addressed.

## Limitations
Authors': cross-sectional; selective sample (long-term relationships, underrepresented groups, possible overestimation of effects); could not separate unfairness to self from to partner; only relative, not absolute, contributions measured. Ours: unfairness is a single coarse 4-point item with most responses at 0; R2 is for linear models of a bounded ordinal outcome; the three shares are partly reported by one respondent so same-source bias is possible; education, gender ideology came from Wave 1; relative shares are task-frequency ratings, not time, so the paper cannot speak to chores vs time (C7); no couple-level matching in analyses so over-claiming (C6) cannot be tested.
