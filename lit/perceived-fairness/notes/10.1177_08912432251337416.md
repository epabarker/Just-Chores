# Urbina 2025: Perceived fairness of couples' division of housework, multi-factorial vignette experiment

SINGLE STUDY, VIGNETTE EXPERIMENT. Respondents rate hypothetical couples. This is not a partner's own allocation, and nobody in it is a partner of the person being rated.

## Design
Multi-factorial vignette experiment (Jasso's justice evaluation function), pre-registered on OSF (note 1). 3 x 3 x 3 x 5 x 2 fully crossed design: relative earnings (60/50/40%), relative paid work time (30/35/40 h), remote work, focal person's gender (name-based), and the focal person's share of housework (10/30/50/70/90%). 270 vignettes in 54 D-efficient decks of 5; each respondent is randomly assigned one deck and rates 5 vignettes in random order. All five dimensions are randomised, so the housework split and the "justifications" (earnings, work time, remote work) are experimentally independent of each other (design correlations below 0.03, per Online Appendix Table A3, not read here).
Outcome: one self-report item, an 11-point scale from -5 (focal spouse unfairly doing too little housework) to +5 (doing too much), 0 = fair. Estimand: the average effect of each vignette dimension on that rating, by linear regression with respondent-clustered SEs; extended models interact vignette gender with respondent education, gender and gender ideology. Chores are not specified: "housework shares" of an undefined weekly set of chores. No satisfaction, comparison-referent or justification-endorsement measure at the respondent level. No R-squared or variance decomposition is reported anywhere in the text.

## Sample
1,502 US adults from Prolific ("nationally representative"), collected August 2022; 7,510 vignette ratings, 7,425 in the models (17 nonbinary respondents x 5 vignettes = 85 dropped, my arithmetic). 51% women, mean age 45.5, 60% college degree (over-represented; weighted results said to be the same, Online Appendix A2), 79% white, 14% traditional gender ideology. 36% of respondents (n = 818) do more housework than their partner (Table 3). Robustness: dropping straight-liners (0.53% of observations) and speeders under 3 minutes (3.13%) leaves results unchanged (Table 5, cols 2-3).

## Key results
- The randomised housework share dominates the rating: coefficients of about -3.5 (10%) and +3.5 (90%) fairness points against the 50% reference, versus 0.2 to 0.6 for each other dimension (Table 4 col 1). The fairness scale is -5 to +5.
- Justifications: focal person with more relative earnings (20-point gap) is rated 0.204 points more overburdened (p < 0.001), with more work time (10 h gap) 0.449 points more overburdened. I.e. a partner who brings more earnings or hours is seen as fairly doing less housework. Remote work moderates the work-time effect (about -0.3 points).
- Gender: vignette wives doing more are rated about 0.4 points more unfair than husbands; no "just gender housework gap" favouring a larger female share. Effects of earnings and work time do not differ by gender except lower earners (wives 0.3 vs husbands 0.1, p < 0.05).
- Respondent characteristics (college, gender ideology) shift the gender asymmetry (about 0.2 and 0.4 points).
- Bears on none of C4, C5, C6, C9 (no satisfaction, no self-reported shares) and only as a stated non-test on C2 and C7. C8: this study uses a single item, which says nothing about what studies "mainly" do.

## Limitations
Authors' own: social comparison processes and process fairness were not examined (cognitive burden), so no comparison-referent test; chore type is not distinguished (time-rigid vs flexible), nor cognitive or emotional labour; race not analysed; results may not generalise beyond couples with adolescent children and different-sex couples; possible social-desirability bias; "our analysis pertains to how people judge others' divisions of labor and not their own arrangements"; 35 h may not read as full time.
Mine: (1) Third-party judgments of hypothetical couples: the rater has no stake, no history, no knowledge of the couple's actual chores, and the authors themselves argue that real partners' fairness reports carry post hoc rationalisation, which the vignette removes by design but which is exactly what a partner-level claim needs. External validity to a partner's own perceived fairness is untested. (2) Single study, single wave, one Prolific sample. (3) The housework share was varied over an extreme range (10-90%) while earnings and hours differences were moderate (20 points, 10 h), so the relative size of the share's coefficient reflects the chosen stimulus ranges, not a population variance share; no R-squared is reported, so "explains a small part of the variation" is not directly tested either way. (4) Dummy-coded effects are per-vignette and rest on 5 ratings per person, clustered; many interaction tests are not corrected for multiplicity (preregistered hypotheses though). (5) Online Appendix (Tables A1-A4, Figure A2) was not available in the text and was not read.
