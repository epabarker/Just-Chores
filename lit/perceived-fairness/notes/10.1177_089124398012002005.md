# Press & Townsley 1998: Wives' and husbands' housework reporting

## Design
Cross-sectional, two-sample comparison of survey methods, not a study of couples. Direct-question weekly hours on four female-typed tasks (meals, dishes, cleaning, laundry) from the National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH, 1987-88) are compared with single-day time-diary hours, scaled to a synthetic week (daily sum times seven), from Americans' Use of Time 1985 (AUT). A Heckman two-step OLS model of diary hours in the AUT is applied to NSFH respondents' covariates to predict each one's diary-equivalent hours; the "reporting gap" is the NSFH self-report minus that prediction, and is regressed on gender, gender ideology, predicted hours, class and family variables. The estimand is the gap between survey modes, with the diary taken as the baseline; it is not a partner's share of chores, and no fairness or satisfaction outcome is measured. Not preregistered.

## Sample
Married individuals only. NSFH: 6,882 married cases (national probability sample of 13,017 adults, 1987-88); AUT: 2,844 married cases (4,939 respondents in the full study; adults 18+ in households with telephones). Reporting-gap models use 3,641 NSFH respondents who reported some housework (2,205 wives, 1,436 husbands); 19.8 percent of NSFH cases were missing on housework hours (26.2 percent of husbands, 14.4 percent of wives, note 9). The two samples are not spouses of one another (note 3).

## Key results
- C6: the paper shows overreport of own hours against diary hours for both sexes, not a sum of partners' shares above the whole. Table 3 means: husbands 7.7 direct-question vs 4.2 diary hours per week; wives 31.8 vs 18.4. Mean overreport 13.4 hours (wives) and 3.5 hours (husbands). In the regression-based gap (Table 5) the means are 12.8 hours for wives and 5.8 for husbands. Because the samples are unmatched, the two directly reported means come from different people, so no couple's reported shares are added up. The inference to C6 is indirect: the evidence is that both sexes inflate against a diary baseline, which is consistent with, but does not test, partners' shares summing to more than 100 percent.
- The diary baseline is itself assumed accurate, and the authors assume no gender difference in diary measurement error (see Limitations).
- Nothing in the paper bears on C1-C5, C7-C9: it measures no perceived fairness, relationship satisfaction or comparison referents. It uses a single scale item on gender ideology only as a predictor of the reporting gap.

## Limitations
Read from a scan: this text is a vision model's reading of a scanned paper (version `unknown-ocr`), so numbers and table cells may carry transcription errors; the extracts are machine-read and a table value should be checked against the printed page before it is relied on.

Authors' own: the AUT diary baseline may itself be inflated ("report inflation probably occurs to some extent even when this method is used"); only the four most frequent female-typed tasks, no child care; the six comparable controls are the only ones available; the models' R-squares are low (adjusted R2 .085 combined, .053 wives, .063 husbands) and the authors say they present them to "explicate what we conceptualize as the underlying causal model"; the NSFH instrument did not forbid overlapping task reports; data are individuals, not matched couples (note 3), which they call "problematic for studies of the domestic division of labor".

Ours: the claim at issue (C6) concerns couples' summed shares, which this design cannot test. The reporting gap is a model-based difference between two different samples, depending on the interchangeability assumption, the Heckman model and the synthetic week (a single diary day times seven). The social desirability account is inferred from covariate patterns, not measured directly; the authors say they cannot distinguish why husbands overreport. Data are from 1985-88 in the United States.
