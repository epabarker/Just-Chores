# Carlson et al. 2018: Stalled for whom? Division of particular housework tasks and relationship quality

## Design
Repeated cross-sectional comparison of two cohorts of couples (NSFH wave 2, 1992-1994; MARS, 2006). Observational, no randomisation or causal identification strategy, no preregistration. Exposure: for each of seven tasks (cooking, dishes, cleaning, shopping, laundry, home maintenance, bill paying), a three-category division (she does the majority / shared equally [reference] / he does the majority). In the MARS this comes from a five-point self-report of who does the task; in the NSFH2 it is derived from each partner's reported weekly hours on the task (male hours over couple total hours), cut at roughly 40% and 60%. Outcomes: sexual frequency, sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction (single item in each survey), relationship trouble, discussed separating, physical arguments. Estimation by OLS (satisfaction), negative binomial (sexual frequency) and logistic regression (discord), with standard errors clustered on couple; multiple imputation for missing values; models for relationship satisfaction run separately for women (Table 4) and men (Table 5) because interaction tests with gender were significant (not shown). In Tables 3-5 all seven task divisions appear as predictors under a single column per outcome, with one shared control note, so they appear to be entered together in one model per outcome and cohort (inferred from the table layout; the text does not say "simultaneously").
Perceived fairness is not measured anywhere in the paper. The paper reads as an actual-split-by-task study with relationship quality as the outcome.

## Sample
MARS: Internet probability sample (Knowledge Networks, early 2006), 1,095 individuals in 605 couples; only 487 couples had both partners complete the survey and the analysis is limited to them; income truncated at the 95th percentile, final n = 932 individuals (466 men, 466 women). NSFH2: 10,005 reinterviewed respondents, restricted to couples with at least 8 of 18 valid housework responses (n = 5,249), with a minor child (n = 3,303), female partner under 45 (n = 2,664), income truncated at $44,500 (about $63,635 in 2006 dollars); final n = 2,628 (1,314 men, 1,314 women). Married and cohabiting, low to moderate income, US. About 50% (NSFH2) and 16% (MARS) of cases would be lost to listwise deletion; imputed with Stata mi, ten imputations.

## Key results
- Task-level division of housework is related to relationship satisfaction and discord; the association is stronger in the 2006 cohort than the early 1990s one, and differs by task and gender (Tables 4 and 5). Women, 2006: responsibility for all routine tasks except laundry is associated with lower relationship satisfaction than sharing (for dishes B = -.317, SE .100, p < .01; cooking -.256*; cleaning -.261*; shopping -.194 at p < .10). Early 1990s women: only dishes and shopping, at p < .10.
- Authors single out dishwashing as the most consequential task, especially for women (Table 4: she does majority of dishes, 2006, relationship trouble B = 1.056***, discussed separating .718*, physical arguments 2.872**).
- Men, 2006: he does the majority of dishes -.305*, of cleaning -.385*, of shopping -.334*; she does the majority of shopping -.165 at p < .10 (Table 5). Men, early 1990s: she does the majority of laundry is marginally positive (.189, p < .10).
- Both partners' total weekly housework hours are a control in every model. Time spent on each individual task is not a control apart from its use in building the NSFH2 split; no model contains an overall measure of the actual split alongside the task identity, because each task enters only through its own split.
- No R-squared or explained-variance figure is given for any model.
- Mechanism: authors state they cannot say why tasks differ; they speculate that equity feelings or teamwork may matter, and they offer relative deprivation as an explanation of why equal sharing matters more as it becomes more common. None of this is tested.
- Secondary reporting of the equity literature in the Background: most studies find perceived equity matters more than equality for relationship quality; Faulkner et al. (2005) found a chores/money/paid-work fairness index did not affect marital satisfaction; Dew and Wilcox (2011) tied the decline in new mothers' marital satisfaction to both more chores and perceived inequity.

Claim C7: the nearest evidence. The paper shows which chore is shared matters for relationship satisfaction and discord, with the actual split (shared vs. majority by one partner) and total household hours in the same model, but the outcome is not perceived fairness and the task effect is estimated only through each task's own split (there is no model with a task-free overall split). So it neither confirms nor refutes C7. The actual split and task type are therefore in the same model only in the sense that the task-level splits are the predictors; the paper has no separate task-type term against an overall split.
Claims C1, C6, C8 and C9 (as an over-benefit test): no direct evidence. C2 and C3: no test; only the authors' speculation about comparison with others (C2).

## Limitations
Authors' own: slight differences in the wording and scales of the relationship items across the two surveys; the NSFH2 measure is hours-based and the MARS measure is categorical, and estimates of change may be overstated if categorical reports are biased toward egalitarian divisions; no upper-class sample; no information on when and how a task is done; only two nonroutine tasks.
Ours: cross-sectional, so no temporal order between division and satisfaction and no handling of reverse causation or confounding by unmeasured traits; each respondent reports both the division and the outcome (shared-method bias); coefficients for seven correlated task dummies in one model are sensitive to collinearity; many outcomes and tasks with p < .10 treated as findings, no multiplicity correction; relationship satisfaction is a single item; the actual split is defined as a three-way category and is not an equity or fairness measure, so nothing here speaks to perceived fairness; a participant who is "over-benefited" by the split is identified only by gender and conventionality of arrangement, not by perceived benefit; the control for total household hours is couple-level and does not hold task time constant; the MARS sample is a restricted one (487 of 605 couples with both partners) with income truncation and imputation; MARS and NSFH2 differ in sample restrictions and mode, so cohort and design differences are confounded.
