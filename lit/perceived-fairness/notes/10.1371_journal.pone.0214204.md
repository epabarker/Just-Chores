# [first author not in text file] 2019 (PLOS ONE): Gendered perceptions of fairness in housework and shared expenses

## Design
Cross-sectional, observational secondary analysis of a self-selected online survey ("Money, Sex & Love Survey", NBCNews.com, 2008). Exposures: respondent's categorical perceived fairness of the division of housework (3 levels: ego unfair / mostly fair / unfair to partner) and of shared expenses (same 3 levels), entered together. Outcomes: relationship satisfaction (1-7, single item) and sex frequency (0-8, single item). Estimands are covariate-adjusted associations (OLS standardised betas, Table 4; seemingly unrelated regression with gender interactions, Table 5). No actual (objective) division of chores is measured, no mediation analysis, no justification or comparison-referent measures, no task-level data. Not preregistered. Analysis population: N = 10,236 different-gender cohabiting respondents who answered both fairness items.

## Sample
10,236 respondents (6,637 men, 3,599 women; 65% male, 89% married or remarried, mean age 42.6), US, volunteer web sample recruited by an invitation on NBCNews.com for ten days in 2008, restricted to different-gender cohabiting couples, excluding gay/lesbian/bisexual respondents (n = 569). One partner per couple (no dyads). Non-probability sample with higher income and education than the national population. Personal income missing for 336 respondents, imputed at the median. No attrition (single cross-section); the number who opened the survey is not given.

## Key results
- Housework fairness item, as worded, tells respondents to consider work/commute schedules and has three collapsed categories (E1). The paper contrasts it with a broader single item used by Lively et al. (E2). This is the only fairness measure used for chores: one self-report item (C8).
- "Unfair to partner" (the over-benefited position) for housework versus "mostly fair": standardised beta on relationship satisfaction -0.03 (p < .001) without covariates and -0.00 (n.s.) with covariates (Table 4); SUR unstandardised b = 0.02 (n.s.) (Table 5). The authors state that unfairness to partner in housework was not associated with satisfaction or sex frequency (E4, E5, E6). Raw means in Table 2 are 5.4 versus 5.4 for men and 5.7 versus 5.9 for women (E7). So for chores the over-benefited group is not lower than the equitable group once covariates are in (C9 contradicted for chores).
- For shared expenses (not chores), unfair to partner is associated with lower satisfaction: beta -0.05 (p < .001) in Model 1.2, b = -0.59 (p < .001) in Table 5 (E8). This is a different domain from chores.
- The authors raise reverse causation and endogeneity (E9), and cite a prior finding that perceived equity links the division of housework to sex (E10), which bears loosely on C5.

## Limitations
Authors' own: correlational, so no causal claims; relationship quality might affect reported fairness; non-probability web sample; single global items for satisfaction; childcare and chores are not separated; fairness-for-partner may reflect biased reports; they did not measure actual housework time.
Ours: "over-benefited" is the respondent's own subjective category, not a measured allocation, and the comparison group "mostly fair" is self-judged fairness, so the C9 comparison is perceived-over-benefit versus perceived-fair, not allocation-based. The housework item mixes chores and childcare and primes work/commute schedules as a justification. Fairness and satisfaction are reported by the same person at the same time (common-method and reverse-causation concerns). Control for years in relationship, marriage and children may over-adjust. Standardised betas of -0.00 and 0.02 are reported without intervals. Nine hypotheses and many subgroup tests are run; the authors apply a .01 threshold only to the Table 2 and 3 comparisons. Gender differences in the proportion reporting each category are large (41.9% of women versus 15.7% of men report housework ego unfairness), so the pooled estimates mix groups. The paper bears on no claim about the actual split (C1, C4, C6, C7), comparison referents (C2) or justifications as predictors (C3), and has no mediation test (C5). The text file does not name the authors, so the heading lacks a first author.

## Follow up
- Lively K. J., Steelman L. C., & Powell B. (2010). Equity, emotion and the household division of labor. Social Psychology Quarterly, 73, 358-379. Single-item fairness measure; relevant to C8 and possibly C9.
- Carlson D. L., Miller A. J., Sassler S., & Hanson S. (2016). The gendered division of housework and couples' sexual relationships: A reexamination. Journal of Marriage and Family, 78(4), 975-995. Perceived equity as a mechanism linking division of housework to sex; relevant to C5.
- Grote N. K. & Clark M.S. (2001). Perceiving unfairness in the family: Cause or consequence of marital distress? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 80, 281-293. Direction of effect between fairness and satisfaction; relevant to C4 and C5.
- Greenstein T. N. (1996). Gender ideology and perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor: Effects on marital quality. Social Forces, 74(3), 1029-1042. Relevant to C4 and C5.
