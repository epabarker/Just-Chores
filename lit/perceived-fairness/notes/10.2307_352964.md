# Mederer 1993: Task accomplishment versus household management in two-earner homes

## Design
Cross-sectional survey, observational, no randomisation or causal identification strategy. OLS hierarchical regression (SPSS) with controls entered first, then wife's proportion of housework time, then a "task" allocation scale and a "management" allocation scale, added separately and together (Models 1-5). Outcomes are wife-reported perceived fairness (3-item scale) and conflict (1 item) about the division of household labor. The estimand is the incremental adjusted R2 and coefficient of each allocation measure for wives' perceptions; it is descriptive and predictive, not causal. Not preregistered. Analysis population in Tables 2-4 is n = 242, drawn from the 359 married respondents (the paper does not explain the drop; presumably listwise deletion).

## Sample
359 married, full-time employed women (of 652 usable questionnaires from female Rhode Island state employees, 1989; mailed to 1,500, 847 agreed, 660 returned, response rate 43%). Median age 44, average marriage about 19 years, 79% have children. Only wives were surveyed; husbands' reports are not available, so both partners' time and task shares are the wife's report.

## Key results
- Control variables alone (age of youngest child, family income, years married, education, gender attitudes): adjusted R2 = .052 for perceived fairness (Table 3 model 1). Adding the wife's proportion of housework time raises it to .417; adding task, .515; management, .495; all three, .535 (Table 3).
- Standardised coefficients in the full fairness model (Table 3 model 5): time -.38, task -.29, management -.22 (t = -5.06, -3.49, -2.55). The author says time is the strongest single predictor.
- Conflict is predicted less well (full model adjusted R2 .256), and only task remains significant in the full model.
- Management and task allocation each add independently to fairness beyond time (increment to R2 significant at p < .05 for each). This is a contrast between kinds of work (planning/orchestrating versus doing), not a test of which specific chores predict fairness.
- Fairness was measured with a 3-item scale (alpha .86), one item of which is satisfaction with the husband's share and another how much more or less housework the respondent should do.

## Limitations
Authors' own: the control variables were intercorrelated despite attempts to reduce colinearity, and they lost significance in the full models; supplementary models (data not shown) were run with fewer controls.

Mine: (1) The "actual split" is the wife's own report of who does each chore and of both spouses' weekly hours, from the same questionnaire as the fairness items. Same-source reporting and shared response style or reporting bias would inflate the association; the R2 of .42 to .54 is therefore not an estimate of how well the actual split predicts fairness. (2) The fairness scale contains items ("how satisfied they were with the amount of housework done by their husband", "should be doing") that are close to the split itself, so part of the R2 is conceptual overlap. (3) Wives only, a single occupational sample, 43% response, full-time employed, and n falls from 359 to 242 in the regressions. (4) The cross-section cannot separate the split causing perceived fairness from fairness colouring the reported split. (5) No comparison referents, justifications, satisfaction or equity categories (over-benefited) were measured, so nothing bears on C2, C3, C4, C5, C6 or C9. (6) The text is a scanned JSTOR page layer (pages 2-14 are images). A few misreadings occur (for example "7006" for 70% and "0.05%" where Table 3 shows .052), so the paper was read from a scan and the prose "0.05%" for the control-only R2 conflicts with the table's .052; the table was used.
