# Blair & Johnson 1992: Wives' perceptions of the fairness of the division of household labor

## Design
Cross-sectional secondary analysis of the 1988 National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH). Outcome: wives' perceived fairness of household chores, a single 5-point self-report item ("very unfair to me" = 1 to "very unfair to him" = 5; "fair" = 3). Predictors: wives' reports of hours per week on eight chores (husbands' hours in "female" tasks, wife's total hours), three 7-point qualities of the work (boring-interesting, complicated-simple, lonely-sociable), one 7-point unappreciated-appreciated item, and two gender-ideology measures. OLS regression (standardised betas, Table 2) and one-way ANOVA with recoded categories (eta, Table 3), run separately for employed and nonemployed wives. No randomisation or causal identification; associations only. Not preregistered. The framework is Thompson's (1991) outcomes / comparisons / justifications; the authors state they had no direct measures of comparisons or justifications and used ideology variables as rough proxies. Wives only; husbands' labor is also taken from wives' reports.

## Sample
778 married white women aged under 65 from the NSFH (13,017 respondents in all), complete on all variables and reporting 120 or fewer weekly housework hours; 496 employed and 282 nonemployed. Data are from the 1988 wave. Listwise deletion from the full survey; no attrition (cross-section). Husbands' self-reports exist in the NSFH but were not used.

## Key results
- Perceived fairness is mostly "fair" or "somewhat unfair to me": mean 2.64 (employed, 61% fair) and 2.76 (nonemployed, 71% fair); about 3-4% see the split as unfair to the husband.
- Husbands' hours in female-typed tasks is the strongest predictor: beta .244 (employed) and .222 (nonemployed), both p < .01, net of wife's total labor. In a separate model, betas for husbands' hours in female, male and gender-neutral tasks are .19, .01 and .07; only the first is significant. Wife's total labor: beta -.112 (p < .01) for employed wives, -.002 for nonemployed wives.
- Perceived appreciation: beta .229 (employed) and .165 (nonemployed); second strongest.
- Qualities of the work are weak (only loneliness for nonemployed wives, beta .132). Ideology is minimal (family-labor ideology beta -.094 for employed wives; sex-role ideology null); the predicted ideology x husband-labor interactions were not found.
- Model R2 = .145 (employed) and .157 (nonemployed). The authors say the models explain "only about 15%". This is the whole model (labor, qualities, appreciation, ideology), so the share due to the actual split alone is no larger.
- Table 3 etas for husbands' hours in female tasks: .32 (employed), .23 (nonemployed); for wife's total labor .16 and .05. Employed wives' mean fairness is lower at 1-4 hours of husband female-task labor (2.32) than at 0 hours (2.66): a non-monotone pattern the regression misses.
- Husbands' and wives' paid-work hours had no significant effect (analysis not shown).
- No measure of relationship satisfaction, over-benefit, referents or justifications is analysed. Relationship satisfaction appears only in the literature review, as a link in a presumed chain from fairness to marital satisfaction to depression.

## Limitations
Authors': no direct measures of comparison processes or justifications; models explain about 15%; ideology measures weak (sex-role alpha .55); sample is only married white women; the ideology finding is puzzling to them.
Mine: cross-sectional, so no causal reading; fairness, appreciation and the quality items are all wives' perceptions from one respondent (shared-method variance, and appreciation may partly restate fairness); husbands' hours come from wives' reports; fairness is a single 5-point item that mixes "unfair to me" and "unfair to him" on one scale; hours in female vs male tasks are measured as time, so "task type" is confounded with time in those tasks, and husbands' total hours are not controlled (the authors note male tasks involve little time relative to female tasks); R2 is reported for the full model, not for the actual split alone; ANOVA categories are coarse and the curvilinear pattern was not modelled; no multiplicity adjustment across the many interaction tests; sign pattern of fairness on a bipolar scale makes "fair" the midpoint, so linear OLS is a blunt fit. Claims C4, C5, C6 and C9 are not testable in this paper.

## Follow up
- Thompson, L. (1991). Family work: Women's sense of fairness. Journal of Family Issues, 12, 181-196 (framework: outcomes, comparisons, justifications; C2, C3).
- Benin, M. H., & Agostinelli, J. (1988). Husbands' and wives' satisfaction with the division of labor. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 50, 349-361 (cited for the claim that only husbands' labor in feminine tasks affects wives; C7).
- Yogev, S., & Brett, J. (1985). Perceptions of the division of housework and childcare and marital satisfaction. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 47, 609-618 (fairness and marital satisfaction; C4, C5).
- Blair & Lichter (1991), Journal of Family Issues, 12, 91-113 (measurement of the division of labor; C7).
