# Wilkie, Ferree & Ratcliff 1998: Gender and Fairness, Marital Satisfaction in Two-Earner Couples

## Design
Cross-sectional observational telephone survey (1989) of two-earner married couples, husbands and wives interviewed separately. Not randomised, no preregistration. Three-stage OLS "path analysis" (not LISREL), run separately for husbands and wives, with nested F tests for gender differences. Stage 1: value orientations on the actual division of labour (Table 2). Stage 2: division of labour, spousal-estimate discrepancies, empowerment, values and demographics on perceived fairness of housework division, perceived fairness of paid-work division, and empathy (Table 3). Stage 3: all of these on global marital satisfaction, with direct, indirect and total effects (Table 4). The actual split is the mean of husband's and wife's estimates of their own and the spouse's contributions (percentage of domestic hours done by wife, percentage of total hours done by wife, percentage of family income earned by husband). Perceived fairness of housework is a dichotomous variable built from two items (self "fair share" item and spouse "right to expect" item); paid-work fairness is one item. Estimand: association of each predictor with fairness or satisfaction, coefficients described as betas (reported to two decimals, no intervals). The analysis population is the 382 couples in which both spouses were interviewed.

## Sample
Random sample of 550 employed married Connecticut women (response rate 62% including those not employed) and, where possible, their husbands; only couples where both were interviewed kept, giving 382 couples. All spouses employed at least 1 hour in the previous week; 57% of wives full-time. 95% White, 51% Catholic, well educated (41% of women and 48% of men with a bachelor's degree or higher), median family income $52,875. Missing individual report items (fewer than 10 of 382) filled from the spouse's report.

## Key results
- Perceived fairness of housework: wives 55% "fair", husbands 89% (Table 1, chi-square p < .05); paid work 88% wives, 82% husbands. Wives average 62% of domestic hours.
- Table 3 (fairness equations): the actual domestic split predicts housework fairness (wives beta -.34, husbands .22; coefficients differ by gender). The full Table 3 model, which also holds empowerment, values, demographics and spousal-estimate discrepancies, has adjusted R2 .19 (wives) and .11 (husbands) for housework fairness and .05 and .05 for paid-work fairness. Predictors were entered only if significant for either gender. The variance due to the split alone is not reported, so these figures are only upper bounds on it (E1, E2). Division-of-labour effects are gendered: the share of total hours predicts unfairness of the gender-atypical domain.
- Comparison referents: none measured. The nearest items are discrepancies between husband's and wife's estimates of each other's contributions ("credit"); the domestic-hours discrepancy predicts wives' housework unfairness (beta -.11) net of the split (E3, E4).
- Justifications: not measured as such. Agenda-setting power ("empowerment") predicts perceived housework fairness net of the actual split (beta .13 wives, .14 husbands), interpreted by the authors as procedural justice (E5, E6). Conventional gender expectations are said to guide judgments of what is fair (E7).
- Table 4 (satisfaction): perceived fairness of housework and paid work have significant direct effects for both spouses (rows read from the page: wives .11 and .08; husbands .16 and .14); the actual-split rows are small (magnitudes of .04 or less in the same table) and the text says conventional role performance affects satisfaction only indirectly via fairness and understanding (E8, E9, E10). Empathy is the largest single predictor (wives .42, husbands .38). Adjusted R2: wives .47, husbands .36. Fairness and empathy are entered together, so fairness is not the only mediator. The abstract (abstract-only, not an extract) says the division of labour and role preferences "affect marital satisfaction mainly through perceptions of fairness".
- Mediation: no formal mediation test is described in the methods; the Table 4 note says indirect paths were tested separately and significance marked if one or more paths were significant (E14). Authors call the causal specification "partially speculative" (E13). The text's mediation statements are E11 and E12.
- Over-claiming: the discrepancy in estimates of the wife's share of domestic hours averages 4.5 points; both spouses are said to report more contribution by themselves than the partner attributes to them (E15, E16). If each respondent's own and spouse's shares sum to 100, a mean wife-minus-husband discrepancy of 4.5 points means the two partners' self-claimed shares sum to about 104.5 on average. That arithmetic is my inference, not a reported figure. For income share the sign runs the other way (E17).
- Over-benefit: an earlier attempt to build overreward measures found no significant relation to satisfaction in any model; the estimates are not shown and overreward was dropped (E22, E23).
- Chore type: domestic work is a single hours measure pooled over cooking, cleaning, repairs, shopping, yard, finances, child care; no chore-specific analysis (E18).
- Measurement of fairness: the paper says direct fairness questions are rare in the literature and that fairness is commonly inferred from "should do more" or satisfaction items (E19, secondary reporting); its own housework fairness variable uses two items, its paid-work variable one (E20, E21).

## Limitations
Authors' own: cross-sectional data, causal model "partially speculative", reciprocal causation assumed present but not tested; a general linear model rather than LISREL because reliable multi-item indicators were lacking; empathy and fairness effects hard to disentangle, so the fairness effect is probably underestimated. Reader's: single state, 1989, 95% White, well educated; response rate 62% and the 382-couple restriction to couples where both responded; actual split is the average of the spouses' own self-reports, so it shares method and reporter with the fairness and satisfaction reports (and egocentric bias is acknowledged); fairness measure is dichotomous with the housework version coded 1 for everyone not under-rewarded, so over-benefited respondents are lumped with equitable ones and husbands show a ceiling (89% fair); reliabilities of the fairness and preference scales are low (alpha .47 and .51 for housework fairness, .35 to .44 for domestic preferences); many coefficients across several equations with no multiplicity correction apart from the Table 1 experimentwise alpha; Table 3 includes only predictors significant for either gender; fairness could follow satisfaction rather than cause it; mediation is judged by sign-and-significance of paths, not by an interval for the indirect effect; the comparison of fairness against the split in C4 rests on standardised-looking OLS betas for a dichotomous fairness item against continuous split measures, with the column layout of Table 4 scrambled in the text layer (blank cells dropped), so values were taken from the row reconstruction of page 15; domestic work is time only, with no chore-level data.

## Follow up
- Lennon & Rosenfield (1994). Relative fairness and the division of housework: The importance of options. American Journal of Sociology, 100, 506-531 (direct fairness question; women who earn more perceive housework division as unfair).
- Major, B. (1993). Gender, entitlement, and the distribution of family labor. Journal of Social Issues, 49, 141-159 (comparison referents and justifications).
- Thompson, L. (1991). Family work: Women's sense of fairness. Journal of Family Issues, 12, 181-196.
- Benin & Agostinelli (1988). Husbands' and wives' satisfaction with the division of labor. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 50, 349-361 (fairness inferred from satisfaction).
- Yogev & Brett (1985). Perceptions of the division of housework and child care and marital satisfaction. Journal of Marriage and the Family, 47, 609-618.
- Berk, S. F. (1985). The gender factory (fairness inferred from "should do more").
- Berk & Shih (1980) and Huber & Spitze (1983) (egocentric bias in spouse reports of household labour).
- Perry-Jenkins & Folk (1994), Journal of Marriage and the Family, 56, 165-180 (perceived fairness as a filter between the split and marital assessments).
- Intercorrelation tables are "available from the first author" and are not in the text. No figures in this paper.
