# Carriero & Todesco 2016: Housework Division and Perceived Fairness: The Importance of Comparison Referents

## Design
Two designs on the same respondents. (1) Cross-sectional telephone survey of both partners in dual-earner couples; outcome is the respondent's own perceived fairness of the housework division, dichotomised (1 = fair, 0 = unfair) and analysed by logistic regression, separately for men and women. Exposures are respondents' self-reported comparisons (own time, or spouse's time, "about equal / lower / higher" than a referent: father, mother, male or female friend, "average" Italian man or woman). Estimand: average predicted probability of "fair" by comparison outcome, with the other outcomes held at observed values; controls include the respondent's own percentage of housework (and childcare), working hours of both partners, paid help, age, education, gender ideology. Observational, so referent choice may itself be shaped by the judgment (the authors discuss self-serving bias). (2) A between-subjects randomised online vignette (one vignette per respondent; friends' behaviour randomised), analysed by OLS; the outcome is the respondent's estimate of how many of 100 couples would see the husband do more housework, not fairness. No preregistration is mentioned. Within-couple dependence was checked with cluster-robust SEs and one-respondent-per-couple samples (results not shown).

## Sample
Survey: 828 couples (1656 individuals) interviewed by phone, October 2010 to February 2011, response rate 42%, random draw from landline directories in four provinces of Piemonte (northwest Italy); dual-earner married or cohabiting couples with at least one child under 13. Analyses use list-wise deletion subsamples (N per model in an appendix table that is an image in the PDF; not in the text layer). Vignette: online follow-up spring 2013; 1365 of 1656 reached by email, 691 answered the vignette (42% of the initial sample), positively selected on education.

## Key results
- The split and fairness are only loosely tied in the descriptive data: most women (62.8%) and men (57.4%) call their share fair while women do about two thirds and men about one third of the chores (no variance explained is reported).
- Survey: with the respondent's own percentage of housework controlled, unfavourable comparisons lower the predicted probability of "fair" for husbands comparing themselves with male referents (about -0.15 on average, e.g. 0.56 to 0.40 for male friends) and for wives comparing their husband with male referents (e.g. 0.63 to 0.45 for the father); favourable comparisons raise it for husbands (e.g. 0.53 to 0.65 for the average Italian man) but are mostly non-significant for wives. No significant effects for comparisons involving women.
- Table 1 values and the appendix regression coefficients are images in the PDF and are not in the text layer; only the prose numbers above were read and quoted.
- Self-serving bias: 83.1% of husbands say they do more or about the same as the average Italian man, but their reported hours (8.9 and 7.5) are below the time-use mean (10.3).
- Vignette: friends sharing housework equally rather than doing almost nothing raises the subjective probability that the vignette husband does more by +6.9 percentage points; a behavioural-intention outcome, not fairness.
- Nothing on relationship satisfaction, justifications, which chores are done, mediation, or the sum of partners' claimed shares.

## Limitations
Authors': referent choice may be self-serving (survey estimates may be biased upward); the vignette has external-validity limits and its outcome is not a fairness judgment; the vignette sample is selected on education; the sample is northern Italy only. Mine: the "actual split" control is the respondent's own estimate built from frequency questions, so it carries reporting error and any self-serving bias, leaving residual confounding of the referent effect; the comparison variables are themselves respondents' perceptions of relative time, close to the outcome construct; dichotomised outcome loses information; Table 1 reports predicted probabilities, with no R-squared or incremental fit, so the paper cannot show how much variance the split alone explains; paired partners are not jointly modelled; the 2/3 and 1/3 averages are by sex and unpaired, so cannot test over-claiming within couples.
