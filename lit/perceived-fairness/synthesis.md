## What makes a partner see the allocation of chores as fair: synthesis, pass 2

**Basis and limits.** Nineteen papers have been read in full; two of them (Gager 1998, Press & Townsley 1998) are scans read by a vision model, so their extracts carry nothing and sit under Leads. That leaves seventeen papers that carry conclusions: two narrative reviews and fifteen single studies. The reviews are Mikula 1998 and Shelton & John 1996. Neither reports a search method, and each reports most findings secondhand from studies it cites; each secondary extract is marked as reported. No meta-analysis of perceived fairness and relationship satisfaction was found in the pass-1 searches. Every other source is **a single study**, and each is flagged as one where it is cited. Almost all studies are cross-sectional surveys of different-gender couples or of one partner only. The method never takes gender as an input, so gendered findings are read as failure modes to design against, not as parameters. A verdict of "partial" means the verifier judged the extract to support the claim only with the caveat named.

### Conclusions

**C1: the actual split explains only a small part of a partner's perceived fairness (decisive). Supported in direction, by whole-model figures; no study reports the split's own share.** Mikula's review concludes that the actual division is "by no means the main determinant" of perceived justice (partial: no variance figure) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E1] and that variance explained by quantitative workload measures is relatively small [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E2]. Shelton & John's review calls the division-fairness findings inconsistent across studies; Mikula's figures for most studies are secondary (see Leads) [10.1146_annurev.soc.22.1.299#E4]. Single studies agree. In a Dutch survey of 462 men and 638 women, the model with the three labour shares plus controls has an R² of 0.047 and 0.134 for housework unfairness (men, women) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1]. In the Connecticut survey of 382 couples, the full models, with the split plus every other predictor, reach an adjusted R² of .19 for wives and .11 for husbands, which caps the split's share (partial) [10.2307_353530#E1]. In a panel of 178 couples in the first year of parenthood (infant care, not general chores), the whole final model explains about 19%, an upper bound for the split [10.1111_jomf.12331#E1]. The same fairness judgements sit at similar splits: in a Swedish study a semi-equal split falls in both a "both fair" and a "she unfair, he fair" class [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E7], and 62.5% of US women who call the split fair do all or most of the chores [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E7]. Two cautions. A small share of variance is not a small slope: the Dutch authors describe the housework-share effect as large (standardised 0.83 and 1.33) [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2], and within-person odds of "I do more than my share" are 5.98 for women and 7.13 for men per unit of own share of hours in the Australian panel (partial: row not in view) [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E5]. And for men in the Israeli sample the split did not significantly affect fairness at all (partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E3]. The split is a strong predictor of one partner's fairness and still leaves most of the variance unexplained.

**C2: comparison referents predict perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for (decisive). Weakly supported: one study, some referents only.** Both reviews report the claim only as an argument or as zero-order associations from the studies they cite (secondary; see Leads). The one full-text test with a control for the split is the Italian survey of 828 dual-earner couples (single study): favourable or unfavourable comparisons predict fairness for men comparing themselves with men and for women comparing their partner with men [10.5153_sro.3871#E5]. For husbands comparing themselves with a male friend the predicted probability of "fair" falls from 0.56 to 0.40 [10.5153_sro.3871#E7]; for wives comparing their husband with their father it falls from 0.63 to 0.45 [10.5153_sro.3871#E8]. The effect is null for comparisons involving women [10.5153_sro.3871#E6]. The authors suspect self-serving choice of referents, so the survey estimates may be too high [10.5153_sro.3871#E9], and the control for the split is a respondent-reported estimate that leaves room for residual confounding [10.5153_sro.3871#E4]. Other studies do not test it: the Australian panel did not measure referents at all [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E6]; the 30-country study finds referent favourability associated with family-life satisfaction, not fairness, and builds the referent index from the split's own rank [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E3]; the Swedish study models class membership, not fairness at a fixed split [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E3]. A referent that is not independent of the split cannot show a net effect.

**C3: accepted justifications predict perceived fairness after the actual split (decisive). No direct test in the papers read in full.** The review evidence is secondary (see Leads). The nearest primary evidence is procedural: in the Connecticut survey a partner's ability to raise issues predicts housework fairness net of how unequal the split is (beta .13 wives, .14 husbands), though this is not a measure of accepted reasons for the split [10.2307_353530#E5]. A reduced-employment justification did not work in the Swedish study: women in the classes where she calls the split unfair did not accept it [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E5]. The 30-country study tests justification variables only as moderators of the fairness-satisfaction slope, not as predictors of fairness (partial) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E4]. The claim stays open.

**C4: perceived fairness is more strongly associated with a partner's relationship satisfaction than the actual split is (decisive). Probably true on average for women, with documented exceptions; no study compares the two strengths on a common scale and tests the difference.** The reviews say so too, with exceptions, but only from the studies they cite (secondary; see Leads). Primary studies that put both in one model: in Connecticut, fairness has direct paths to satisfaction of .08 to .16 against at most .04 for the split's coefficients (partial, table layout reconstructed) [10.2307_353530#E9], and the authors say the split's effect runs only through perceptions and is small [10.2307_353530#E10]. In the pooled Israeli models fairness is the stronger correlate, .45 against -.13 (ns) for women and .51 against -.04 (ns) for men (partial: value read from a figure) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9]; by group it reverses for Jewish women, where the split matters more than fairness [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E7]. In the 30-country study (women only, family-life satisfaction, not relationship satisfaction) the split's coefficient falls to 0.001 once both fairness items enter (partial, unstandardised) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6]. In the German panel both are in one model, but the coefficients are on different scales and the paper does not compare them [10.20377_jfr-1377#E7]. In the UK couple study the discrepancy in the reported split, not the discrepancy in fairness, tracked poorer relationship quality, the reverse direction [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8]. Frisco & Williams put fairness in the models and the split only in a crosstab, so cannot compare them [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E1].

**C5: perceived fairness mediates the split-satisfaction association (supporting). Partly supported for women; no formal mediation test with time ordering exists.** Mikula says whether perceived injustice mediates between the division and marital quality has had little systematic study [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E15]. In the Connecticut survey the split's effects on satisfaction are largely explained by perceived unfairness and perceived empathy, so fairness is not the sole mediator [10.2307_353530#E11]; the authors call the causal order partially speculative [10.2307_353530#E13] and tested indirect effects path by path (partial) [10.2307_353530#E14]. In the Israeli sample (1,124 couples) full mediation holds for women pooled (partial, no indirect-effect interval) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E11], does not hold for men [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E12], and holds by group only for Christian Arab women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E13]. The 30-country study shows the split's coefficient shrinking once fairness enters and says its data show association only [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E10]. Three other studies run no mediation test and say so [10.1177_2378023118765867#E7] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E10] [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E9].

**C6: each partner over-claims, so the two partners' shares sum to more than the whole (decisive). Supported in direction by three dyadic studies, with small magnitudes.** The founding test is Ross & Sicoly's Experiment 1 (single study, 37 couples in student residences, 20 couple activities): the couples' summed allocations exceeded the available 150 units by a mean of 4.67, F(1,35) = 12.89, p < .001, and 27 of 37 couples over-estimated (partial: pooled over all 20 activities) [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E4]. Over-estimation occurred on 16 of 20 items on average, with the effect small and the scale range restricted (partial) [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E5] [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E6]. The 20 activities mix chores with childcare, decisions and affective items, and no chores-only result is reported [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E2]. In the Connecticut survey (382 couples) the authors report that both spouses credit themselves with more than the partner attributes to them [10.2307_353530#E15]; the mean discrepancy in the wife's share of domestic hours is 4.5 points, which on the reader's arithmetic implies the self-claims sum to about 104.5% (partial, derived) [10.2307_353530#E16], and the pattern does not hold for income share [10.2307_353530#E17]. In 235 UK couples (single study, preregistered) the partners' own-share means sum to 103.9% across three loads (partial, arithmetic on group means) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1], and to 107.0% for cognitive labour [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2], 102.5% for physical chores [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E3] and 102.3% for emotional labour [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E4]; each participant divided 100% across self, partner and others, so a sum above 100 indicates over-claiming [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E5]. Not every sample shows it: the Italian survey's group means sum to about one, unpaired and rounded [10.5153_sro.3871#E2], and the 30-country study surveyed one partner per couple and cannot test it [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E12]. The excess is a few percentage points on average; cognitive labour carries the largest excess in the one study that separated it.

**C7: which chores a partner does predicts perceived fairness beyond the time the chores take (decisive). No direct test in the papers read in full.** Both reviews report, from other studies, that female-typed chores predict perceived fairness best, by comparison of correlations (secondary; see Leads), and Mikula infers that task meaning matters more than time and effort, from correlations without covariate adjustment (partial) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E20]. Primary studies do not separate time and chore type: Koster's housework and childcare shares were not differently related to unfairness (Wald p = 0.79 men, 0.61 women), with measures that are relative frequencies [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E7] [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E8]; the Connecticut survey pools all domestic work into one hours measure [10.2307_353530#E18]; the Australian panel does not measure which chores are done [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E10]. Carlson et al. find that who does the dishes matters for women's relationship satisfaction, net of the other task divisions and total hours (single study, outcome is satisfaction, not perceived fairness) [10.1177_2378023118765867#E4], and say the mechanism through equity is outside their scope [10.1177_2378023118765867#E6]. In mothers, managing the household's cognitive labour (responsibility for child adjustment and finances) but not routines is linked to lower partner satisfaction, with no time control [10.1007_s11199-018-1001-x#E2]. The claim stays open.

**C8: studies measure perceived fairness of the division of chores mainly with a single self-report item (decisive). Supported.** Mikula's review says the NSFH one-item fairness measure underlies most recent studies and that one-item measures of questionable reliability are common to many studies; the studies are not counted [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E21], and it names about ten studies using the NSFH item [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E22]. Ten of the twelve primary studies that measure perceived fairness in full text use one self-report item: US telephone panel [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E5], Australian panel [10.1016_j.ssresearch.2014.12.010#E1], Swedish couples [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E8], the Dutch survey [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E9], the US volunteer survey with three categories [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E1], the Italian survey (partial) [10.5153_sro.3871#E13], the 30-country study (partial) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E13], the German panel [10.20377_jfr-1377#E6], the Israeli sample (partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E14], the UK couple study (partial) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E6]. The Connecticut survey's housework fairness is a dichotomous variable built from two items [10.2307_353530#E20], and Mikula notes that some studies used composites mixing fairness and satisfaction [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E23]. The Israeli authors say a single item is the most common measure in earlier studies (secondary; see Leads). The item wording differs between studies: "fair share" in most, a fair-to-partner item in others, and housework is combined with paid work in the German item [10.20377_jfr-1377#E5].

**C9: an over-benefited partner reports lower relationship satisfaction than a partner in an equitable allocation (supporting). Little evidence, mixed where it exists.** In the US volunteer survey (single study, N = 10,236) "unfair to partner" against "mostly fair" shows no association with satisfaction for chores (adjusted beta -0.00; partial) [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E4], b = 0.02, not significant (partial) [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E5], and the penalty appears for shared expenses, not chores [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E8]. In the German panel (single study, N = 11,012) partners who do "a bit less" than their fair share report satisfaction 0.390 points lower [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1]; the estimate for "much less" is not significant [10.20377_jfr-1377#E2], and the item mixes housework with paid work [10.20377_jfr-1377#E5]. In the Israeli sample only 2.1% of women and 3.9% of men were over-benefited, and an unreported sensitivity analysis found their marital quality similar to the fair group [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E16]. In Connecticut, overreward was unrelated to satisfaction in any model, with no estimates reported, and was dropped [10.2307_353530#E22]. Four studies cannot test it: the 30-country study codes over-benefit as the fairest end of a linear scale [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E16], the US telephone panel folds "partner does more" into an uninterpretable category [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E6], the UK study's item has no direction [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11] and the Dutch study could not separate the groups [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E10].

**Design implications (each a failure mode, not a result).** (1) A method cannot infer a partner's perceived fairness from the allocation: whole models leave most variance unexplained and partners at similar splits land in different fairness classes [10.2307_353530#E1] [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E7]. Each partner's own judgement has to be collected. (2) Partners over-claim, most for cognitive labour [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2], so an allocation should not rest on each partner's unaided account of who does what; the sum of the two partners' reports is a usable check. (3) Referent effects differ by who the partner compares with [10.5153_sro.3871#E5] [10.5153_sro.3871#E6], so referents are worth recording, not assuming. (4) Procedural justice, having a voice in how chores are divided, predicts perceived fairness net of the split [10.2307_353530#E5], which supports including a participation step. (5) Over-benefited and under-benefited partners are different positions and need an item that records direction [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11]. (6) Fairness and the split can diverge in what they predict about satisfaction by group [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8], so neither should be the sole target.

### Disagreements

**C4, fairness versus the split for satisfaction.**
- *Fairness absorbs or outranks the split:* Connecticut [10.2307_353530#E9], the pooled Israeli models [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9], the 30-country study [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6], and Mikula's review of studies that include both (secondary) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E12].
- *The split keeps its weight:* Jewish women in the Israeli sample [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8], men in one study reported by Shelton & John (secondary) [10.1146_annurev.soc.22.1.299#E7], working-class wives' marital conflict (secondary) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E13], and the UK couple study's discrepancy in the reported split [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8].
- *Why they may differ:* the outcome (marital quality, family-life satisfaction, single-item satisfaction), the partner (women only in some, both in others), the culture and cohort, and coefficients on different scales, so no paper compares the strengths directly. The disagreement is open.

**C9, over-benefit.** The German panel finds lower satisfaction for those who do "a bit less" [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1]; the US volunteer survey finds none for chores [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E6]. They differ in item (a five-category "fair share" item mixing two domains against a three-category housework item), in sample (a panel against self-selected volunteers) and in contrast ("fair share" against "mostly fair"). Neither tests over-benefit against an externally judged equitable allocation.

**C2, referents.** Effects for male referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E5] against nulls for female referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E6] inside one survey, with the authors suspecting self-serving referent choice for some effects [10.5153_sro.3871#E9]. The abstract of Grote 2002 reports a failed same-sex-referent prediction (see Leads).

**C1, how small is small.** Whole-model R² of 0.05 to 0.19 [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1] [10.2307_353530#E1] against a model explaining 66% in the study Mikula cites (secondary) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E3] [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E4]. The high figures come from models of cognitions that overlap the fairness measure; the low ones from models built on the split and demographics. A steep slope [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2] is compatible with a small R².

**C5, mediation.** It holds for women pooled and Christian Arab women, and fails for men and for Jewish women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E11] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E12] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E13].

### What would change this

- **C3 and C7 are open.** A study that measures accepted justifications and the actual split in one model would settle C3; Mikula 2009, Hawkins 1995 and Urbina 2025 are the likeliest. A study that puts the split of particular chores and total time in one model with perceived fairness as the outcome would settle C7; Blair & Johnson 1992, Sanchez 1994 and Grote 2002 are the candidates.
- **C2** would be overturned if Mikula 2009 or Hawkins 1995 show referent effects vanishing once the split is in the model, which is what Grote 2002's abstract hints at for one referent. It would be firmed up if Mikula 2009 shows them holding net of the split in both partners.
- **C1** would flip if a study reports the split's own R² above roughly a third of the variance, or if Sanchez 1994's "powerful determinants" turns out to mean a large share.
- **C4 and C5** need a study with both partners, time-ordered data, a satisfaction outcome and a common scale for the two predictors. The three direct tests currently disagree by group.
- **C6** would be firmed up by Kamo 2000 or any matched-couple report of chores alone; a chores-only sum under 100 would overturn it. The scanned Press & Townsley should be checked by eye before anything in it is used.
- **C9:** a study that separates over-benefited from equitable partners with an item that records direction would settle it; none found tests it cleanly.
- **Gager 1998 and Press & Townsley 1998** are machine-read. Check their extracts against the page by eye before any reading rests on them.
