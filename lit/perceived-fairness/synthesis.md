## What makes a partner see the allocation of chores as fair: synthesis, pass 3

**Basis and limits.** Twenty-eight papers have been read in full. Four of them are scans read by a vision model (Gager 1998 and Press & Townsley 1998 in pass 2; Hawkins 1995 and Lennon & Rosenfield 1994 in pass 3), so their extracts are machine-read, carry no conclusion and sit under Leads. That leaves twenty-four papers that carry conclusions: three narrative reviews (Mikula 1998, Shelton & John 1996, Coltrane 2000) and twenty-one single studies. None of the reviews reports a search method, and each reports most findings secondhand from studies it cites; each secondary extract is marked as reported. No meta-analysis of perceived fairness and relationship satisfaction was found in the pass-1 searches. Every other source is **a single study**, and each is flagged as one where it is cited. Almost all studies are cross-sectional surveys of different-gender couples or of one partner only. The method never takes gender as an input, so gendered findings are read as failure modes to design against, not as parameters. A verdict of "partial" means the verifier judged the extract to support the claim only with the caveat named. Where this pass changed an earlier reading, the text says so.

### Conclusions

**C1: the actual split explains only a small part of a partner's perceived fairness (decisive). Supported in direction. The one adjusted analysis of the hours split puts its share at about 3% of the variance; bivariate figures run to about 11% and, for one child-care measure, about 22%; every other figure comes from whole models.** The cleanest figure is Sánchez's NSFH analysis of 2,618 matched couples (single study): both partners' housework and employment hours together give an adjusted R² of .03 for women and .03 for men on a single fairness item [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E1], and .04 with every control [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E2]. In a panel of 181 couples crossing the first birth (single study), the self-rated split correlates with fairness at |r| .00 to .16 for wives and .30 to .33 for husbands, at most about 11% of variance (partial: bivariate, a self-rated relative index, signs unrecoverable) [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E1]. Blair & Johnson's models on 778 wives (single study) explain about 15% in full, so the split alone explains no more than that (partial) [10.2307_353243#E1] [10.2307_353243#E2]. In Mikula's dyadic model of 632 couples (single study, convenience samples), the split and hours explain 8% (domestic work) and 10% (child care) of women's variance and nothing significant for men, against 21 to 33% for the entitlement block (partial: the split's share inside a joint model) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E1] [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E2]. Mikula's review (earlier pass) concludes that the actual split is "by no means the main determinant" [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E1], and Shelton & John call the split-fairness findings inconsistent [10.1146_annurev.soc.22.1.299#E4]. The earlier Dutch and Connecticut figures agree: whole-model R² of 0.047 and 0.134 [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1] and adjusted R² of .19 and .11 [10.2307_353530#E1]. Cautions. First, a small R² is not a small slope: the Dutch authors describe the housework-share effect as large [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2], and in Urbina's vignette experiment (single study, 1,502 raters judging hypothetical couples) against a 50% reference, the 10% and 90% housework shares move the rating by about -3.5 and +3.5 points (about 7 points across the range), ten times any other dimension, but the range was the experimenter's choice and no variance share is reported [10.1177_08912432251337416#E2] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E3]. Second, the unadjusted association can be larger than the adjusted share: in Mikula's data the women's share of child care correlates -.47 with women's child-care justice, about 22% of variance (partial: column headers absent) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E3]. Third, Coltrane's review ranks husbands' share of routine housework as the single most important predictor of a wife's fairness, without quantifying it (secondary; see Leads). For men in the Israeli sample the split did not significantly affect fairness at all (partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E3]. The split is a strong predictor of the direction of a partner's fairness and still leaves most of its variance unexplained.

**C2: comparison referents predict perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for (decisive). Weakly and inconsistently supported; three studies control for the split, two of them only in part.** Mikula's dyadic model (single study) controls the split and hours: the outcome of the man-versus-other-men comparison predicts justice (beta .181) and the woman-versus-other-women outcome does not (beta -.041) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E4]; the frequency of both referential comparisons was also not significant, so only one referent predicts [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E5]; the authors say the comparison predictors stay predictive when the balance is controlled, in a cross-sectional design [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E6]. The Italian survey of 828 couples (single study) finds effects for men comparing themselves with men and wives comparing their husband with their father and none for comparisons involving women [10.5153_sro.3871#E5] [10.5153_sro.3871#E6], and the authors suspect self-serving choice of referent [10.5153_sro.3871#E9]. In the panel of 181 couples (single study) the authors say all Table 3 correlations but one stayed significant when the split was controlled, with no controlled coefficients reported (partial) [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E4]; spousal-comparison betas in the regressions that leave the split out are .33, .25, .19 for wives and .13, .13, .08 for husbands, signs not recoverable [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E6]; same-sex referent frequency predicts lower, not higher, fairness, opposite to the hypothesis [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E5]; and prospectively, spousal comparisons did not predict later fairness [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E7], with the authors reading the dominant direction as perceived inequity prompting comparison [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E9]. Three studies measured no referents: Blair & Johnson [10.2307_353243#E4], Sánchez [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E5] and Urbina, who left comparison out for respondent burden [10.1177_08912432251337416#E4] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E5]. The reviews report the claim from other studies only (see Leads). A referent that is chosen after the fact or built from the split itself cannot show a net effect.

**C3: accepted justifications predict perceived fairness after the actual split is accounted for (decisive). Still no direct test; proxy evidence exists but none for a partner's own accepted reasons.** Mikula's model uses objective proxies for justification: the woman's weekly professional hours predict both partners' justice ratings net of the split (beta -.087; partial: a proxy, not a reason the partner reports accepting) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E7], and only the woman's hours and SES predict, not the man's [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E8]. Urbina's randomised vignettes show raters judging a focal person with more income 0.204 points more overburdened and one working more hours 0.449 points more overburdened, net of the randomised split (partial: third-party raters, hypothetical couples) [10.1177_08912432251337416#E6] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E7] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E8]. The authors say this concerns judgements of others' arrangements and not a partner's own [10.1177_08912432251337416#E9]. Two studies measured no justification: Sánchez, whose models hold only hours, employment and demographics (partial: the passage is about unresolved beliefs, and the absence follows from the model design) [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E6], Blair & Johnson, whose ideology proxy showed little association [10.2307_353243#E6] [10.2307_353243#E7]. In the panel of 181 couples, enjoyment and competence are tested as outcome values; they may work as accepted reasons for an unequal split, but the paper does not frame or test them as justifications (partial) [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E10]. The earlier pass's nearest primary evidence was procedural (ability to raise issues, beta .13 and .14) [10.2307_353530#E5], and a reduced-employment justification did not work in the Swedish study [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E5]. The claim stays open: it is untested for the justifications a partner actually accepts.

**C4: perceived fairness is more strongly associated with a partner's relationship satisfaction than the actual split is (decisive). Probably true on average for women, with documented exceptions; no study compares the two on a common scale and tests the difference. No paper read this pass changes this.** The new full texts either measure no satisfaction or compare nothing: Blair & Johnson cites the link from other work and analyses only the first step, and Mikula's 2009 paper cites it and compares nothing against the split (both secondary; see Leads), and Coltrane's review says that for men split and fairness are typically unrelated to well-being or satisfaction [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E10]. Earlier primary tests stand: Connecticut, fairness paths .08 to .16 against at most .04 for the split (partial) [10.2307_353530#E9]; pooled Israeli models, .45 against -.13 (ns) for women (partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9] with a reversal for Jewish women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8]; the 30-country study, split coefficient falling to 0.001 once fairness enters (partial, unstandardised) [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6]; and in the UK couple study the discrepancy in the reported split, not in fairness, tracked poorer relationship quality [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8].

**C5: perceived fairness mediates the split-satisfaction association (supporting). Partly supported for women; no formal mediation test with time ordering exists.** Blair & Johnson state that they analyse only the first step of the chain from the split to fairness to satisfaction [10.2307_353243#E9]. Coltrane's report that eight studies treat fairness as a mediator is secondary (see Leads). The earlier evidence stands: Connecticut effects largely explained by perceived unfairness and empathy [10.2307_353530#E11] with the causal order called partly speculative [10.2307_353530#E13]; full mediation for Israeli women pooled and not for men (partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E11] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E12]; three studies run no test [10.1177_2378023118765867#E7] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E10] [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E9].

**C6: each partner over-claims, so the two partners' shares sum to more than the whole (decisive). Weaker than at the end of pass 2: three dyadic studies find small excesses, a large matched-couple panel finds over-claiming by husbands only, and two new studies cannot test it cleanly.** In the NSFH matched-couple panel (Kamo 2000, single study, 4,122 couples), husbands' own estimates of their weekly housework exceed their wives' estimate of it by about 3 hours, significantly [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E1]; wives' estimates of their own work and their husbands' estimates of it are about equal (about 37 hours), with a mean discrepancy of 0.63 hours that is not different from zero [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E2], exactly half of couples have the wife's estimate above her husband's [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E3], and the author concludes that over-claiming holds for husbands and not for wives [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E4]. The same paper's mean husband's share is 35.86 by the husband and 31.64 by the wife, which implies self-claimed shares of about 104 if the wife's share is the complement, an inference the paper does not make or test (partial) [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E5]. The earlier findings: Ross & Sicoly's 37 couples summed allocations exceeded the available 150 units by a mean of 4.67 [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E4] (single study, activities not only chores [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E2]); Connecticut's implied 104.5% (partial, derived) [10.2307_353530#E16]; the UK couple study's 103.9% overall and 107.0% for cognitive labour (partial, arithmetic on group means) [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1] [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2]. Two new studies are indeterminate. In Mikula's dyadic model, Table 1 gives only the woman's share per respondent, and the literal reading implies sums of own claims of 88% (domestic work) and 101% (child care) with an ambiguous column labelling (partial, reader's arithmetic) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E11]. In the panel of 181 couples the self-rated ratings sum to 6.12 to 6.25 against a consistent 6, but the scale is ordinal relative amount, not a share, so the authors' data cannot be summed to a share above 100% [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E11] [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E12] [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E13]. Coltrane's review reports overestimation of one's own time against diaries, which is not a sum of shares (secondary; see Leads). Not every sample shows it: the Italian survey's group means sum to about one [10.5153_sro.3871#E2], and Blair & Johnson use wives' reports for both spouses, so the claim cannot be examined there [10.2307_353243#E10]. For design: expect an excess of a few points on average, concentrated in one partner in some data and clearest for cognitive labour.

**C7: which chores a partner does predicts perceived fairness beyond the time the chores take (decisive). Still no direct test; the best primary evidence entangles chore type with time and concerns the other partner's chores.** Blair & Johnson (single study, 778 wives) enter husbands' hours by task type: betas of .19 for female-typed, .01 for male-typed and .07 for neutral tasks, only the first significant (partial) [10.2307_353243#E11], standardised .244 and .222 for employed and nonemployed wives with the wife's total labour in the model (partial) [10.2307_353243#E12] [10.2307_353243#E13]. The authors themselves note that male tasks take little time, so task type and time are entangled [10.2307_353243#E14], and the control is the wife's total labour, not the husband's total time. Everything else is secondary or silent. Sánchez reports Blair & Johnson's result second hand and uses only total hours over nine tasks (secondary; see Leads). Coltrane's review reports task-type findings from other studies (secondary; see Leads) and says a total-hours measure is less successful than alternatives [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E18]. Mikula tests only domestic work against child care [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E13]; Grote's panel did not measure time and analyses fairness as a domain index [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E14] [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E15]; Urbina does not specify chores and names task type as open [10.1177_08912432251337416#E11] [10.1177_08912432251337416#E12]. Earlier evidence: Koster's housework-childcare shares were not differently related to unfairness [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E7]; Carlson et al. find the dishes matter for women's satisfaction net of other tasks and total hours, with satisfaction as outcome [10.1177_2378023118765867#E4]. The scanned Lennon & Rosenfield and Hawkins papers bear on this and are under Leads.

**C8: studies measure perceived fairness of the division of chores mainly with a single self-report item (decisive). Supported.** Mikula's review says the NSFH one-item measure underlies most recent studies and that one-item measures of questionable reliability are common [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E21]. Pass 2 counted ten of twelve full-text studies using one item. This pass adds four single-item studies: Sánchez's five-category chores-fairness question (one study) [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E8], Blair & Johnson's five-point NSFH item [10.2307_353243#E15], Mikula's one item per domain on a six-point scale [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E9] and Urbina's single 11-point item per vignette [10.1177_08912432251337416#E13]. Two use composites: Kamo's four-item average across chores, paid work, money and child care, with alphas of .56 and .51 [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E6] [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E7], and Grote's multi-item index at Times 1 and 2, with one-item measures only at Time 3 [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E16] [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E17]. That makes fourteen single-item studies of eighteen. Coltrane's remarks on the NSFH item and on 1990s instruments are secondary (see Leads). Wording varies: "fair share" in most, a fair-to-partner item in others, and housework combined with paid work in the German item [10.20377_jfr-1377#E5]. Lennon & Rosenfield (single item) and Hawkins (a two-item scale) are scans and sit under Leads.

**C9: an over-benefited partner reports lower relationship satisfaction than a partner in an equitable allocation (supporting). Little evidence, mixed where it exists, and what is new points against.** Coltrane's review summarises that for men, the usual over-benefited partner, split and fairness are typically unrelated to marital satisfaction (partial: men stand in for over-benefit; prose summary) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E23]; and its secondary reports on men who do little (see Leads) point the same way. Earlier primary evidence: no association for chores in the US volunteer survey (partial) [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E4]; satisfaction 0.390 points lower for those who do "a bit less" in the German panel, not significant for "much less" [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1] [10.20377_jfr-1377#E2]; a similar marital quality in the 2.1% and 3.9% over-benefited in the Israeli sample [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E16]; and unrelated in Connecticut, with no estimates reported [10.2307_353530#E22]. Four studies cannot test it [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E16] [10.1177_0192513x02238520#E6] [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11] [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E10]. The scanned Lennon & Rosenfield paper (under Leads) finds a U-shaped depressive-symptom result.

**Design implications (each a failure mode, not a result).** (1) A method cannot infer a partner's perceived fairness from the allocation: the split alone accounts for about 3% of variance in the one adjusted analysis (and up to about 22% unadjusted) and partners at similar splits land in different fairness classes [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E1] [10.4054_demres.2017.36.16#E7]. Each partner's own judgement has to be collected. (2) Partners may over-claim, but not in every dataset and perhaps for one partner only [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E4], and most for cognitive labour where it was separated [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E2], so an allocation should not rest on one partner's unaided account of who does what; the sum of the two partners' reports is a usable check, not a calibrated one. (3) Referent effects differ by who the partner compares with and may run from perceived inequity to comparison [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E9], so referents are worth recording, not assuming or manipulating. (4) Procedural justice, having a voice in how chores are divided, predicts perceived fairness net of the split [10.2307_353530#E5], which supports including a participation step. (5) Partners may accept an unequal split when the other partner has more income or paid hours [10.1177_08912432251337416#E7], a vignette finding for third parties and not for a partner's own judgement; the participation protocol should therefore record the reasons a partner accepts rather than assume none. (6) C7 is untested, so the burden model has to guard against the possibility that a time-equal allocation still feels unfair when the chores differ in kind: the only primary test entangles chore type with time [10.2307_353243#E14], and a model that equates a chore with its minutes would be blind to it. (7) Over-benefited and under-benefited partners are different positions and need an item that records direction [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E11]. (8) Fairness and the split can diverge in what they predict about satisfaction by group [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8], so neither should be the sole target. (9) Single-item fairness measures dominate the literature, and a composite that averages four domains reported low reliability (alpha .56 and .51) [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E7], so averaging across domains is no cure; the method should ask about perceived fairness for each chore and for the whole allocation.

### Disagreements

**C6, over-claiming.**
- *Both partners over-claim:* Ross & Sicoly [10.1037_0022-3514.37.3.322#E4], the UK couple study [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E1], Connecticut (derived) [10.2307_353530#E16], and Coltrane's review for hours against diaries (secondary, partial) [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E14].
- *Only husbands over-claim:* the NSFH matched-couple panel [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E1] [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E2] [10.1006_ssre.2000.0674#E4].
- *Indeterminate:* Mikula 2009 (88% and 101%) [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E11] and the panel of 181 couples (ordinal scale) [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E13].
- *Why they may differ:* hours against shares, an observed excess measured against a partner's report and not a diary, the population (students, dual-earner couples, a national panel), and the unit (a few activities against all housework). No study uses the same measure twice.

**C2, referents.** Effects for male referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E5] [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E4] against nulls for female referents [10.5153_sro.3871#E6] [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E5] and prospective nulls [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E7], with a reverse direction argued [10.1037_0893-3200.16.4.510#E9]. Controls for the split differ between studies, and the Italian authors and Mikula 2009 measure the referent's outcome differently.

**C1, how small is small.** Whole-model R² of 0.03 to 0.19 [10.1093_sf_73.2.533#E2] [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E1] [10.2307_353530#E1] [10.2307_353243#E2] and a share of 8 to 10% in a joint model [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E1], against bivariate correlations up to .47 [10.1111_j.1475-6811.2009.01239.x#E3], against a model explaining 66% in the study Mikula cites (secondary) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E3]. The high figures come from models of cognitions that overlap the fairness measure; the low ones from models built on the split and demographics. A steep slope [10.1177_0192513x21993899#E2] is compatible with a small R².

**C4, fairness versus the split for satisfaction.**
- *Fairness absorbs or outranks the split:* Connecticut [10.2307_353530#E9], the pooled Israeli models [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E9], the 30-country study [10.1093_esr_jcx085#E6], and the reviews (secondary) [10.1023_a-1023282615718#E12] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E8].
- *The split keeps its weight:* Jewish women in the Israeli sample [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E8], the UK couple study [10.1038_s41598-026-60727-z#E8], and men in one study reported by Shelton & John (secondary) [10.1146_annurev.soc.22.1.299#E7]. Coltrane's review reads both the split and fairness as typically unrelated to men's satisfaction [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E10].
- *Why they may differ:* the outcome, the partner, the culture and cohort, and coefficients on different scales, so no paper compares the strengths directly. Open.

**C5, mediation.** It holds for women pooled and Christian Arab women, and fails for men and for Jewish women [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E11] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E12] [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2002.00027.x#E13].

**C9, over-benefit.** The German panel finds lower satisfaction for those who do "a bit less" [10.20377_jfr-1377#E1]; the US volunteer survey finds none for chores [10.1371_journal.pone.0214204#E6]; Coltrane's review reads men's fairness and split as typically unrelated to satisfaction [10.1111_j.1741-3737.2000.01208.x#E23]. They differ in item, sample and contrast, and none tests over-benefit against an externally judged equitable allocation.

### Leads

Machine-read or abstract-only material. None of it carries a conclusion.

*Scanned papers read by a vision model; compare with the page by eye:*

### What would change this

- **C3 and C7 are open.** A study that measures the justifications a partner accepts and enters them with the actual split in one model, with the partner's own fairness as the outcome, would settle C3; Thompson 1991, Major 1993, Grote & Clark 1998 and Braun 2008 are the candidates. A study that puts the split of particular chores and total time in one model with the doer's own perceived fairness as the outcome would settle C7; none read in full tests it, Blair & Johnson comes closest for the other partner's chores, and the scanned Lennon & Rosenfield (E15, pending an eye check) is the nearest test for the doer's own chores. Mederer 1993 and DeMaris & Longmore 1996 are requested for this.
- **C2** would be overturned if a study that controls the actual split and measures referents independently of it finds the effect gone; it would be firmed up by one that shows effects for both partners and for female referents. Grote's prospective null and Hawkins's lost direct effect (scan) lean toward overturning.
- **C1** would flip if a study reports the split's own R² above roughly a third of the variance; the bivariate -.47 in Mikula 2009 and Greenstein 1996's full text are the places to look.
- **C4 and C5** need a study with both partners, time-ordered data, a satisfaction outcome and a common scale for the two predictors. Greenstein 1996 and Grote & Clark 2001 (same sample as the panel of 181 couples) are the candidates.
- **C6** would be overturned by a matched-couple study of chores alone, in shares, whose two own-claims sum to 100 or less; Kamo's wives already come close. It would be firmed up by one that replicates the sum above 100 in a national sample.
- **C9:** a study that separates over-benefited from equitable partners with an item that records direction would settle it; none found tests it cleanly.
- **Hawkins 1995, Lennon & Rosenfield 1994, Gager 1998 and Press & Townsley 1998** are machine-read. Check their extracts against the page by eye before any reading rests on them; Hawkins's figures and Lennon & Rosenfield's table rows are the most exposed.
